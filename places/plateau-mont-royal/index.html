Residential Architectural Styles of Le Plateau-Mont-Royal (borough of Montréal)

Le Plateau-Mont-Royal is where the Montréal plex was invented and where it remains the rule. The borough was farmland until 1845, then a cluster of villages (Coteau-Saint-Louis 1846, Saint-Jean-Baptiste 1861, De Lorimier 1870, Saint-Louis-du-Mile-End 1878) that Montréal annexed between 1886 and 1909. Its housing stock was built almost entirely between 1880 and 1914 by developers working to the City's new rules on lot size, lanes, front setbacks and masonry cladding, and those rules, more than any style, gave the district its form: the duplex of the first boom (1885–91), the triplex with exterior stair of the second (1904–14), the interwar apartment blocks of the boulevards. In 2004 the borough commissioned Patri-Arch to survey the territory (some 1,500 photographs) and classify its buildings by four criteria: general massing, siting, number of dwellings and mode of access to them. Fifteen base types in five families resulted, and they underpin the borough-wide PIIA. This page presents them in the same form as Mount Royal's thirteen.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

Before 1845 · Formation of the territorial structure

1654 – 1845

Fiefs granted in 1654 and 1665, chemin Saint-Laurent opened in 1717, a manor and tannery around 1740, and a first subdivision on the Cadieux land in 1834 that a succession dispute froze until 1842. No building survives from this period.

1845–1880 · The villages

1845 – 1880

The first residential subdivision (Courville, 1845) opened the villages: Coteau-Saint-Louis (1846), Saint-Jean-Baptiste (1861), De Lorimier (1870); the Comte farm was subdivided in 1872, the horse tram arrived in 1864, the railway to Saint-Jérôme in 1876, and Saint-Louis-du-Mile-End was born in 1878. The dominant house was the attached single-family dwelling, often at the street line, with rear-lot houses that later hygiene rules banned; a few workers' faubourg houses survive in the old Village des Carrières.

Detached urban house La maison urbaine isolée

Style: Victorian eclecticism / Second Empire / mansard
Photograph pendingpending

The bourgeois villa of the Plateau's first decades: a free-standing stone house set back from the street, individually designed, now rare and often converted.

Where it comes from

Built for the well-to-do west of Saint-Laurent and south of avenue des Pins, then along Sherbrooke, Square Saint-Louis and Saint-Hubert; the family also includes a two-unit superposed variant that differs from the duplex by storeys per dwelling, uniform stone facing and richer ornament.

1880–1914 · The golden age of development

1880 – 1914

The golden age. In 1880 Montréal standardised lots and lanes, then required a front setback and stone or brick cladding; the villages were annexed (Saint-Jean-Baptiste 1886, Coteau-Saint-Louis 1893/96, Mile End and De Lorimier 1909). Two booms: 1885–91 (bourgeois single-family houses and the duplex for workers) and 1904–14 (the triplex, with front setback, exterior stair and L-plan). Boulevard Saint-Joseph was laid out around 1905 with a 28-foot median.

Duplex without front setback Le duplex sans marge de recul avant

Style: Montréal faubourg vernacular / Victorian eclecticism
Photograph pendingpending: extract from Annexe C / fiche 2.1

The plainest and oldest plex: two superposed flats in a brick box built right on the sidewalk line, paired front doors at one end, an interior stair to the upper flat, and a moulded cornice as the only crown. Its three sub-variants add a small entrance balcony, rusticated stone facing, or a porte cochère.

Where it comes from

The duplex was the first housing type built in the borough under the large speculative subdivisions of the 1880s and belongs to the first construction boom of 1885–1891. It is the developer's product of the pre-1880 regime, before Montréal imposed the front setback: a simple rectangle of two flats, one over the other, that the later duplexes and the triplex all descend from.

Duplex with front setback Le duplex avec marge de recul avant

Style: Montréal faubourg vernacular / Second Empire / mansard / Victorian eclecticism
Photograph pendingpending: fiche 2.2

The same two-flat box pulled back a metre or two from the sidewalk, which is enough to raise the ground floor a few steps, hang a balcony over the porch and, very often, top the façade with a false mansard and dormers. This is the duplex most people picture: the one with the mansard.

Where it comes from

Around 1880 Montréal began applying building by-laws to the new districts: standardised lots and lanes, then a mandatory front setback and stone or brick cladding. The setback produced this variant. Its false-mansard crown is the Second Empire fashion of the 1870s–80s applied to a speculative type; the mansard is decorative (a fausse mansarde), not a full storey.

Siting & landscape

Massing

  • Occasional front gallery on the ground floor and sometimes the upper floor; small straight entrance stair.
  • Frequent use of a false-mansard roof.
  • Corps de bâti rectangulaire sans saillies, 2 étages avec une surélévation du rez-de-chaussée par rapport au niveau du sol inférieure à 8 contremarches, toit plat, balcon généralement présent au-dessus du porche d'entrée.
Articulation

Duplex with exterior stair Le duplex avec escalier extérieur

Style: Montréal faubourg vernacular
Photograph pendingpending: fiche 2.3

Two flats, but the upper one now has its own door and its own stair climbing the front of the house. The façade turns symmetrical, doors in the middle, windows either side, and the cornice can give way to a brick parapet. It is the duplex learning the triplex's trick.

Where it comes from

Built from the same moment as the triplex (type 3.2) and the multiplex (3.3), i.e. the second boom of 1904–1914. The exterior stair is the direct consequence of the mandatory front setback: with the building pushed back, the stair to the upper dwelling could be moved outside and the interior floor area given back to the flats.

Triplex with exterior stair Le triplex avec escalier extérieur

Style: Montréal faubourg vernacular
Photograph pendingpending

The Montréal triplex: three flats stacked in a three-storey brick front, the upper two reached by an outside stair, often the famous curving iron one, with balconies at each level and an L-shaped footprint that leaves a light-well at the back. It is the dominant building of the borough.

Where it comes from

The Étude typomorphologique calls the triplex "la forme architecturale typique de ce secteur de Montréal" and dates it to the second boom, 1904–1914, driven by housing demand and by municipal regulation: standard lots and lanes (1880), then the mandatory front setback and stone or brick cladding, which "amènera la translation de l'escalier d'accès aux logements des étages supérieurs vers l'extérieur". Réjean Legault (1989) is the standard scholarly account.

1914–1960 · Consolidation

1914 – 1960

Near saturation. The Université de Montréal's departure for the mountain around 1920 began a bourgeois exodus and demographic change; the 1930s brought federal make-work projects. No new signature type appeared; the triplex stayed in fashion into the 1940s, joined on the boulevards by the interwar édifices de rapport and, after 1945, by replacement apartment buildings.

After 1960 · Great transformations and the return to the city

1960 – 1980

Metro line 2 along Berri (1962–66); Saint-Joseph's median cut from 28 to 6 feet (1962); "rénovation urbaine" in Milton-Parc (the Pins interchange; La Cité, 1974–75, 255 properties demolished); then the citizen counter-movement and the Milton-Parc housing co-operatives, the largest in Canada.

Notes on the approach

The framework's four criteria are morphological, not stylistic; families are defined by dwelling count and mode of access (interior versus exterior stair), which is why the same brick façade vocabulary appears across duplex, triplex and multiplex. Four specialised, non-residential types (mixed commercial-residential, commercial, industrial, institutional) complete the 2004 list but are not encoded here. Codes are document-specific: the exterior-stair triplex is 3.2 in Plateau documents and 2.6 in the Sud-Ouest borough's. The consolidated Annexe C (2025) counts ten types; the PIIA fascicule speaks of nine dominant typologies; the 2004 Annexe D of fifteen plus four. "Maison de faubourg" here is a family heading for the earliest workers' houses; Québec City's thesaurus uses the term for two distinct types (a steep-roofed one-storey neoclassical house and a flat-roofed two- or three-storey vernacular one), and Mount Royal's "maison faubourienne" is a detached suburban house of 1915–35.
