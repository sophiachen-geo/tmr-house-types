Residential Architectural Styles of Baie-D'Urfé

Baie-D'Urfé is a shore, a road and a set of farm lots that never became a village. The chemin du Roy — later the Queen's Highway, now the chemin du Lakeshore — runs along the north bank of the lac Saint-Louis, and the Sulpicians conceded the land behind it in narrow strips reaching back from the water. The 2005 heritage evaluation of the merged arrondissement of Beaconsfield–Baie d'Urfé says that this road holds « la plus grande densité de bâtiments d'intérêt patrimonial de l'arrondissement », and describes what is on it in one clause that is the closest thing this town has to a typology: « d'anciennes maisons de ferme, des maisons bourgeoises et des villas d'été de la fin du XIXe siècle et du début du XXe siècle », standing well back from a winding road with mature trees and no sidewalks.

The oldest thing here is a farmhouse of about 1700 at 20122 chemin Lakeshore, built by Hubert Rangé-Dit-Laviolette on a lot the Sulpicians had conceded in 1686 and which he bought in 1695. It sits at the back of its lot, a hundred metres from the modern road, because the chemin du Roy used to pass between it and the water — the paving stones are said to survive under the lawn — and its main front faces south to the lake and the îles Sainte-Geneviève for the same reason. It is the only protected building in the town.

Almost everything else is postwar. The town was chartered in 1911 with fewer than two hundred inhabitants, and its own history records that in the 1950s the population increased fivefold. In the 1950s, the 2005 cahier notes, the only drivable streets in the whole municipality were the rues Oxford and Cambridge, the avenue St. Andrew, the chemin du Lakeshore and the rues Morgan and Lakeview — six roads. What has been built since covers the rest, and the heritage argument here is entirely about the shore road.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

1663–1760 · Sulpician concessions on the chemin du Roy

1663 – 1760

The Sulpicians become seigneurs of the island of Montréal in 1663 and begin conceding land at the western end. In 1687 an Iroquois raid kills settlers on the Pointe-à-Caron, the chapel is burned and the population falls back on Montréal; the colonists return after the peace of 1701 and farm. The house that survives from this phase — the only one — is the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette, built about 1700 by Hubert Rangé-Dit-Laviolette (1661 – vers 1727) on the concession he had bought in 1695. The RPCQ reads its oldest part as belonging to the French building tradition: a stone carré barely raised off the ground, an asymmetrical arrangement of openings, an almost square plan of small dimensions, and chimney stacks carried up in the line of the gable walls, which it says are « plus particulièrement associés aux maisons de l'île de Montréal et des régions environnantes ».

Style: French regime

Phase attribution: provisional

A low fieldstone box of one and a half storeys under a steep two-slope roof, stacks rising inside the gable walls, openings placed where they were wanted rather than where symmetry asked for them. Three of them survive across three of the six West Island places — a trading post of 1669–71 at Lachine, a farm house of about 1700 at Baie-D'Urfé, and one of 1769–91 at Pointe-Claire — and all three are protected individually while nothing around them is. One record, three municipalities: the photograph on this card is Lachine's, and Baie-D'Urfé's and Pointe-Claire's stand beside it on the type page.

Where it comes from

The Sulpicians became seigneurs of the island of Montréal in 1663 and conceded the côtes that run out to its western end; the houses built on those concessions were stone because stone was what the fields gave up and because fire and raiding made anything else a poor bet. What survives is very little. Lachine's is the oldest and least altered: two rectangular stone buildings put up in 1669–71 as a fur-trade post for Charles Le Moyne and Jacques Le Ber, restored in the 1980s toward their seventeenth-century appearance and now the Musée de Lachine. Baie-D'Urfé's was built about 1700 by Hubert Rangé dit Laviolette on a concession he bought in 1695, and then quietly rebuilt by the Pilon family between 1839 and 1874 — the walls raised, the roof pitch eased off, moulded chambranles and projecting eaves added in what RPCQ calls a taste imported after the Conquest. Pointe-Claire's went up between 1769 and 1791 on a censive conceded in 1722, and was altered again in 1828. Read together the three make the point the West Island pages keep making: the form is one form across three municipalities, the protection is three separate individual instruments granted in 1964, 2001 and 2001, and not one of them protects an ensemble.

Siting & landscape
  • Each of the three survivors sits on ground that predates the street grid around it. Le Ber-Le Moyne stands on a raised point of land beside lac Saint-Louis at the head of the Lachine rapids, on a slight mound planted with mature trees.
  • The Baie-D'Urfé house stands at the back of its lot, about a hundred metres from the public road and close to the shore, its main front turned south to the lake and the îles Sainte-Geneviève — RPCQ reads that orientation as evidence that the chemin du Roy once ran between the house and the water.
  • Pointe-Claire's house stands at the far end of a large treed lot that now isolates it from the postwar subdivision around it, on a censive conceded by the Sulpicians in 1722 along the côte Saint-Jean.
Massing
  • A low stone carré on a near-square or short rectangular plan, one and a half storeys, sitting close to the ground. The Baie-D'Urfé carré measures 9.4 by 9.3 m.
  • Steep two-slope roof with straight slopes. Eaves are shallow at Lachine, and carried out over a covered gallery at Baie-D'Urfé and Pointe-Claire — in both cases a nineteenth-century alteration, not an original feature.
  • Ancillary buildings belong to the type where they survive - Lachine keeps a stone dépendance of the same date, and an 1781 census records a house, a barn and a stable at Baie-D'Urfé.
Articulation
  • Almost none in the original fabric. What ornament these houses carry — moulded wood chambranles, gable dormers, a neo-Gothic porch, a bell-cast eave — was added between about 1828 and 1874 to bring them up to date.
Openings
  • Openings set out asymmetrically, which RPCQ names as a French-tradition trait at both Baie-D'Urfé and Pointe-Claire.
  • Casement windows with small panes. At Pointe-Claire the south front is opened up for light while the north wall carries only two windows.
Materials
  • Fieldstone throughout, laid up rubble. Pointe-Claire is left bare, and RPCQ names the absence of crépi as a Montréal-region trait; Baie-D'Urfé is whitewashed on three sides.
  • Two chimney stacks carried up in the line of the gable walls — the trait RPCQ singles out as belonging "plus particulièrement" to the houses of the island of Montréal and its neighbouring regions.

A thin record by design, and honest about it. None of the six West Island places has a per-type typology document — no inventory, no thesaurus, no per-type table — so this record is built from the three individual houses that do carry statutory records, plus the three 2005 Ville de Montréal évaluation cahiers, which characterise sectors and not building types. What that leaves empty: `window_proportion` is null because all three RPCQ fiches describe the windows as casements with small panes and none gives a proportion; `roof.pitch_deg` is null because the fiches say "aigu" and "à pente moyenne" and never a figure; `lot_width_m`, `setback_front_m`, `setback_side_m` and `front_yard_green_pct` are null because no source measures a lot or a setback anywhere in the West Island; `sectors` is null because a shared record cannot carry sector codes that exist in only one of its places. `storeys` is entered as 1.5 from the RPCQ "Nombre d'étages" field, which reads 1½ for all three — note that the Le Ber-Le Moyne description text contradicts its own data field, saying the buildings "s'élèvent sur un étage". The canonical is `french-urban-house-stone` rather than `french-rural-house-1st-hip` even though all three are farm or trading-post houses in open country: the discriminant in this vocabulary is fabric and roof, not siting, and all three are stone under a straight two-slope roof with stacks in the gable walls, where the rural canonical is pièce-sur-pièce timber under a hip carried to the wall head with no cellar. RPCQ says as much itself, calling the nearby Sainte-Anne example « la maison rurale d'inspiration urbaine, fréquente dans la région de Montréal et les régions avoisinantes ». The phase is p1 and marked provisional: Lachine's house is 1669–71 and Baie-D'Urfé's about 1700, both inside p1, but Pointe-Claire's classed survivor was built between 1769 and 1791, inside p2 — RPCQ states plainly that this building tradition « se poursuit au-delà de la Conquête (1760) », so the type is filed at its origin and the reader is told the fabric outlives it.

1760–1855 · Farms on the shore, and the Anglo-Saxon retrofit

1760 – 1855

Farming, and then the Anglo-Saxon retrofit. A census of 1781 records a house, a barn and a stable on the Rangé property. Charles Lenoir buys it after 1781 and may have run it as an inn for some fifteen years at the turn of the nineteenth century; it then passes to Gabriel Pilon, who farms it and returns it to a dwelling, and stays in the Pilon family until 1874. Between 1839 and 1874 the Pilons raise the stone walls, slacken the pitch of the roof, and add moulded wooden window casings, overhanging larmiers and a covered gallery on the south side — elements the RPCQ attributes to the Anglo-Saxon architectural tradition introduced after the Conquest, and to the picturesque taste of the period, « une sensibilité nouvelle par rapport à la nature ». In 1874 André Perrier buys the property and gives the central pavilion neo-Gothic attributes and the old south front its three dormers. This is a phase with no village in it: no parish church, no core, only farms on a road.

1855–1930 · The railway, the summer villas and the charter of 1911

1855 – 1930

The Grand Trunk reaches the West Island in 1855, and a railway station close to the shore opens the lac Saint-Louis to summer people. The cahier describes the pattern: at the end of the nineteenth century the lakeshore is taken up for villégiature, and in summer « les abords du lac sont envahis par une population aisée d'entrepreneurs et de professionnels »; residents then settle permanently and commute by train. Beaconsfield is incorporated in 1910 with fewer than 400 permanent residents; a year later Baie-d'Urfé is incorporated with fewer than 200, its territory detached from Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue. The charter was granted in March 1911 and the first council met on 10 July, with no election: the first mayor, Vivian de Vere Dowker, and the councillors were acclaimed. At that first meeting James Morgan announced that he would give the town a waterfront property for a town hall, a park for all the citizens, and a street that still bears his name; in October 1912 he gave the white-frame farmhouse that was converted into the town hall, and in 1913 the architects Edward and William Maxwell renovated and enlarged it. Edward Maxwell also built the large bourgeois villa at 211 rue Oakridge.

1930–2002 · The population quintuples, and the merger

1930 – 2002

Farming holds on into the early twentieth century while the summer people animate the shore; streets are opened and subdivided but development stays south of the railway and moves far more slowly than in Pointe-Claire. Then, in the 1950s and 1960s, the Metropolitan expressway and the postwar demographic boom accelerate a suburb that is essentially residential, and building crosses to the north side of the tracks. The town's own account is blunt about the scale of it: the population increased fivefold in the 1950s. The cahier's verdict on the result is that the rural character disappears but the tree-lined streets keep a country look, and that the parks and the lac Saint-Louis go on attracting the more comfortable classes of Montréal society. In 2001 the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette is cited by the municipality. On 1 January 2002 Baie-d'Urfé is merged into the new ville de Montréal.

Wikimedia Commons was searched for a freely licensed photograph of a postwar bungalow in any of the six places and none was found. The one candidate that looked promising by its address — « Quatre-Vents, 12, avenue Dahlia (Dorval) », on the Model Homes street itself — was downloaded and inspected and is not a bungalow at all but a two-and-a-half-storey stuccoed house with a tiled coping and an arcaded loggia, so it was rejected. The Cité de Dorval's own heritage study of that address (LEMAYMICHAUD + Réal Paul architecte, « Expertise historique et étude patrimoniale, 12, avenue Dahlia, Dorval ») confirms the rejection and dates the house to 1803, enlarged 1874–85 in a neo-Gothic manner and wholly remade 1927–37 into the Italianate it wears now — one of the first stone houses in the district, standing on the street the bungalows were later built along, and not one of them. The same study independently repeats the cahier's figures for the 1951 project - 28 dwellings, 12 detached and 16 semi-detached bungalows, and two apartment buildings.

One storey, hipped roof, brick with artificial stone and clapboard for variety, a carport or a garage, a cul-de-sac and mature trees. Built from a short catalogue of models across all six West Island places from the late 1940s, and the only twentieth-century housing the 2005 evaluation series took seriously enough to invent a category for.

Where it comes from

The wave arrives at different moments and for different reasons. At Dorval it is the airport: the aerodrome opened in 1941, air traffic grew fast after the war, the rest of the territory was opened to subdivision and the population went from under 3,000 in the 1940s to more than 20,000 by 1971. At Pointe-Claire it is a straight housing shortage — a veterans' sector on the Wartime Housing Limited model about 1947, then the Magil project of 1953–55. At Lachine it is what the cahier calls « l'urbanisation massive des années 1950-1970 », running east across the last open ground and producing a catalogue of contractors' types so consistent that the dominant one is still known locally by its builder's name. At Baie-D'Urfé it is the Métropolitaine and the demographic boom together, pushing settlement north across the railway for the first time. What the 2005 cahiers do with all of it is the interesting part. The series invented a category, Ensembles urbains d'intérêt, to hold postwar suburbs, said openly that the exercise was exploratory, and then valued these places for their street layouts, their planting and the consistency of their materials rather than for their architecture — while recording, in the same paragraphs, that almost every house had already been raised, extended, re-clad or re-roofed. It is the one wave on these six pages that the source treats as heritage-in-progress rather than as heritage.

Siting & landscape
  • Detached on its own lot, set back from the street with side margins, and with parking in the front yard — the Lachine cahier's description of the same wave, and the one place any West Island source describes the siting rule rather than the building.
  • Cul-de-sac and crescent street patterns recur, sometimes with a planted median down a wide avenue, and Dorval's cahier ties the effect of every one of these sectors to mature trees on the properties rather than to the houses.
  • Built in small series from a short catalogue of models. Dorval's 7.U.5 is praised for using « un nombre relativement restreint de modèles » while letting the materials vary house to house; Lachine's 19.U.2 calls the catalogue of types on its territory « particulièrement impressionnant ». In Lachine the dominant form of that catalogue is not this one but the taller contractor's box known locally as the maison « Gameroff », which carries its own record on that page; what belongs here is the alignment of Californian-inspired bungalows the cahier singles out at the end of the 51e Avenue.
Massing
  • One storey, plain-pied. The Dorval cahier repeats the phrase « bungalows de type plain-pied, toit pavillon, brique » for two separate sectors, 7.U.1 and 7.U.4, and treats it as the norm for the municipality from the 1940s–50s onward.
  • A hipped roof is the commonest form. Lachine records more variety in the same years — pitched, hipped, or hipped combined with a false mansard.
  • Split-level versions appear in the same wave and the same decade; Dorval's 7.U.5 dates a whole quadrilateral of them to the 1950s.
Articulation
  • Almost none, and what there is varies within a single street rather than between streets. Dorval's Model Homes are described as houses that look alike until you look closely, at which point the window types, the roofs, the use of artificial stone and clapboard and the handling of the brick are all different.
Openings
  • Not described by any West Island source beyond the note that window types differ between otherwise identical Dorval houses.
Materials
  • Brick dominant, with artificial stone and clapboard used as accent — the three materials Dorval's cahier names on avenue Dahlia.
  • Red brick at Pointe-Claire's Magil project, built 1953–55.

Better evidenced than the other four shared records, and still not evidenced everywhere. The 2005 Ville de Montréal series created a category — Ensembles urbains d'intérêt — expressly to catch postwar suburban housing, and Dorval's cahier alone gives it eight sectors with real building description, so the columns here are unusually well supported for a place with no typology document. But the support is not even across the six places, and that is the thing to know before trusting this record. Dorval and Pointe-Claire are described in detail. Lachine is well described too, but most of what its sector 19.U.2 describes is the one- or two-storey contractor's box that Lachine's own page records as the maison « Gameroff »; only the Californian bungalow alignment at the 51e Avenue is unambiguously this type, and that is what is claimed here. Senneville is attested by one clause in sector 2.E.4, which records postwar houses inside the estate belt and says they continue its tradition of setting buildings into the landscape — no bungalow sector of its own is named, and the well described bungalow sector in the same cahier, 2.U.2 Belvédère, is in the Pierrefonds half of that arrondissement and not in Senneville. Baie-D'Urfé is attested only by its cahier's historique, which dates the acceleration of its suburban growth to the 1950s–60s and to the Métropolitaine, and names no postwar sector; the postwar ensembles in that cahier, 4.U.1 Devon-York with its « bâtiments de l'après-guerre … rappelant le "ranch" californien », 4.U.2 Sunny Acres and 4.U.3 Lakeview-Morgan, are introduced under a paragraph about the territory of Beaconsfield. Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue is the one membership no retrieved source attests at all: its cahier, once found, names no postwar sector and its single ensemble urbain d'intérêt, 1.U.1, is the Garden City Press ensemble of J.-J. Harpell, which is not this type. It is kept in `places` because the record is the shared West Island postwar record and because a later source may supply what this one does not, and it is flagged here rather than asserted quietly. Fields left null and why: `roofing` — no source names a roof covering anywhere in the six places; `roof.pitch_deg` — « toit pavillon » with no figure; `lot_width_m`, `setback_front_m`, `setback_side_m`, `front_yard_green_pct` — the cahiers describe setbacks, front-yard parking and landscaping at length and measure none of it; `sectors` — a shared record cannot carry sector codes that exist in only one of its places, and here they would run 7.U.x, 5.U.x, 19.U.x and 2.U.x at once. `window_proportion: horizontal` is the weakest entry in the record: it is read from the form and from the Dorval note that window types vary between otherwise identical houses, not from any stated proportion, and it should be the first field to correct. `styles: [minimal-traditional]` is a classification made here, not a style any West Island source names; the nearest a source comes is Dorval's « banlieue de type californien » and Lachine's « bungalows d'inspiration californienne ».

Conservation guidance (fiche)
  • This is the only wave in the West Island whose recognition is explicitly experimental. The 2005 series says its own identification of these sectors « est exploratoire » and aims to link them to conservation concerns « pour une première fois ».
  • Where the cahiers return to it, they say the same thing twice - the value lies in the plan, the planting and the consistency of the materials rather than in any individual house, and personalisation is what erodes it. Lachine's 19.U.2 records that practically every building in its sector has been modified, and lists how: raising, building to the lot line, added upper storeys or a garage, changed cladding, changed openings, changed roof form.
  • Pointe-Claire's veterans' sector is the sharpest case: the cahier says few of the wartime houses are left and that what still marks the sector is the street layout and the subdivision, not the buildings.

2002–2026 · Merger, demerger, and inventory

2002 – 2026

The town is reconstituted on 1 January 2006 under Bill 9, after the referendums of 20 June 2004, with effect from 1 January. In 2024 the agglomeration of Montréal begins the inventory of pre-1940 buildings that the 2021 Loi sur le patrimoine culturel requires it to finish by 1 April 2026, using the ministry's methodology and grading on historical, architectural and social value, the survival of original characteristics, and state of maintenance. Baie-D'Urfé publishes its share of the result as annexe C of a new plan d'urbanisme, règlement 1108, adopted on 17 June 2025 and in force on 20 October 2025: forty-eight records, thirty-nine addresses, four grades, and not one line of description. The same by-law sets out, for the first time in a public document, exactly what protects them.

Notes on the approach

Baie-D'Urfé has no protected ensemble, and its own plan d'urbanisme says what it has instead. The RPCQ holds exactly one immovable record for the entire municipality — the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette — and a search of the RPCQ filtered to catégorie de bien « Site patrimonial » and municipality Baie-D'Urfé returns « Aucun résultat »; the same search across the Montréal region returns forty site records, none of them here. That is the provincial side. The municipal side is stated by the town itself in section 2.2.3.1 of règlement 1108, which is worth reading closely because it is an unusually honest piece of drafting. The exceptional sector — the parc Fritz and the hôtel de ville site — is safe, it says, « du fait qu'il est entièrement propriété municipale ». Every other heritage building, including the temple Dao En, is protected « par une application stricte du règlement sur les démolitions ». Infill is managed by the sheer size of the lots — 2,000 and 4,000 m² minimums, with the required front and side margins and the abundance of planting — and the by-law notes with satisfaction that those minimums « présentent aussi l'avantage d'exclure pratiquement toute possibilité de morcellement des propriétés ». Finally the PIIA. Ownership, a demolition permit, big lots and design review: four instruments, none of them a designation.

The one designation there is, is narrower than it looks. The maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette was citée by the municipality on 11 December 2001, and the RPCQ states the limit precisely: « La protection s'applique uniquement à l'enveloppe extérieure de la partie la plus ancienne de l'ensemble, soit la section est du bâtiment. » The house today is three volumes in an L — the c. 1700 east section, a central pavilion the Pilons are thought to have raised between 1839 and 1874 and Perrier gave neo-Gothic attributes in 1874, and a west pavilion built in 1949 by William Lawrence Glen. Only the first is cited, and only its skin. It is still a private dwelling.

The brief that commissioned this page counted two buildings where the sources have one. It listed Maison Rangé-dit-Laviolette (c. 1700) and, separately, a 1703 stone building at 20122 Lakeshore. The RPCQ gives 20122 chemin Lakeshore as the address of the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette and dates its construction « vers 1700 – 1700 »; the 2005 cahier lists 20122 chemin du Lakeshore as « Maison Rangé (maison Lenoir), monument historique cité »; the town's annexe C has one principal and one secondary building at that address. There is one house, and no source this project read gives it the date 1703. The brief also spelled the name with a lower-case dit; the RPCQ writes Maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette, and that spelling is used here.

The fiche disagrees with itself about the windows. Its valeur patrimoniale lists « la disposition asymétrique des ouvertures » among the elements that tie the house to the French tradition; its éléments caractéristiques list « les ouvertures, dont leur disposition symétrique ». Both statements are in the same record. They are reproduced here as they stand, and neither is used to fill a column.

The fiche is also out of date about where the house is. Its description ends « La maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette est située dans l'arrondissement municipal de Beaconsfield-Baie-D'Urfé de la ville de Montréal », and its source line reads Ville de Montréal, 2005. That arrondissement was dissolved when Baie-D'Urfé was reconstituted on 1 January 2006, and the RPCQ has not revised the sentence.

Three dates circulate for the Sulpician concession, and they are not the same event. The 2005 cahier says the first lands here were conceded by the Sulpicians in 1678. The RPCQ says « une première concession est accordée en 1677 » and that the lot under the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette was conceded by the Sulpicians in 1686, the year the abbé Lascaris d'Urfé became the first resident curé of Saint-Louis-du-Bout-de-l'Île. The place record uses 1686 because that is the date attached to the one surviving building, and the others are recorded here rather than reconciled.

The town hall's dates come from two different sources and both are kept. The town says James Morgan gave the waterfront land at the first council meeting of 10 July 1911 and gave « the white-frame farmhouse for conversion to a Town Hall » in October 1912. The 2005 cahier and the 2025 plan d'urbanisme both say the building was renovated and enlarged in 1913 by the Maxwells — the plan naming them as Edward and William Maxwell. A farmhouse given in 1912 and enlarged by Montréal's most fashionable architects in 1913 is one story, not two.

The two lists of heritage buildings do not match, and the mismatch is published above. The Ville de Montréal named seventeen Baie-D'Urfé addresses as immeubles de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle in 2005. The town's own annexe C of 2025 grades thirty-nine addresses. Sixteen appear on both. Twenty-three are new in 2025. And one — 20658 chemin du Lakeshore, exceptional in 2005 — is simply not in the 2025 inventory, with no explanation in either document. The full row-by-row comparison is in the published CSV.

What could not be obtained. The Inventaire des bâtiments patrimoniaux du secteur Baie-d'Urfé de l'arrondissement Beaconsfield–Baie d'Urfé de la Ville de Montréal by Pierre Beaupré and Josette Michaud — cited by the 2005 cahier as Cahier 1 – Textes préliminaires, sommaire de l'inventaire et liste des bâtiments, a rapport d'étape dated 7 April 2003, and by the RPCQ in its completed 2004 form — is the one document that would turn this page's address lists into building descriptions. It is cited twice by institutional sources: in the 2005 cahier's bibliography, and in the RPCQ's own reference list for the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette, together with the same firm's 19-page Évaluation de l'intérêt patrimonial de l'édifice situé au 20 122, chemin Lakeshore of 2000. A second and separate 2004 document, Inventaire des bâtiments patrimoniaux du secteur Baie D'Urfé, cahier des fiches, published by the Ville de Montréal in the summer of 2004 and not the work of Beaupré and Michaud, is reported to exist but has not been seen either. Neither is published by the town; neither is on the architects' site, which lists the inventory only as a completed project; and no repository copy was found. Behind all of them stands the Répertoire d'architecture traditionnelle de la Communauté urbaine de Montréal, the 1980s survey that both towns' later documents draw on, likewise not obtained. Until one of them is read, the columns on this page's type cards stay empty.
