Residential Architectural Styles of Pointe-Claire

The pointe Claire is a flat spit of land running south into Lac Saint-Louis, with a small presqu'île hanging off it on a neck of ground. Everything institutional this parish ever built stands on it. The Sulpicians, seigneurs of the island since 1663, reserved the point in 1698 to put up a windmill, and the mill went up in 1709–1710 — a fieldstone moulin-tour with two opposed doors, a conical roof and a tail to turn it into the wind, built by the carpenters Léonard and Charles Paillé and the master mason Jean Mars. A presbytère-chapelle came in 1705 and the first church in 1713. Around 1729, with Franco-Iroquois relations deteriorating, a stockade of stakes went up around church and presbytery and the mill, loopholed, served as the redoubt. What stands today is the fourth church, of 1882–1885, by Victor Bourgeau and his associate Alcibiade Leprohon; the convent of 1867–1868 by Henri-Maurice Perrault; the presbytery of 1913 by Théodose Daoust, built on the masonry body of its 1848 predecessor; and the école Marguerite-Bourgeoys, whose oldest wing dates from 1928.

On 2 April 2013 the city cited the whole of it — about 2.7 hectares — as a site patrimonial under by-law PC-2808, and the protection it enacted is not cosmetic. It covers the ground as well as the buildings: « La protection s'applique aux terrains ainsi qu'à l'enveloppe extérieure des constructions qui s'y élèvent. » Subdivision, new building, alteration, repair, signage, street furniture, landscaping, excavation and the felling of a mature tree all need forty-five days' notice and may be made subject to conditions; demolition needs council authorisation; every excavation needs archaeological supervision; and three named public artworks must stay where they are.

Hold that beside the Mille carré doré. Two point seven hectares of parish infrastructure on a point in the West Island are formally designated, with the ground included, by a by-law with a number and a date. The Mille carré doré — the richest concentration of domestic architecture ever built on the island of Montréal — has no ensemble protection at all. Designation in Québec tracks legibility and local politics, not architectural quality, and this page is one half of the demonstration.

Behind the point the village itself runs back along the chemin du Bord-du-Lac in an L, and the city governs it through something unusual: a Code villageois, a form-based code that replaces zoning, subdivision, PIIA and conditional uses for the village sector with one document organised around ten unités paysagères. It claims, in its own preamble, to be the first of its kind in Québec. Its third chapter is the only text anywhere in Pointe-Claire that names house types, and it names three: the traditional Québec architecture of the oldest sectors, the Boomtown, and the Cubique.

Beyond the village the ground was subdivided in a chain of garden suburbs laid out parallel to the shore road: Cedar Park from 1893, the Bowling Green by Frederick G. Todd in 1905 for the Canadian Nursery Company, Upper Bowling Green and Killarney Gardens after 1912, and the houses on the avenue du Golf, built by the founding members of the Beaconsfield Golf Club under a design committee and a minimum spend. Then the resorts around the baie de Valois; then, after 1947 and north of the railway, a veterans' sector on the Wartime Housing Limited model. One document describes all of that and nothing else does — the Ville de Montréal's 2005 Évaluation du patrimoine urbain, made while Pointe-Claire was a borough and left without force when it demerged.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

5.E.1 La Pointe exceptional The same ground the city cited in 2013, described eight years earlier by the Ville de Montréal. The cahier calls the mill one of the oldest vestiges of the settlement of the island, says it served as the seigneurs' moulin banal until 1857, that the Congrégation de Notre-Dame acquired it in 1866 and that it was restored in the 1950s and 1960s; nearby stands the second convent, which Henri-Maurice Perreault designed for the sisters in 1867-1868 to replace the first convent of 1787. The present church of 1881-1885 it calls a replica of a first Bourgeau church begun in 1868 and destroyed by fire in 1881, and it records that the presbytery of 1848 was transformed in 1913 to plans by Théo Daoust. The wooden cross raised on the point in 1900, the « croix des missions », is one of the rare survivors on the island of Montréal and replaces an older one put up by the sisters in the 19th century.
Read against the RPCQ fiche and by-law PC-2808, this paragraph disagrees with them and with itself — see the place notes. 1663–1760 · Seigneurial côtes and the fur-trade posts

1663 – 1760

In 1698 the priests of the Séminaire de Saint-Sulpice, seigneurs of the island of Montréal since 1663, reserve a piece of the ground called the pointe Claire for a windmill, which the seigneurial regime obliges them to build for their censitaires. The mill goes up in 1709–1710 and is leased to millers who work, maintain and repair it against the annual dues. Settlement follows the mill: a presbytère-chapelle in 1705, the first church in 1713, all at the end of the point. Around 1729 a stockade is thrown round church and presbytery and the mill becomes the redoubt; the same defensive use is recorded at Senneville and Châteauguay, on the same lake. Over the next three decades a handful of emplacements conceded behind the first cemetery form the embryo of a bourg.

Stone house of the French tradition La maison de pierre de tradition française

Style: French regime
source ›
Phase attribution: provisional

A low fieldstone box of one and a half storeys under a steep two-slope roof, stacks rising inside the gable walls, openings placed where they were wanted rather than where symmetry asked for them. Three of them survive across three of the six West Island places — a trading post of 1669–71 at Lachine, a farm house of about 1700 at Baie-D'Urfé, and one of 1769–91 at Pointe-Claire — and all three are protected individually while nothing around them is. One record, three municipalities: the photograph on this card is Lachine's, and Baie-D'Urfé's and Pointe-Claire's stand beside it on the type page.

Where it comes from

The Sulpicians became seigneurs of the island of Montréal in 1663 and conceded the côtes that run out to its western end; the houses built on those concessions were stone because stone was what the fields gave up and because fire and raiding made anything else a poor bet. What survives is very little. Lachine's is the oldest and least altered: two rectangular stone buildings put up in 1669–71 as a fur-trade post for Charles Le Moyne and Jacques Le Ber, restored in the 1980s toward their seventeenth-century appearance and now the Musée de Lachine. Baie-D'Urfé's was built about 1700 by Hubert Rangé dit Laviolette on a concession he bought in 1695, and then quietly rebuilt by the Pilon family between 1839 and 1874 — the walls raised, the roof pitch eased off, moulded chambranles and projecting eaves added in what RPCQ calls a taste imported after the Conquest. Pointe-Claire's went up between 1769 and 1791 on a censive conceded in 1722, and was altered again in 1828. Read together the three make the point the West Island pages keep making: the form is one form across three municipalities, the protection is three separate individual instruments granted in 1964, 2001 and 2001, and not one of them protects an ensemble.

Siting & landscape
  • Each of the three survivors sits on ground that predates the street grid around it. Le Ber-Le Moyne stands on a raised point of land beside lac Saint-Louis at the head of the Lachine rapids, on a slight mound planted with mature trees.

None of the six West Island places has a per-type typology document — no inventory, no thesaurus, no per-type table — so this record is built from the three individual houses that do carry statutory records, plus the three 2005 Ville de Montréal évaluation cahiers, which characterise sectors and not building types. What that leaves empty: `window_proportion` is null because all three RPCQ fiches describe the windows as casements with small panes and none gives a proportion; `roof.pitch_deg` is null because the fiches say "aigu" and "à pente moyenne" and never a figure; `lot_width_m`, `setback_front_m`, `setback_side_m` and `front_yard_green_pct` are null because no source measures a lot or a setback anywhere in the West Island; `sectors` is null because a shared record cannot carry sector codes that exist in only one of its places. `storeys` is entered as 1.5 from the RPCQ "Nombre d'étages" field, which reads 1½ for all three — note that the Le Ber-Le Moyne description text contradicts its own data field, saying the buildings "s'élèvent sur un étage". The canonical is `french-urban-house-stone` rather than `french-rural-house-1st-hip` even though all three are farm or trading-post houses in open country: the discriminant in this vocabulary is fabric and roof, not siting, and all three are stone under a straight two-slope roof with stacks in the gable walls, where the rural canonical is pièce-sur-pièce timber under a hip carried to the wall head with no cellar. RPCQ says as much itself, calling the nearby Sainte-Anne example « la maison rurale d'inspiration urbaine, fréquente dans la région de Montréal et les régions avoisinantes ». The phase is p1 and marked provisional: Lachine's house is 1669–71 and Baie-D'Urfé's about 1700, both inside p1, but Pointe-Claire's classed survivor was built between 1769 and 1791, inside p2 — RPCQ states plainly that this building tradition « se poursuit au-delà de la Conquête (1760) », so the type is filed at its origin and the reader is told the fabric outlives it.

Traditional Québec house of the village core La maison de tradition québécoise du noyau villageois

Style: Québec neoclassical
source ›

The ordinary house of the two West Island village cores — modest, one and a half to two storeys, rectangular, under a steep two-slope roof with dormers, standing close to a narrow street, in clapboard at Sainte-Anne and roughcast at Pointe-Claire. Both places advertise a heritage village. Between them they have three protected buildings and no protected ensemble. One record, two municipalities: the photograph on this card is a Sainte-Anne street, and Pointe-Claire's stands beside it on the type page.

Where it comes from

Pointe-Claire's parish dates from 1698 and Sainte-Anne's core grew around a mission that took its present name in 1714, and both produced a small village around the church rather than a string of farms along a côte. A plan of 1767 already shows Pointe-Claire's clustered on the three avenues it still occupies; by the nineteenth century it held hotels, artisans' workshops and shops serving the surrounding farms and the first summer people on the lake shore. Most of what stands there now is younger than that, because a fire in 1900 took a good part of it — the cahier says so plainly, and then argues that the value survived the fire anyway, in the width of the streets and the height of the houses. Sainte-Anne's core sits at the point of the island between the canal locks of 1843 and the railway bridges, and the 2005 cahier separates it into two things worth naming - the îlot villageois round the church, of two-storey clapboard houses built close to the street, and the workers' cottages of rue Saint-Jean-Baptiste, steep-roofed and dormered and low-floored, which it calls the most characteristic thing in the town's architectural heritage. One classed house, built between 1790 and 1810 for the fur trader Peter Grant and bought by Simon Fraser in 1820, shows the whole arc in a single building: a French-tradition rubble carré under a steep roof, then a bell-cast eave and two rows of ornamented dormers added as taste changed, then two flats in 1892, then a bank counter from 1906 to 1954, then a restoration in 1962 that kept the evidence of all of it.

1855–1930 · The railway, the resorts and the country estates

1855 – 1930

In 1867 the Fabrique wants a bigger church and a bigger cemetery and has no room, so it takes the ground behind the church where the convent stands and gives the sisters the pointe du Moulin just south in exchange. Their new convent, to plans by the architect and surveyor Henri-Maurice Perrault, is finished in 1868. The unfinished new church burns in 1881; a fourth, monumental, néogothique church is built between 1882 and 1885 to plans by Victor Bourgeau and Alcibiade Leprohon, and the new cemetery behind it swallows the rue du Couvent. At that point the village has about a hundred houses — three times as many as a century before — and about five hundred inhabitants, and it forms an L with the chemin du Bord-du-Lac as its main street, lined with hotels and shops. In 1896 the Fabrique buys the last property on the south of the point, an inn, and turns it into a school. The mill falls out of use between 1880 and 1885, loses its conical roof to a terrace carrying a wind pump for the convent's private aqueduct, and becomes an observatory. A fire — dated 1900 twice and 1901 once in the Code villageois — takes out much of the village, and the two styles that rebuild it are the Boomtown and the Cubique. On the avenue du Golf, meanwhile, a country suburb of Shingle, Arts and Crafts and Craftsman houses goes up on very large treed lots in a single short campaign.

A massive, symmetrical two-storey block with an imposing gallery across the front, under a low hipped roof. It is the second of the two styles the Code villageois says rebuilt Pointe-Claire after the fire, and the Code describes it in a single sentence — which is exactly why it is worth carding: the record shows what a form-based code does and does not bother to say about a house.

Where it comes from

The Cubique arrives with the Boomtown, after the fire that took much of the village at the turn of the 20th century, and the Code sets the two side by side as the pair that "créent une certaine homogénéité". It survives in three of the ten landscape units, and the company it keeps in each of their style rows is worth reading: in UP5 it stands with Boomtown, Second Empire, mansard and vernaculaire industriel; in UP8 with vernaculaire industriel and Boomtown; in UP10 with Boomtown alone. Elsewhere on this site the same form is the maison cubique of Charlesbourg and the maison cubique of Gatineau and of the Île d'Orléans — a form that half of Québec's sources treat as a top-level type and the other half as a sub-variant of the industrial vernacular. Pointe-Claire's Code, characteristically, does neither: it treats it as a style.

The Code ties it to a rebuilding. The traditional Québec architecture of the oldest sectors, it says, recalls the village as it was up to the fire; after the fire, two styles produce a certain homogeneity, and the Boomtown, "apparue au début du 20e siècle", is the first of them. That is the whole of the origin story the source gives, and it is worth noticing what kind of document gives it: not an inventory, not a conservation plan, but a zoning by-law written on form-based principles, which had to describe the village's architecture in order to prescribe more of it. Where it comes from

The wave arrives at different moments and for different reasons. At Dorval it is the airport: the aerodrome opened in 1941, air traffic grew fast after the war, the rest of the territory was opened to subdivision and the population went from under 3,000 in the 1940s to more than 20,000 by 1971. At Pointe-Claire it is a straight housing shortage — a veterans' sector on the Wartime Housing Limited model about 1947, then the Magil project of 1953–55. At Lachine it is what the cahier calls « l'urbanisation massive des années 1950-1970 », running east across the last open ground and producing a catalogue of contractors' types so consistent that the dominant one is still known locally by its builder's name. At Baie-D'Urfé it is the Métropolitaine and the demographic boom together, pushing settlement north across the railway for the first time. What the 2005 cahiers do with all of it is the interesting part. The series invented a category, Ensembles urbains d'intérêt, to hold postwar suburbs, said openly that the exercise was exploratory, and then valued these places for their street layouts, their planting and the consistency of their materials rather than for their architecture — while recording, in the same paragraphs, that almost every house had already been raised, extended, re-clad or re-roofed. It is the one wave on these six pages that the source treats as heritage-in-progress rather than as heritage.

Historical context (fiche)
  • « Le Plan d'urbanisme reconnaît l'intérêt de certains développements résidentiels d'après-guerre, qui composent une part importante du patrimoine des anciennes banlieues, en créant une catégorie appelée Ensembles urbains d'intérêt. On y retrouve, entre autres, des ensembles homogènes de bungalows, des secteurs de maisons qui adoptent le modèle développé par la Wartime Housing Limited ou des exemples des premières coopératives d'habitation. Leur repérage est exploratoire … » (the same paragraph in all twenty-seven cahiers)

Même si le caractère rural disparaît, les rues bordées d'arbres maintiennent une apparence champêtre. » (§ 2, Historique)
Siting & landscape
  • Detached on its own lot, set back from the street with side margins, and with parking in the front yard — the Lachine cahier's description of the same wave, and the one place any West Island source describes the siting rule rather than the building.
  • Cul-de-sac and crescent street patterns recur, sometimes with a planted median down a wide avenue, and Dorval's cahier ties the effect of every one of these sectors to mature trees on the properties rather than to the houses.
  • Built in small series from a short catalogue of models. Dorval's 7.U.5 is praised for using « un nombre relativement restreint de modèles » while letting the materials vary house to house; Lachine's 19.U.2 calls the catalogue of types on its territory « particulièrement impressionnant ». In Lachine the dominant form of that catalogue is not this one but the taller contractor's box known locally as the maison « Gameroff », which carries its own record on that page; what belongs here is the alignment of Californian-inspired bungalows the cahier singles out at the end of the 51e Avenue.
Massing
  • One storey, plain-pied. The Dorval cahier repeats the phrase « bungalows de type plain-pied, toit pavillon, brique » for two separate sectors, 7.U.1 and 7.U.4, and treats it as the norm for the municipality from the 1940s–50s onward.
  • A hipped roof is the commonest form. Lachine records more variety in the same years — pitched, hipped, or hipped combined with a false mansard.
  • Split-level versions appear in the same wave and the same decade; Dorval's 7.U.5 dates a whole quadrilateral of them to the 1950s.
Articulation
  • Almost none, and what there is varies within a single street rather than between streets. Dorval's Model Homes are described as houses that look alike until you look closely, at which point the window types, the roofs, the use of artificial stone and clapboard and the handling of the brick are all different.
Openings
  • Not described by any West Island source beyond the note that window types differ between otherwise identical Dorval houses.
Materials
  • Brick dominant, with artificial stone and clapboard used as accent — the three materials Dorval's cahier names on avenue Dahlia.
  • Red brick at Pointe-Claire's Magil project, built 1953–55.

Better evidenced than the other four shared records, and still not evidenced everywhere. The 2005 Ville de Montréal series created a category — Ensembles urbains d'intérêt — expressly to catch postwar suburban housing, and Dorval's cahier alone gives it eight sectors with real building description, so the columns here are unusually well supported for a place with no typology document. But the support is not even across the six places, and that is the thing to know before trusting this record. Dorval and Pointe-Claire are described in detail. Lachine is well described too, but most of what its sector 19.U.2 describes is the one- or two-storey contractor's box that Lachine's own page records as the maison « Gameroff »; only the Californian bungalow alignment at the 51e Avenue is unambiguously this type, and that is what is claimed here. Senneville is attested by one clause in sector 2.E.4, which records postwar houses inside the estate belt and says they continue its tradition of setting buildings into the landscape — no bungalow sector of its own is named, and the well described bungalow sector in the same cahier, 2.U.2 Belvédère, is in the Pierrefonds half of that arrondissement and not in Senneville. Baie-D'Urfé is attested only by its cahier's historique, which dates the acceleration of its suburban growth to the 1950s–60s and to the Métropolitaine, and names no postwar sector; the postwar ensembles in that cahier, 4.U.1 Devon-York with its « bâtiments de l'après-guerre … rappelant le "ranch" californien », 4.U.2 Sunny Acres and 4.U.3 Lakeview-Morgan, are introduced under a paragraph about the territory of Beaconsfield. Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue is the one membership no retrieved source attests at all: its cahier, once found, names no postwar sector and its single ensemble urbain d'intérêt, 1.U.1, is the Garden City Press ensemble of J.-J. Harpell, which is not this type. It is kept in `places` because the record is the shared West Island postwar record and because a later source may supply what this one does not, and it is flagged here rather than asserted quietly. Fields left null and why: `roofing` — no source names a roof covering anywhere in the six places; `roof.pitch_deg` — « toit pavillon » with no figure; `lot_width_m`, `setback_front_m`, `setback_side_m`, `front_yard_green_pct` — the cahiers describe setbacks, front-yard parking and landscaping at length and measure none of it; `sectors` — a shared record cannot carry sector codes that exist in only one of its places, and here they would run 7.U.x, 5.U.x, 19.U.x and 2.U.x at once. `window_proportion: horizontal` is the weakest entry in the record: it is read from the form and from the Dorval note that window types vary between otherwise identical houses, not from any stated proportion, and it should be the first field to correct. `styles: [minimal-traditional]` is a classification made here, not a style any West Island source names; the nearest a source comes is Dorval's « banlieue de type californien » and Lachine's « bungalows d'inspiration californienne ».

Conservation guidance (fiche)
  • This is the only wave in the West Island whose recognition is explicitly experimental. The 2005 series says its own identification of these sectors « est exploratoire » and aims to link them to conservation concerns « pour une première fois ».
  • Where the cahiers return to it, they say the same thing twice - the value lies in the plan, the planting and the consistency of the materials rather than in any individual house, and personalisation is what erodes it. Lachine's 19.U.2 records that practically every building in its sector has been modified, and lists how: raising, building to the lot line, added upper storeys or a garage, changed cladding, changed openings, changed roof form.
  • Pointe-Claire's veterans' sector is the sharpest case: the cahier says few of the wartime houses are left and that what still marks the sector is the street layout and the subdivision, not the buildings.

Houses built about 1947 to the Wartime Housing Limited pattern, on the ground north of the railway that was the last part of Pointe-Claire to be opened. The programme was a wartime answer to a housing crisis; afterwards promoters took the model up for buyers who wanted an affordable detached house of their own. Few of the houses are left. What is left is the street plan.

Where it comes from

Pointe-Claire's population went from 1,240 in 1925 to 8,753 in 1951 and 21,926 ten years after that, and the veterans' sector is the front edge of that curve. The cahier sets it against the projet Magil in Lakeside Heights, one-and-a-half-storey cottages and red brick bungalows of 1953–1955 that it calls remarkable as an ensemble and little altered — the same postwar wave, ten years later, and much better preserved. Between them they are why the sentence the cahier closes its assessment with is worth quoting: « le tracé des rues et le lotissement de l'ancien quartier des vétérans de même que les formes architecturales du projet Magil nous plongent dans les principes d'urbanisme popularisés après la Seconde Guerre mondiale. »

2002–2026 · Demerger, citation and a form-based code

2002 – 2026

Pointe-Claire demerges. The referendums of 20 June 2004 and Bill 9 reconstitute it as a city on 1 January 2006, and it uses the municipal power that comes back with reconstitution: a notice of motion on 3 December 2012, and on 2 April 2013 the adoption of by-law PC-2808 citing the institutional core as a site patrimonial. In the decade after, the city rewrites its urbanism instruments around the village: the PIIA by-law PC-2787 grows a chapter listing some three hundred buildings of heritage interest by civic address and another making the chemin du Bord-du-Lac a protected panoramic route, and by-law PC-2880, the Code villageois, replaces the zoning of the village sector with a form-based code of ten landscape units, amended three times between November 2024 and October 2025.

Notes on the approach

The by-law number, which Part 11 listed as outstanding, is PC-2808. It is « Règlement sur la citation du noyau institutionnel de Pointe-Claire comme site patrimonial », carried by resolution 2013-123 at the ordinary sitting of the council of Tuesday 2 April 2013 at 19h30, moved by conseiller Geller and seconded by conseiller Smith, and signed by mayor Bill McMurchie and the greffier Jean-Denis Jacob. It is listed on the city's own by-law index and the full text is published. Two details are worth recording. First, the title cites the noyau institutionnel, not the pointe: the RPCQ carries both names, and the by-law's annexed énoncé d'importance calls the place « le site du patrimoine du Noyau-Institutionnel-de-Pointe-Claire » throughout. Second, the by-law's cover carries a line that reads oddly beside the adoption date — « En vigueur le 12 décembre 2012 / Publication le 10 avril 2013 » — so the instrument is in force from a date four months before the council adopted it, nine days after the notice of motion of 3 December 2012. That is the same shape as the Vieux-Terrebonne declaration on this site, where protection also runs from the ministerial notice rather than from the decree that concludes the process; but this site has not retrieved the provision that produces the 12 December date, and does not assert one.

Where the RPCQ fiche garbles its own source. The site fiche says of the mill: « Après une restauration dans les années 1960, deux ailes sont ajoutées à l'école, et le toit conique est rétabli. » Two sails added to the school, in a sentence that is otherwise about the mill — and two, when the mill has four. The building-level fiche for the mill, id 93473, gives the sequence cleanly: « Sa restauration est amorcée en 1954. Le toit conique est rétabli en 1962 et les ailes sont ajoutées en 1967. » Four sails, on a propeller at the centre of a gable at the base of the roof. This page follows the building fiche. The same pair of fiches also disagrees about the mill's date: the site fiche and by-law PC-2808 both say 1709-1710, the building fiche says construit en 1709 three times. Both are published here; neither is corrected.

The Code villageois cannot decide what year the village burned — but another document can. Chapter 3 of the Code says the traditional Québec architecture recalls the traces of the village's establishment « jusqu'à l'incendie de 1901 », and then, four lines later on the same page, « Après l'incendie de 1900, deux styles principaux créent une certaine homogénéité ». The description of unité paysagère UP3 says that unit was « Épargnée par la déflagration de 1900 ». Two occurrences of 1900 against one of 1901, in one by-law, about one fire. The Ville de Montréal's 2005 cahier settles it independently and adds a detail: « En mai 1900, un feu prenant son origine dans une boutique de ferblanterie détruit une partie du noyau villageois. Malgré tout, Pointe-Claire se reconstruit en conservant la même trame de rues. » May 1900, from a tinsmith's shop, and the street plan kept. This site prints 1900 and records the Code's 1901 as an error in it.

Three documents, three geometries, and a fourth layer that has no map at all. This page carries thirty-three sector rows because Pointe-Claire has been surveyed three times by three bodies for three purposes, and none of the three covers the same ground. The 2013 citation covers 2.7 hectares and is the only one with statutory force under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel. The Code villageois of 2024–2025 covers the village sector in ten landscape units and is a zoning instrument, not a heritage one. The Ville de Montréal's 2005 Évaluation du patrimoine urbain covers the whole municipal territory in twenty-two sectors and lost whatever force it had when Pointe-Claire demerged in 2006 — and it is the only document that describes Cedar Park, the Bowling Green, the Beaconsfield golf-club houses, the Valois bay resorts or the postwar quarters at all. On top of those three sits chapter 13 of the PIIA by-law, which protects roughly three hundred buildings one address at a time, with no sector boundary anywhere in it.

The 2005 cahier gives the plainest statement in this whole part of how a place gets its looks. Of the houses on the avenue du Golf it says: most were built by the founding members of the Beaconsfield Golf Club, « Leur design devait être approuvé par un comité formé par le Club et leur coût devait être supérieur à un minimum prédéterminé. Ces restrictions ont contribué à créer un ensemble harmonieux de paysage naturel et bâti. » A design committee and a minimum spend, run by a private club in 1904, produced the coherence that a heritage instrument would later be asked to protect. The Bowling Green next door is the same story in another key: laid out in 1905 by Frederick G. Todd, at the start of his career and trained by the Olmsted firm, for the Canadian Nursery Company, which subdivided its own nursery ground when the nursery stopped paying.

Three things the two Pointe-Claire sources disagree about, and one the brief gets wrong. The mill's date: the RPCQ site fiche and by-law PC-2808 say 1709-1710, the RPCQ building fiche says 1709 three times, the 2005 cahier says 1710 in its sector text and 1709-1710 in its overview. The church's dates: 1882-1885 on the RPCQ fiche and in the by-law, 1881-1885 in the cahier, which also calls the present church a replica of a first Bourgeau church begun in 1868 and burnt in 1881 — where the RPCQ dates the Fabrique's decision to 1867 and says only that the unfinished church burned in 1881. And the mill's ownership, which is where the brief for this part is wrong: it describes the mill as owned by the Sulpicians. The RPCQ says the Sulpicians built it in 1709 and leased it to millers, and says nothing about who owns it now; the 2005 cahier says the Congrégation de Notre-Dame acquired it — in 1866 according to its sector text, in 1845 according to its own historique twenty pages earlier. Three published dates for one transfer of ownership, and no current owner asserted anywhere on this page. The cahier also calls the mill « classé bien archéologique en 1983 »; the RPCQ statut block says Classement, Immeuble patrimonial, 21 March 1983, which is a different category, and the RPCQ is followed here.

A form-based code is not a heritage instrument, and its style rows are prescriptions. The ten unités paysagères below come from by-law PC-2880, whose purpose is to determine in advance « le style et les caractéristiques du développement immobilier ». Its tables therefore say what belongs in each unit as much as what stands there, and the two can differ visibly: unit UP7 is described as being made of one-storey bungalows and its table prescribes contemporary blocks of three to six storeys. Read the description and the table together, and the direction of travel is legible in a way that a conventional zoning by-law would hide. The building-by-building heritage protection in the village comes from somewhere else — chapter 13 of the PIIA by-law PC-2787, which lists roughly three hundred addresses and applies to them wherever they are.

What Pointe-Claire contributes to the shared West Island type records. Four of the five shared records touch this place, and the evidence for each is different in kind. The maison de tradition québécoise is chapter 3 of the Code villageois, verbatim: a one-storey carré under a pitched roof, present in the oldest sectors, "rappelle les traces de l'établissement du village". The maison de pierre du Régime français is a single classed building, the maison Hyacinthe-Jamme-Dit-Carrière of 1769–1791, whose RPCQ fiche gives a full five-column description. The bungalow d'après-guerre is named in the Code's description of unit UP7. And the villa anglaise de villégiature is unit UP9, the avenue du Golf nord, which Part 11 did not list Pointe-Claire under and which the source supports better than almost anything else in this part: the Code attributes the sector explicitly to the British Arts and Crafts movement reaching Québec at the start of the 20th century "dans des déclinaisons issues des adaptations stylistiques américaines ayant donné naissance aux maisons de style Shingle et Craftsman", on lots the table calls "très larges et profonds" at a coverage of about 20 per cent behind a "très profonde" front margin. Its two own type records — the Boomtown and the Cubique — are the two styles chapter 3 describes that no shared record covers.

What is not claimed here. No date, architect or description is asserted for any of the roughly three hundred addresses on the PIIA heritage list beyond the names the list itself prints; it gives addresses and names only. The individual RPCQ records for the church, the convent, the presbytery, the school and the mission cross were not retrieved, so everything said about them comes from the site fiche and from by-law PC-2808. And no free photograph identified as a Pointe-Claire Cubique was found, so that card carries a placeholder rather than a lookalike from somewhere else.
