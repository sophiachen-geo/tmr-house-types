Residential Architectural Styles of Lachine (borough of Montréal)

Lachine is a borough of Montréal, and that is the first thing to say about it. Every other place in this section of the site — Pointe-Claire, Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue, Dorval, Senneville, Baie-D'Urfé — was merged into Montréal in 2002 and reconstituted as a city or a village on 1 January 2006 after the referendums of 20 June 2004. Lachine was not. It went into the merger and stayed, and it is governed today by a borough council under the Ville de Montréal, with a borough zoning by-law and a borough PIIA, while its neighbours along the same lakeshore run their own. The consequence for this site is direct: Lachine's heritage sectors come from the Ville de Montréal's own borough-by- borough Évaluation du patrimoine urbain series, the same instrument that governs Verdun, the Sud-Ouest and Rosemont, and not from a municipal inventory of its own.

Geography made the place before politics did. The rapides de Lachine stop everything coming up the St Lawrence, and the canoe routes from the pays d'en Haut converge just above them; the ground here is where cargo had to come out of the water. In 1667 the Sulpicians, seigneurs of the island since 1663, conceded an arrière-fief called the côte Saint-Sulpice to René-Robert Cavelier de La Salle, who sold part of it in 1669 to the merchants Charles Le Moyne and Jacques Le Ber. Between 1669 and 1671 they built a stone house there to serve as a trading post, a warehouse for outfitting the coureurs des bois, and a dwelling. It is still standing, it is classed, and it is the oldest building on this page by a century and a half.

What came after arrived in two waves that never really mixed. The canal, opened in 1825 and enlarged twice, and the railway that reached the Lachine quay in 1847, made the east end an industrial town: Dominion Bridge from 1883, the Dawes brewery from 1811, and behind them a dense grid of village houses, one-storey brick houses and brick duplexes and triplexes. The west end went the other way. The 1855 railway brought wealthy anglophone families out to the lake, Summerlea was created in 1895 and annexed in 1912, and the ground west of the 32nd Avenue filled with Shingle Style and Arts and Crafts summer houses on very large, very deep, well-planted lots. The cahier's own summary of the split is blunt: the east is dense, mixed and short of green space, "surtout du logement ouvrier"; the west is strictly residential, "moins dense, beaucoup plus verdoyante et habitée par une population économiquement plus à l'aise qui en fait très tôt un lieu de villégiature".

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

19.E.2 Boulevard Saint-Joseph exceptional Both sides of the old shore road, and the cahier's judgement is that the buildings, the winding line of the road and the natural attributes of the site cannot be separated from one another. It is a tracé fondateur — the chemin du Coteau Saint-Pierre, then Upper Lachine Road, apparently dating from the end of the 17th century — and following it reads off the whole development of the territory: a variety of buildings representing a great number of architectural currents in vogue from the 17th to the 20th century, industrial, residential, commercial and institutional at once. The siting of the buildings follows the topography of the thin riverside band, and the road's curves open framed views of the canal and of Lac Saint-Louis. 19.E.3 Noyau institutionnel et public exceptional The block of religious and institutional buildings between the 12th and 15th Avenues, which the cahier says contrasts with the village of Lachine by its monumentality and is the only green enclave in that part of the borough. It holds the couvent des Sœurs de Sainte-Anne, begun in 1862 and enlarged — the sanctuary among other parts — to designs by Maurice Perreault and Albert Mesnard in 1888–1890; the église des Saints-Anges-Gardiens, by Alphonse Venne and Dalbé Viau in 1919; the église Saint-Andrew by John Wells in 1832 and its presbytery; and St. Stephen's Anglican Church of 1831. 19.E.5 Sainte-Anne exceptional The conventual ensemble of the Sœurs de Sainte-Anne with its gardens and cemetery, impressive for its monumentality, its spatial hold on the territory and the quality of its architecture, and remarkable — the cahier's word — for still serving its original purpose as the community's mother house. 19.I.1 Vieux-Lachine interesting The oldest part grows first along the boulevard Saint-Joseph, then reaches rue Saint-Louis and rue Victoria as Lachine's industrial vocation is consolidated by the arrival of the Dominion Bridge. An orthogonal grid of regular rectangular blocks without back lanes carries buildings sited variously — sometimes on the street line, sometimes set back far enough for an exterior stair or a short path to the front door. This is the sector whose description doubles as a typology: village houses in wood; attached or terraced houses with mansards or false mansards, flat roofs or two-slope roofs; small one-storey brick single-family houses, wider than deep, with crowns; and detached or continuously aligned duplexes and triplexes, most often in brick, sometimes with carriage gates. Rue Notre-Dame is the commercial spine, and the market zone by the railway at the 18th Avenue is called out as worth attention. The cahier does not flatter it: parking lots, vacant lots, and shopfronts stripped of their ornament and crowns.
« On y retrouve un très large éventail d'usages et de typologies : maisons villageoises en bois et maisons mitoyennes ou en série avec mansarde ou fausse mansarde, toit plat ou toit à deux versants; petites maisons unifamiliales d'un étage en brique, plus larges que profondes et avec des couronnements; duplex et triplex détachés ou en alignement continu, le plus souvent en brique, avec parfois des portes cochères. »

19.I.2 Dawes interesting The industrial core of the Dawes brewery complex between the 18th and 34th Avenues, one of the oldest in Lachine, sitting between the junction points of the successive railway alignments — the Montreal and Lachine Railroad was built in 1847 — with the first and second entrances to the canal, a geometry still legible in the triangular block crossed by the 19th and 20th Avenues. Brewery buildings, warehouses, the employees' houses, commercial buildings and sheds; very narrow streets and blocks without lanes. 19.I.3 De la 32e à la 43e Avenue interesting Large wide blocks without lanes, a looser grain than the east end, deep front setbacks, and very wide streets whose pavements are separated from the roadway by rows of lime or apple trees, which the cahier says gives the whole a great homogeneity. A wide variety of architectural styles typical of the first half of the 20th century: large summer-resort houses of Arts and Crafts and Shingle Style inspiration, brick cottages and detached brick town houses built singly or in series, with bungalows and recent houses inserted among them. 19.I.5 Summerlea – 51e Avenue interesting Traces of Summerlea's gradual transformation, read through a relatively recent fabric: summer houses, former chalets, and town houses old and new interleaved in a very heterogeneous streetscape. The riverside band of the boulevard Saint-Joseph west of the 44th Avenue to the borough limit holds fine bourgeois residences on large lots and 18th-century houses such as the maison Quesnel and the maison Picard. The 19th-century layout survives in the sector's H-shaped back lanes. Called out particularly: the fine flat-roofed brick houses built in series on rue Saint-Antoine, and the stone houses with false mansards, also built in series, on the 18th Avenue; also fine semi-detached brick houses of Victorian inspiration and runs of dwellings of more or less complex massing. 19.I.7 McLaughlin interesting A small piece of ground at the junction of Lac Saint-Louis and the canal, plus the fairly homogeneous residential sector of the chemin de LaSalle and rue McLaughlin next to the Musée de Lachine. The cahier reads a village atmosphere here, produced mainly by low density and regular siting; back gardens give directly onto the lake and the spacing between buildings opens fine views. The architecture is of small gabarit and modest, but there are also more opulent houses of Victorian inspiration. The former presbytery of the now-vanished St. Stephen's church at 31-35 chemin de LaSalle — fieldstone, of refined composition, well preserved — is noted as a reminder that many Irish worked on the canal in the mid-19th century. chemin de LaSalle, rue McLaughlin

1663–1760 · Seigneurial côtes and the fur-trade posts

1663 – 1760

The rapids are the reason. The navigable routes from the west converge at the head of a five-kilometre barrier, and traffic between Montréal and the interior has to stop and portage. Traces of Indigenous occupation going back to the early Middle Woodland have been found on the point. In 1667 La Salle is conceded the arrière-fief of the côte Saint-Sulpice by the Sulpicians, seigneurs of the island since 1663; in 1669 he gives the fief back to its first owners and sells a share to Charles Le Moyne (1626–1685) and Jacques Le Ber (vers 1633–1706), fur-trade partners who see a hinge of the trade in it. Between 1669 and 1671 they build in stone: a cellar, an attic, two rooms and a chimney. The post is a secondary one and lasts about fifteen years; in 1687 it is sold to François Guillemot dit Lalande. On the morning of 5 August 1689 the colony at Lachine is attacked by the Iroquois, settlers are killed and many buildings destroyed — the Lachine massacre — and the fur trade and settlement both stall until the Grande Paix of 1701. Historians long assumed the Le Ber-Le Moyne house burned that morning; the excavations begun in 1998 have found no trace of fire. The site is abandoned for a few years and then laid out as a farm by Marguerite Chorel (vers 1670–1736) and her husband Guillaume de Lorimier des Bordes (1657–1709), who settle there in 1695 and whose descendants stay until 1765. Registers for the parish of Les Saints-Anges-de-Lachine open in 1676, over a territory then running from the côte des Argoulets, now Verdun, all the way to the pointe Claire.

Stone house of the French tradition La maison de pierre de tradition française

Style: French regime
source ›
Phase attribution: provisional

A low fieldstone box of one and a half storeys under a steep two-slope roof, stacks rising inside the gable walls, openings placed where they were wanted rather than where symmetry asked for them. Three of them survive across three of the six West Island places — a trading post of 1669–71 at Lachine, a farm house of about 1700 at Baie-D'Urfé, and one of 1769–91 at Pointe-Claire — and all three are protected individually while nothing around them is. One record, three municipalities: the photograph on this card is Lachine's, and Baie-D'Urfé's and Pointe-Claire's stand beside it on the type page.

Where it comes from

The Sulpicians became seigneurs of the island of Montréal in 1663 and conceded the côtes that run out to its western end; the houses built on those concessions were stone because stone was what the fields gave up and because fire and raiding made anything else a poor bet. What survives is very little. Lachine's is the oldest and least altered: two rectangular stone buildings put up in 1669–71 as a fur-trade post for Charles Le Moyne and Jacques Le Ber, restored in the 1980s toward their seventeenth-century appearance and now the Musée de Lachine. Baie-D'Urfé's was built about 1700 by Hubert Rangé dit Laviolette on a concession he bought in 1695, and then quietly rebuilt by the Pilon family between 1839 and 1874 — the walls raised, the roof pitch eased off, moulded chambranles and projecting eaves added in what RPCQ calls a taste imported after the Conquest. Pointe-Claire's went up between 1769 and 1791 on a censive conceded in 1722, and was altered again in 1828. Read together the three make the point the West Island pages keep making: the form is one form across three municipalities, the protection is three separate individual instruments granted in 1964, 2001 and 2001, and not one of them protects an ensemble.

Siting & landscape
  • Each of the three survivors sits on ground that predates the street grid around it. Le Ber-Le Moyne stands on a raised point of land beside lac Saint-Louis at the head of the Lachine rapids, on a slight mound planted with mature trees.
  • The Baie-D'Urfé house stands at the back of its lot, about a hundred metres from the public road and close to the shore, its main front turned south to the lake and the îles Sainte-Geneviève — RPCQ reads that orientation as evidence that the chemin du Roy once ran between the house and the water.
  • Pointe-Claire's house stands at the far end of a large treed lot that now isolates it from the postwar subdivision around it, on a censive conceded by the Sulpicians in 1722 along the côte Saint-Jean.
Massing
  • A low stone carré on a near-square or short rectangular plan, one and a half storeys, sitting close to the ground. The Baie-D'Urfé carré measures 9.4 by 9.3 m.
  • Steep two-slope roof with straight slopes. Eaves are shallow at Lachine, and carried out over a covered gallery at Baie-D'Urfé and Pointe-Claire — in both cases a nineteenth-century alteration, not an original feature.

1760–1855 · Villages, parishes and the canals

1760 – 1855

Most of the population lives on land conceded by the Sulpicians and stays agricultural through the 18th century, and the number of inhabitants barely moves. Two things change that. In 1811 the Dawes brewery is established on the shore of Lac Saint-Louis and becomes the third largest on the island; workers and shops gather round it, more than half of Lachine's working population concentrates there, and the farms around convert to barley and hops for it. And in 1825 the canal is dug. The idea is old — Dollier de Casson pushed the Sulpicians to attempt it in the 17th century — but it takes Canadian government money in the early 19th to carry it through. A new urban and economic pole grows at the canal mouth: innkeepers, hoteliers, artisans and small traders living off the traffic. A large enough Anglo-Protestant population supports two churches of different denominations in the 1830s. In 1845 the parish municipality is created and in 1848 the village municipality. In 1847 the first railway on the island, linking Montréal to the Lachine quay, sharpens the trade again. In 1861–1865 the église des Saints-Anges is built on its present site next to the two Protestant churches, and the institutional core takes shape.

1855–1930 · The railway, the resorts and the country estates

1855 – 1930

In 1872 Lachine becomes a ville with water, sewers and street lighting, well ahead of the other lake municipalities. At the end of the century the big firms arrive at the canal: Dominion Bridge, a maker of steel structures, settles near the locks in 1883 and goes on to build bridges and buildings across Québec and Canada; one of its first contracts is the Canadian Pacific railway bridge nearby. Workers arrive in numbers and settle in the east end, which densifies and spills across the railway; the firms also employ skilled tradesmen and professionals, who go to the west end instead. In 1912 Lachine loses its eastern territory, which becomes the municipality of LaSalle, and gains Summerlea in the far west, created in 1895 — a place of summer houses, chalets and large bourgeois residences near a golf course. Ville Saint-Pierre, created as the village of Saint-Pierre-aux-Liens in 1894 and a ville in 1908, is meanwhile drawing industry of its own with tax exemptions and cheap land: Canadian Car and Foundry in 1905, Consumers Glass in 1917.

1930–2002 · Suburbanisation and the merger

1930 – 2002

The municipal policy of attracting industry keeps the population climbing until the 1930 crisis levels it off. Then, like other towns, Lachine answers the postwar boom by turning its unbuilt sectors into dormitory quarters. The mass urbanisation of the 1950s to 1970s fills the north and west of the territory and works its way into the older fabric as well, and the forms it produces — the cahier says so plainly — are the ones that testify to the emergence of a middle class, individual ownership and a way of living organised around the car. By the 1970s the population is over 50,000. That is also when the canal, after more than a century of intense traffic, closes to through navigation; it passes to Parks Canada in 1978. In 2000 Ville Saint-Pierre is annexed to Lachine, and in 2002 the merged municipality becomes a borough of the Ville de Montréal.

Where it comes from

The wave arrives at different moments and for different reasons. At Dorval it is the airport: the aerodrome opened in 1941, air traffic grew fast after the war, the rest of the territory was opened to subdivision and the population went from under 3,000 in the 1940s to more than 20,000 by 1971. At Pointe-Claire it is a straight housing shortage — a veterans' sector on the Wartime Housing Limited model about 1947, then the Magil project of 1953–55. At Lachine it is what the cahier calls « l'urbanisation massive des années 1950-1970 », running east across the last open ground and producing a catalogue of contractors' types so consistent that the dominant one is still known locally by its builder's name. At Baie-D'Urfé it is the Métropolitaine and the demographic boom together, pushing settlement north across the railway for the first time. What the 2005 cahiers do with all of it is the interesting part. The series invented a category, Ensembles urbains d'intérêt, to hold postwar suburbs, said openly that the exercise was exploratory, and then valued these places for their street layouts, their planting and the consistency of their materials rather than for their architecture — while recording, in the same paragraphs, that almost every house had already been raised, extended, re-clad or re-roofed.   • « Dans les années 1950-1960, grâce à la construction de l'autoroute Métropolitaine et au boom démographique de l'après-guerre, le développement de cette banlieue essentiellement résidentielle s'accélère et l'urbanisation gagne alors le nord de la voie ferrée. Même si le caractère rural disparaît, les rues bordées d'arbres maintiennent une apparence champêtre. » (§ 2, Historique)
Siting & landscape
  • Detached on its own lot, set back from the street with side margins, and with parking in the front yard — the Lachine cahier's description of the same wave, and the one place any West Island source describes the siting rule rather than the building.
  • Cul-de-sac and crescent street patterns recur, sometimes with a planted median down a wide avenue, and Dorval's cahier ties the effect of every one of these sectors to mature trees on the properties rather than to the houses.
  • Built in small series from a short catalogue of models. Dorval's 7.U.5 is praised for using « un nombre relativement restreint de modèles » while letting the materials vary house to house; Lachine's 19.U.2 calls the catalogue of types on its territory « particulièrement impressionnant ». In Lachine the dominant form of that catalogue is not this one but the taller contractor's box known locally as the maison « Gameroff », which carries its own record on that page; what belongs here is the alignment of Californian-inspired bungalows the cahier singles out at the end of the 51e Avenue.
Massing
  • One storey, plain-pied. The Dorval cahier repeats the phrase « bungalows de type plain-pied, toit pavillon, brique » for two separate sectors, 7.U.1 and 7.U.4, and treats it as the norm for the municipality from the 1940s–50s onward.
  • A hipped roof is the commonest form. Lachine records more variety in the same years — pitched, hipped, or hipped combined with a false mansard.
  • Split-level versions appear in the same wave and the same decade; Dorval's 7.U.5 dates a whole quadrilateral of them to the 1950s.
Articulation
  • Almost none, and what there is varies within a single street rather than between streets. Dorval's Model Homes are described as houses that look alike until you look closely, at which point the window types, the roofs, the use of artificial stone and clapboard and the handling of the brick are all different.
Openings
  • Not described by any West Island source beyond the note that window types differ between otherwise identical Dorval houses.
Materials
  • Brick dominant, with artificial stone and clapboard used as accent — the three materials Dorval's cahier names on avenue Dahlia.
  • Red brick at Pointe-Claire's Magil project, built 1953–55.

Better evidenced than the other four shared records, and still not evidenced everywhere. The 2005 Ville de Montréal series created a category — Ensembles urbains d'intérêt — expressly to catch postwar suburban housing, and Dorval's cahier alone gives it eight sectors with real building description, so the columns here are unusually well supported for a place with no typology document. But the support is not even across the six places, and that is the thing to know before trusting this record. Dorval and Pointe-Claire are described in detail. Lachine is well described too, but most of what its sector 19.U.2 describes is the one- or two-storey contractor's box that Lachine's own page records as the maison « Gameroff »; only the Californian bungalow alignment at the 51e Avenue is unambiguously this type, and that is what is claimed here. Senneville is attested by one clause in sector 2.E.4, which records postwar houses inside the estate belt and says they continue its tradition of setting buildings into the landscape — no bungalow sector of its own is named, and the well described bungalow sector in the same cahier, 2.U.2 Belvédère, is in the Pierrefonds half of that arrondissement and not in Senneville. Baie-D'Urfé is attested only by its cahier's historique, which dates the acceleration of its suburban growth to the 1950s–60s and to the Métropolitaine, and names no postwar sector; the postwar ensembles in that cahier, 4.U.1 Devon-York with its « bâtiments de l'après-guerre … rappelant le "ranch" californien », 4.U.2 Sunny Acres and 4.U.3 Lakeview-Morgan, are introduced under a paragraph about the territory of Beaconsfield. Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue is the one membership no retrieved source attests at all: its cahier, once found, names no postwar sector and its single ensemble urbain d'intérêt, 1.U.1, is the Garden City Press ensemble of J.-J. Harpell, which is not this type. It is kept in `places` because the record is the shared West Island postwar record and because a later source may supply what this one does not, and it is flagged here rather than asserted quietly. Fields left null and why: `roofing` — no source names a roof covering anywhere in the six places; `roof.pitch_deg` — « toit pavillon » with no figure; `lot_width_m`, `setback_front_m`, `setback_side_m`, `front_yard_green_pct` — the cahiers describe setbacks, front-yard parking and landscaping at length and measure none of it; `sectors` — a shared record cannot carry sector codes that exist in only one of its places, and here they would run 7.U.x, 5.U.x, 19.U.x and 2.U.x at once. `window_proportion: horizontal` is the weakest entry in the record: it is read from the form and from the Dorval note that window types vary between otherwise identical houses, not from any stated proportion, and it should be the first field to correct. `styles: [minimal-traditional]` is a classification made here, not a style any West Island source names; the nearest a source comes is Dorval's « banlieue de type californien » and Lachine's « bungalows d'inspiration californienne ».

Conservation guidance (fiche)
  • This is the only wave in the West Island whose recognition is explicitly experimental. The 2005 series says its own identification of these sectors « est exploratoire » and aims to link them to conservation concerns « pour une première fois ».
  • Where the cahiers return to it, they say the same thing twice - the value lies in the plan, the planting and the consistency of the materials rather than in any individual house, and personalisation is what erodes it. Lachine's 19.U.2 records that practically every building in its sector has been modified, and lists how: raising, building to the lot line, added upper storeys or a garage, changed cladding, changed openings, changed roof form.
  • Pointe-Claire's veterans' sector is the sharpest case: the cahier says few of the wartime houses are left and that what still marks the sector is the street layout and the subdivision, not the buildings.

The maison « Gameroff » — contractor-built house of the 1950s–1970s quarters La maison « Gameroff »

19.U.2

A detached one- or two-storey house on a square or rectangular plan, simple in massing, clad in brick with wooden trim, under a roof that might be pitched, hipped, or hipped with a false mansard; set back from the street with side yards and a car parked in front, and built in small series. What makes it worth a card is its name. The cahier records that among the contractors who built the 1950s–1970s quarters of Lachine, the best known locally gave the dominant house type its familiar name — the maison « Gameroff » — and that is a piece of purely local vernacular naming that made it into a municipal heritage document.

Where it comes from

Lachine answered the postwar boom the way other towns did, by turning its unbuilt sectors into dormitory quarters, and the sector called Victoria – Sir-George-Simpson is what came out: a vast, very homogeneous stretch of detached houses that the cahier says testify to the emergence of a middle class, to individual ownership, and to a way of living made possible by the car. It also says the catalogue of types deployed there is "particulièrement impressionnant" and lists three at once — Boomtown houses, bungalows, and the contractors' houses of which the Gameroff is the dominant one. That is the note this record should end on: a document written to identify heritage found a 1950s–1970s tract worth naming as an ensemble, and named its houses after the man who built them.

Everything above is transcribed from one sector description. The cahier gives the plan, the massing, the storeys, the siting, the cladding, the ornament and the roof-form range, and gives nothing else: no dimensions, no window proportion, no roof pitch, no garage rule, no dates for individual houses beyond the 1950–1970 range for the wave that produced them. It also gives nothing at all about the contractor — no first name, no firm, no number of houses built, no years — so none of that is asserted here. The type carries no style attribution because the cahier makes none and because the form is defined by a builder rather than by an idiom.

2002–2026 · A borough, not a city — and the canal reopens

2002 – 2026

Lachine does not demerge. The referendums of 20 June 2004 and Bill 9 give six of the seven places in this part of the site their independence back on 1 January 2006; Lachine remains an arrondissement. The same year the merger took effect, two other things happen on its ground: the canal reopens to pleasure craft — the dignitaries gathered at lock no. 5 on 17 May 2002 — and the Le Ber-Le Moyne site, classed by Québec in 2001, is designated a national historic site by the Historic Sites and Monuments Board of Canada on 1 November 2002. A transfer of responsibility registered 21 September 2017 and effective 21 September 2018 moves authorisation of work inside the classed site from the ministère to the Ville de Montréal. In 2025 the borough council takes up a wholesale replacement of its urbanism by-laws, extending the PIIA to the entire territory, and Parks Canada marks the canal's bicentenary.

Notes on the approach

Lachine never demerged, and this page says so twice on purpose. The other six places of Part 11 are either a central borough (Ville-Marie) or reconstituted municipalities. Lachine is the only West Island core that went into the 2002 merger and stayed in it. It matters for how the page is built: Lachine has no municipal heritage inventory, no citation by-law and no local site patrimonial cité. What it has instead is a chapter of the Ville de Montréal's twenty-seven-volume Évaluation du patrimoine urbain, and the borough-level zoning and PIIA by-laws that any Montréal borough has. Compare Pointe-Claire, five kilometres west, which used its restored municipal powers to cite a 2.7-hectare site in 2013.

Two instruments over one house, one year apart. The Le Ber-Le Moyne site carries a provincial classement as a site patrimonial dated 26 April 2001 and a federal designation as a lieu historique national du Canada dated 1 November 2002. They are not the same thing and they do not do the same work. The provincial classement is what makes work on the site subject to authorisation under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel — and since 21 September 2018, to authorisation by the Ville de Montréal rather than by the ministère, under a transfer of powers registered a year earlier. The federal designation is commemorative. The RPCQ fiche states the reach of the provincial protection exactly: « La protection s'applique à l'extérieur des bâtiments et au terrain. » Exterior and ground, not interiors. The excavated collection was classed separately, as an objet patrimonial, at the same time as the site.

What the sources will and will not say about the age of the Le Ber-Le Moyne house. The RPCQ says the two stone buildings are contemporary, that masonry analysis confirmed it, and that the house, "érigée à la fin du XVIIe siècle, […] est l'un des rares témoins de l'architecture rurale de cette époque". The 2005 cahier says it dates from the French regime and is "l'une des plus anciennes au Canada". Neither says it is the oldest complete building on the island of Montréal, and this site does not say so either. What both do say is that a major restoration between 1981 and 1985 returned the house to its 17th-century appearance and that the museum reopened in 1985 — so what stands is a restored 17th-century fabric, not an untouched one, and the 1768 rebuilding of the front façade by the innkeeper Hugh Heney is part of its history whether or not it is now visible.

Parks Canada contradicts itself about the canal, and the contradiction is worth printing. The Directory of Federal Heritage Designations record for the canal (id 627) gives the designation date 1929-05-17 and a construction period of 1821 to 1825. The commemorative plaque text carried on the same record says the canal « fut inauguré en 1824 » and « Il fut fermé en 1971, 12 ans après l'ouverture de la Voie maritime du Saint-Laurent ». The French statement of value on that record says the canal was built « à des fins commerciales en 1821-1825 »; the English version of the same paragraph says "built as a commercial canal in 1825-26". Parks Canada's current public pages, meanwhile, say the works began in 1821 and the canal was finished in 1825, that it closed in 1970, that it reopened to pleasure craft in 2002, and that 2025 is its bicentenary. This site follows the current pages for 1825, 1970 and 2002, records 1824 and 1971 as the plaque's readings, and notes that the 1907 Pinsoneault atlas and the 2005 cahier both take 1825 as the opening. The length is no better settled: the Directory record says 14 kilometres, the navigation page says 14 kilometres of navigable channel and five locks, the visitor page says 14,5 km between lock no. 1 and lock no. 5, and the multi-use trail is 13,5 km. The statutory record's figure — 14 km — is the one encoded on the instrument row.

Where the type content comes from, and why there are only four cards. The 2005 cahier characterises sectors, not building types. It has no per-type table, no fiches, and nothing like the Gatineau or Lévis catalogues. But its description of sector 19.I.1 contains, in one sentence, a four-item typology of the old town — village houses in wood; attached or terraced houses under mansards and false mansards, flat roofs or two-slope roofs; small one-storey brick single-family houses, wider than deep, with crowns; and detached or continuously aligned brick duplexes and triplexes, sometimes with carriage gates — and sector 19.U.2 names a postwar type after the contractor who built most of it. Those sentences, plus the paragraphs in 19.I.2, 19.I.6 and 19.I.8 that repeat and extend them, are the whole documentary basis of the four records below. Every dimension the cahier does not state — lot widths, setbacks, roof pitches in degrees, window proportions — is null. The village house in wood is not carded here: it is the shared West Island record maison de tradition québécoise, and the French-regime stone house is the shared record maison de pierre du Régime français, to which Lachine contributes the Le Ber-Le Moyne buildings and the 18th-century houses of the western shore.

The Vétérans ensemble is a sector here and not a type, deliberately. Sector 19.U.1 is described in four lines: a small ensemble of dwellings intended for veterans, very homogeneous, particularly well preserved, with a beginning tendency to densification and alteration. That is all. No storeys, no roof form, no cladding, no plan. Veterans' housing is a real type and it has a record elsewhere on this site; making one for Lachine out of this paragraph would mean inventing four of the five columns, so the ensemble is recorded as a sector and left there.

The maison « Gameroff » is the most local name on this site. Sector 19.U.2 says the catalogue of types deployed across the 1950s–1970s quarters is "particulièrement impressionnant" and that among the contractors who built them the best known locally "a laissé au type architectural dominant son appellation familière de maison « Gameroff »". The cahier does not give the contractor's first name, the firm, the number of houses or the years, and none of those is asserted here. What it does give is a description of the dominant form and one captioned example, "3, 40e avenue". The name is a piece of vernacular naming that survived into a municipal document, and it is worth a card for that alone.

The archaeological sites the cahier lists apart. Besides the three sectors of high archaeological potential encoded above, § 3.2.3 C lists two sites archéologiques that are not sectors: 19.A.1, the canal de Lachine, where remains associated with the upstream entrance walls of the 1825 lock may lie under the north pavement of the boulevard Saint-Joseph or behind the fill of the îlot Monk's north revetment wall; and 19.A.2, the maison mère des Sœurs de Sainte-Anne on the boulevard Saint-Joseph, the community's first mother house and first convent. They are recorded here rather than as rows, because the cahier does not treat them as sectors.
