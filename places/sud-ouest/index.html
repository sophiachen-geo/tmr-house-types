Residential Architectural Styles of Le Sud-Ouest (Montréal)

The Sud-Ouest is where the Montréal plex was made, and it is the one place on this site whose own planning documents explain how, in a sentence you can check. Everything else here follows from a canal. The first canal de Lachine opened in 1825 after four years' digging, was widened in the 1840s, and turned a stretch of Sulpician farmland into the industrial heart of the country: the Montreal & Lachine Railway in 1847, the Victoria Bridge in 1859, and then tanneries, rolling mills, sugar, textiles, malt, rope and shoes. The people who worked in them had to be housed on the spot, quickly, on narrow lots, and by developers subdividing their own land village by village — Griffintown, Sainte-Cunégonde, Saint-Augustin, Saint-Henri, village Turcot, Pointe-Saint-Charles, Victoriatown, Côte-Saint-Paul. Each was constituted as a municipality in its own right in the 1870s and annexed to Montréal between 1887 and 1910, and each carries the rules that were in force when its blocks were cut.

That is why the borough's housing is legible as a sequence rather than a style. Patri-Arch's Étude typomorphologique, made for the borough in 2005 and updated in 2013, reads the territory as five aires de paysage — La Petite-Bourgogne, Pointe-Saint-Charles, Côte-Saint-Paul, Saint-Henri, Griffintown — cut into sixty-five unités de paysage, and reads the buildings as nineteen architectural types on one template. Seven of the nineteen are plexes, and the study defines the whole family in a single line that the borough's design-review by-law repeats: « Ces immeubles possèdent de deux à six logements. » Three more are apartment buildings, and the line between the two families is drawn just as sharply — an immeuble d'appartements has « un hall d’entrée commun qui dessert de six à douze logements », and once you have a common hall you have left the plex behind.

The exterior stair, which is the thing everyone knows about Montréal, arrives in this document as a consequence rather than a trait. Montréal began regulating residential construction around 1880. The new rules produced a new kind of lotting — ruelles, first simple or in an « I », then in an « H », giving regular blocks with four built sides — and they required buildings to stand behind a front setback. The study draws the conclusion in one clause: « l’obligation de l’implantation avec une marge de recul avant amènera la translation de l’escalier d’accès aux logements des étages supérieurs vers l’extérieur, ce qui donnera la caractéristique si typique des bâtiments des quartiers montréalais de cette époque ». Put the stair outside and you keep the floor area inside and stop heating a shared hall. Lotting without a ruelle and building without a setback « ne sera abandonnée que vers la fin des années 1880 »; the triplex with exterior stair « fait son apparition principalement à partir de 1900 »; the duplex with exterior stair and the multiplex follow immediately. Which means this borough contains the before and the after side by side, and its type list — duplex with interior stair, triplex with interior stair, three-storey duplex, then duplex, triplex and multiplex with exterior stairs — is a record of one regulatory change working itself through a housing stock.

The one other thing that makes this record unusual is the shape of the fiche. Section C.4, Traitement de la façade, divides every façade into three horizontal parts and names them: le socle, the low part, generally the foundation but sometimes taking in the ground floor; le corps, the central part; le couronnement, the high part, which absorbs the roof whenever the roof is visible. No other source on this site does this. Twelve of the fifteen residential types are described in all three divisions, and reading down them is the fastest way to see what actually changes between the plexes: the socle goes from rusticated stone to exposed concrete, the corps from two asymmetrical bays to three symmetrical ones, and the couronnement from a moulded wood cornice on consoles to a strip of metal flashing.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

 Canal de Lachine exceptional The canal itself, its banks, its infrastructure and a heavy concentration of industrial, residential, public and commercial buildings of heritage interest — the sector that crosses the whole borough and several of the former municipalities annexed to Montréal. On the north bank the Griffintown portion, continuous with the faubourg des Récollets, was industrial from the start; the cahier describes an urban fabric dotted with vacant lots and buildings of 1880–1950 built to the street line, clay brick, vertical fenestration in regular bays, and a second generation of shed-like steel-frame volumes from the 1950s. Named at length: the Canada Malting with its terracotta silos, the Redpath Sugar Refinery, Belding Corticelli, the Brennan warehouse, the Nordelec, the New City Gas Company, the marché Atwater on its edge, and the declining wood-clad row of workers' houses at 4351-4363 rue Saint-Ambroise, a cited monument historique.
« Ce secteur comprend le canal lui-même, ses berges, ses infrastructures et une concentration importante d’immeubles industriels, résidentiels, publics et commerciaux d’intérêt patrimonial. »

22.AP.5 Saint-Henri-des-Tanneries et canal de Lachine archaeological-potential One of fourteen secteurs d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel in the cahier — an overlay, not a heritage grade, and the reason the sector vocabulary on this site carries an AP value at all. Around 1770 a village specialising in leather work and known as Saint-Henri-des-Tanneries already stood here beside the little rivière Saint-Pierre. When the canal was begun in 1825 Saint-Henri counted 466 inhabitants, of whom 160 were leather workers, 250 farmers and 30 innkeepers. The second half of the 19th century brought the first industrial tanneries, shipyards, textiles and metals. The cahier's archaeological text runs through the Canada Malting and Montreal Fire Bricks, the Merchants Manufacturing, the Globe Hat Works and the Moseley tannery — the last established on land Edward Moseley bought in 1858, in operation from 1859, a complex of about a dozen buildings, whose rebuilt red-brick main building still stands at the south-west corner of rues Saint-Ambroise and Turgeon.
« Vers 1770, un village spécialisé dans le travail du cuir et connu sous l’appellation de Saint-Henri-des-Tanneries s’élève déjà dans ce secteur, à proximité de la petite rivière Saint-Pierre. Au moment de la construction du canal de Lachine, en 1825, Saint-Henri compte 466 habitants incluant 160 artisans du cuir, 250 agriculteurs et 30 hôteliers et aubergistes. »

avant 1825 · Période agricole: farms, côtes and the portage roads

1760 – 1825

Before the canal, farmland. The territory was first cut in 1663, when Dollier de Casson conceded the seigneurie of the island of Montréal to the Sulpicians, who divided it progressively into côtes whose lots were then granted for settlement. The earliest côtes run perpendicular to the St Lawrence; later ones follow topography and water instead, which is why the côte Saint-Paul, tracing the old shores of Lac Saint-Louis, sits at an angle to the côte des Argoulets, and why the street grid still changes direction where they meet. The roads of this phase are the roads that structured everything after: the Upper Lachine Road (rue Notre-Dame Ouest, continuing as rue Saint-Jacques west of rue du Couvent), the Lower Lachine Road (rue Wellington), the Middle Lachine Road (rue Saint-Patrick), and the links between them — the chemin de la Côte-Saint-Paul and the avenue de l'Église. One building survives from it, and it is one of the oldest in the city: the maison Saint-Gabriel, built in 1698 on the foundations of an older building that had housed the Filles du roi, classed a monument historique and now a museum. There were tanneries at Saint-Henri-des-Tanneries by about 1770, which is why the archaeological overlay 22.AP.5 exists.

The oldest housing form still standing in the borough, and the rarest: thirty-eight of them, which the study says is what gives the type its value. One and a half storeys, a two-slope roof with two dormers, almost no front setback unless a galerie fills it, and a side passage to the back. They were built singly, in wood, in the first villages — Saint-Henri, Turcot, Saint-Augustin, Pointe-Saint-Charles — and burned or were pulled down for denser brick. Only the village Turcot, too far from the mills to be worth redeveloping, still has them as its principal type.

Where it comes from

The same small vernacular house that this site catalogues at Charlesbourg, on the Île d'Orléans and in the faubourgs of Québec City, arriving here as the pre-industrial layer under everything else. Note the name: the 2013 study calls it maison villageoise, but the PIIA by-law's own summary of the seven typologies calls the same thing maison « de faubourg ». Two arms of the same borough, two names for one form.

Description (source)

1825–1875 · Période d'industrialisation: the canal de Lachine and the first villages

1825 – 1875

The canal opens in 1825 and the faubourgs begin. The widening of the 1840s is the real trigger, because it made the canal's hydraulic potential usable by industry; the Montreal & Lachine Railway followed in 1847 and the Victoria Bridge in 1859. Residential lotting is done village by village and mostly by individual promoters subdividing their own land. Griffintown is first, on the fief Nazareth: leased by the Sœurs hospitalières to Thomas McCord in 1792 on a ninety-nine-year emphyteutic lease, sold on by his associates to Robert and Mary Griffin in 1796 without his agreement, subdivided, and left largely unbuilt until the canal was dug. Sainte-Cunégonde follows the building of Benjamin Brewster's basin in 1840 and the mills that came with it. Saint-Augustin is subdivided out of Arthur Webster's farm in 1855. The housing of this phase is the housing of a city with no setback rule: the maison villageoise where the old villages survived, and, where density was wanted, the duplex and triplex with the stair inside and the three-storey duplex under a false mansard.

1875–1945 · Période de création des infrastructures municipales: standardised housing, ruelles in « I » then « H »

1875 – 1945

The villages become municipalities and then parts of Montréal, and municipal power arrives with them: Saint-Gabriel constituted 1874 and annexed 1887; Sainte-Cunégonde a parish in 1875, a village in 1876, annexed 1906; Saint-Henri constituted 1875, annexed 1905; Côte-Saint-Paul constituted 1875, with Émard detaching in 1878 and constituted in 1902, both annexed 1910. What municipal power buys is public works, and the most visible of them are open spaces: parc Saint-Gabriel in 1874, place Saint-Henri about 1880, squares Alma and Saint-Patrick in 1880, square Iberville in 1886, square Saint-Henri in 1892, square Gallery in 1898. With them come the aqueduct, post offices, fire stations, town halls, public baths and markets. This is also the phase in which standardised housing is born, and in which the building rules that produce the plex come into force. By its end almost the whole borough is lotted and built, with free land left mainly in Côte-Saint-Paul and Ville-Émard.

  • Originally sash or casement with or without a transom; most of what survives is replacement, frequently sliders.
  • The dominant proportion is one width to half a height, and segmental-arched openings are frequent.
  • Doors plain, with or without a transom, the transom's height variable.
  • Les fenêtres d’origine sont généralement à guillotine ou à battants avec ou sans imposte. Cependant, les fenêtres que l’on retrouve aujourd’hui sont des modèles de remplacement. Par conséquent, on retrouve fréquemment des modèles de fenêtres plus récents telle la fenêtre coulissante. La proportion dominante des ouvertures équivaut à 1 largeur = ½ hauteur. Il est aussi fréquent que les ouvertures possèdent une forme en arc surbaissé.

  • Les linteaux des ouvertures sont composés d’un élément rectangulaire en pierre de taille ou d’un appareillage de briques en soldat. La pierre de taille est utilisée seulement pour les fenêtres rectangulaires. Dans le cas des bâtiments dont le corps de la façade est revêtu de pierres à bossages, l’encadrement des ouvertures est formé de pierres de taille. Dans ces cas, le linteau est parfois intégré à un bandeau qui couvre la largeur de la façade.
Materials

  • Brick dominant on the body of the façade, with some buildings in rusticated stone or wood clapboard.
  • Wood balconies with metal guards.

Two dwellings over three occupied levels under a false mansard — the type that answers the question of what Montréal built before the exterior stair, and the one the study reaches for when it explains why the fabric of the 1875–1945 period is not homogeneous. Built with no setback in the earlier phase and with one in the later, which is when the projecting window and the raised ground floor arrive.

Where it comes from

It is the plex family's tallest pre-1900 member and its closest relative to the Second Empire terraces of Ville-Marie and the Plateau. The study notes a rare third dwelling squeezed in — variante 2, a triplex with a false-mansard roof — which is how the boundary between duplex and triplex actually behaves on the ground.

What Patri-Arch says about this type ›
Description (source)
  • Le duplex de trois étages est un bâtiment qui possède deux logements de un ou deux étages chacun. Le toit en fausse mansarde est caractéristique de ce type de bâtiment. Les caractéristiques qui peuvent varier sont la présence ou l’absence d’une marge de recul avant, l’utilisation de la brique ou de la pierre comme matériau de revêtement principal et la présence d’une fenêtre en saillie.
Historical context (fiche)
  • Ce type de bâtiment a été construit un peu partout dans l’arrondissement du Sud-Ouest, sauf dans l’aire de paysage Côte-Saint-Paul. Les bâtiments de ce type, construits dans la période historique dite d’industrialisation (1825-1875), sont souvent plus sobres que ceux construits dans le premier tiers de la période dite de création des infrastructures municipales (1875-1945) qui possèdent plus fréquemment la pierre comme matériau de revêtement, des détails ornementaux plus complexes et une surélévation marquée du rez-de-chaussée par rapport au niveau du sol.
Siting & landscape
  • Built almost everywhere in the borough except the aire de paysage Côte-Saint-Paul.
  • Dominantly contiguous and with no front setback. Where there is one it exists to take an exterior stair up to the ground floor.
  • In alignment, unless built independently of its neighbours; the shallow setback means no landscaping is characteristic of the type.
  • L’implantation dominante est contiguë et sans marge de recul avant. Lorsqu’une marge de recul avant est présente, elle permet généralement l’insertion d’un escalier d’accès extérieur pour le rez-de-chaussée. Le duplex de trois étages est implanté en alignement sauf s’il est construit indépendamment des bâtiments voisins. En raison de la faible marge de recul avant, aucun aménagement paysager n’est caractéristique de ce type de bâtiment.
Massing
  • A rectangular body of three storeys, generally with a false mansard.
  • The ground floor is raised three risers, or up to eight where there is a front setback.
  • Balconies, where present, are on the first floor above the entrance doors.
  • Projecting windows on every level occur only on buildings with a setback.
  • Le duplex de trois étages possède un corps de bâti rectangulaire de 3 étages. On remarque généralement la présence d’une fausse mansarde. Le rez-de-chaussée est surélevé de 3 contremarches sauf s’il y a présence d’une marge de recul avant. Le rez-de-chaussée peut alors être surélevé jusqu’à 8 contremarches. Lorsque présents, les balcons sont situés au premier étage, au-dessus des portes d’entrée. Les fenêtres en saillie sur tous les niveaux sont présentes uniquement sur les bâtiments implantés avec une marge de recul avant.
Articulation
  • The dominant composition is asymmetrical, on two vertical bays: the first aligning the paired ground-floor doors with the windows above, sometimes with a first-floor balcony; the second windows only, and it is there that the projecting windows sit.
  • The socle is one to three courses of stone, and the top course is sometimes of a different texture so as to make a horizontal band between socle and corps; where the whole façade is stone, the texture or block size changes at the same line.
  • The crowning is a false mansard over a moulded cornice on modillions or consoles, its dormers varied in shape and more or less ornamented.
  • Buildings of the 1825–1875 period are soberer; those of the first third of 1875–1945 use stone more often, with more complex ornament and a marked raising of the ground floor.
  • Socle — Le socle est composé de un à trois rangs de pierres. Sur certains bâtiments, le rang de pierre supérieur peut être d’une texture différente que les rangs inférieurs de manière à créer un bandeau horizontal entre le socle et le corps du bâtiment. De même, lorsque toute la façade est en pierre, on remarque généralement un changement de texture et/ou de taille des blocs de pierre entre le socle et le corps de la façade.
  • Corps — Le mode de composition dominant pour le corps de la façade est asymétrique et formé de deux travées verticales. La première travée est formée d’un alignement des portes jumelées au rez-de-chaussée et des fenêtres aux étages. Il est possible d’y observer la présence d’un balcon au premier étage. La deuxième travée est formée uniquement d’un alignement vertical de fenêtres. C’est là que l’on retrouve les fenêtres en saillie lorsque le bâtiment est implanté avec une marge de recul avant.
  • Couronnement — Le couronnement est formé d’une fausse mansarde et d’une corniche moulurée à modillons ou à consoles sous la mansarde. Les lucarnes de la fausse mansarde sont de formes diversifiées et plus ou moins ornementées selon les cas.
Openings
  • Sash or casement with a transom, one width to half a height.
  • Doors generally plain or paired, with a transom.
  • Surrounds are a straight stone lintel or a brick flat-arch in a segmental curve; rusticated-stone buildings get a complete cut-stone surround, and on the more ornamented buildings the lintel is absorbed into a horizontal band.
  • Les ouvertures dominantes sont à guillotine ou à battants avec imposte. Leur proportion sont de 1 largeur = ½ hauteur.
  • Les portes sont généralement simples ou jumelées et possèdent une imposte.
  • Les encadrements des ouvertures sont composés d’un linteau droit en pierre ou d’une platebande en brique formant un arc surbaissé. Pour les bâtiments revêtus de pierres à bossages, on retrouve un encadrement complet en pierre de taille. Sur certains bâtiments plus ornementés, le linteau est intégré à un bandeau horizontal.
Materials
  • Generally rusticated stone at the socle, sometimes cut stone.
  • Brick dominant, with some three-storey duplexes clad in rusticated stone.
  • False mansards originally slate or sheet metal, often replaced by asphalt shingle.
  • Balconies and stairs generally wood with metal guards.
  • Le socle est généralement revêtu de pierres à bossages et parfois de pierres de taille
  • La brique est le matériau de revêtement dominant. On retrouve toutefois quelques duplex de trois étages revêtus de pierres à bossages. Les fausses mansardes étaient originellement revêtues d’ardoise ou de tôle mais ces matériaux ont souvent été remplacés par du bardeau d’asphalte.
  • Les balcons et escaliers sont généralement en bois avec des garde-corps en métal.

Ainsi, la composition de la façade est la même que celle décrite précédemment mais une travée verticale supplémentaire est ajoutée entre les deux bâtiments. Three dwellings, three storeys, flat roof, no setback and no stair in the yard — the densest thing the borough built before 1900, and a denser variant of the duplex with interior stair rather than an ancestor of the triplex with exterior stair. Most dwellings have their own stair, but it is frequent for the two upper dwellings to share one. Where the block has no ruelle, a porte cochère cuts through to the back yard.

Where it comes from

The interior-stair triplex is the form the exterior-stair triplex displaced. Both are carried on this site, and their canonical ids are siblings under plex-family precisely so that the swap can be read as one event rather than two unrelated types.

What Patri-Arch says about this type ›
Description (source)

Historical context (fiche)

Il est principalement présent dans les secteurs lotis à la fin de la période d’industrialisation (1825-1875) et au début de la période de création des infrastructures municipales (1875-1945). À cette époque, la construction avec marge de recul n’est pas encore généralisée et on retrouve un escalier intérieur pour accéder aux logements.
Siting & landscape

  • No landscaping at all.
  • Mainly in the sectors lotted at the end of the industrialisation period and the start of the infrastructure period, when building behind a setback was not yet general.
  • Frequently sited in a fabric with no ruelle, which is why the porte cochère appears (variante 3).

  • The ground floor is raised less than three risers.
  • Balconies possible at the upper levels; no projecting windows.

  • Les portes sont simples ou jumelées et possèdent une imposte.

1900–1945 · The exterior stair: the plex family arrives, from 1900

1900 – 1945

The exterior stair. This is not a heading in the study — it is the sub-period the study describes inside the third, and it is given a phase of its own here because half the borough's residential types belong to it and none of them exists before it. The regulatory sequence is the point. Setback requirements push the stair out of the building; the ruelle in an « H » gives the block four built sides and a service lane; the triplex with exterior stair appears from 1900 and becomes the base type of that fabric; the duplex with exterior stair follows in the parts of the borough that needed less density, and the multiplex — three storeys, two dwellings a floor, one shared access at the top — follows on wider lots from the First World War. Eleven of the sixty-five landscape units name the triplex with exterior stair as their principal type — behind only the two interior-stair plexes it displaced, at thirteen and twelve, which is the arithmetic of a form that arrived fifty years later and still caught them. The raised duplex, which arrives at the very end of the period with a garage under it, is the first sign of what comes next.

The archetypal Montréal house, and this is the document that says how it happened. It appears from 1900. Its parent is the block with an « H » ruelle and four built sides. Its defining feature is not the stair as an object but the stair as a consequence: the front-setback requirement gave the stair somewhere to go, and putting it there kept the interior floor area and saved heating a shared hall. Eleven of the borough's sixty-five landscape units name it as their principal type.

Where it comes from

The mechanism, verbatim from the study's synthèse: « l’obligation de l’implantation avec une marge de recul avant amènera la translation de l’escalier d’accès aux logements des étages supérieurs vers l’extérieur, ce qui donnera la caractéristique si typique des bâtiments des quartiers montréalais de cette époque ». The sequence is dated: lotting without a ruelle and building with no setback « ne sera abandonnée que vers la fin des années 1880 »; this type « fait son apparition principalement à partir de 1900 »; the duplex with exterior stair and the multiplex follow shortly after.

1945– · Période de rénovation et de revitalisation urbaine: expressways, expropriations, and the canal reopened

1945 – 2013

After 1945 the borough comes apart and is put back together. Trucking and the St Lawrence Seaway destroy the canal's reason to exist; navigation on it stops in 1970. Firms close or move, manufacturing declines, and the population falls from 94,833 in 1971 to 66,474 in 2001. The Ville intervenes on the fabric itself, and the interventions are what most of this phase's housing records. The Ville-Marie, Décarie and Bonaventure expressways take out residential blocks. Victoriatown, at the foot of the Victoria Bridge, is levelled outright for the Bonaventure. Griffintown is emptied by a wave of municipal expropriations and then cut off from downtown by the Bonaventure and the rail viaduct into Central Station. In Sainte-Cunégonde the work is done block by block, so the street grid survives but the two sides of a street can now carry different types — which is a fair description of what typological analysis is for. The community networks that formed to fight the renewal programmes turn into rehabilitation strategies, and the recognition of the canal de Lachine as a national historic site turns its banks into a recreational corridor; it reopened to pleasure craft in 2002, and the mills along it have been converted to housing. The types of this phase are the veterans' house from the 1940s, the town house from the 1960s and especially the 1980s, and the two apartment forms — the conciergerie and the lift-served immeuble d'appartements — which the study describes plainly as bâtiments de substitution.

Three or four storeys, one shared entrance, an irregular number of window bays and a flashing for a cornice. Almost all of them are replacements — the study calls them « bâtiments de substitution » standing where something older was pulled down — and their gauges vary enormously with the number of dwellings in each project. Only the group on boulevard des Trinitaires was planned for.

Where it comes from

The second of the study's three apartment types, and the one that carries the postwar history of the borough on its face: expropriation, demolition, and rebuilding at a scale unrelated to the lot pattern around it. The same canonical form as the maison d'appartements and the immeuble d'appartements — the common hall — at a different height.

The redevelopment house: two storeys under a pitched roof, an integral garage, a deep planted setback, built from the 1960s and mostly from the 1980s in the cleared parts of La Petite-Bourgogne and in the sectors reached by the Opération 20 000 logements. The study is blunt about what it is — « généralement en rupture avec les développements urbains traditionnels » — and equally clear that each project is internally consistent.

Where it comes from

No canonical form is claimed for this record. It is the one Sud-Ouest residential type that does not match anything else on this site: not the 19th-century terrace the English word town house normally names, not the plex it replaced, and not a suburban model catalogue. It belongs to the fifth phase and to the machinery of urban renewal, and it is recorded here because the borough's own typology records it.

The one type in the borough that arrived from Ottawa rather than from a Montréal builder. Wartime Housing Limited, created by the federal government, drew economical, quickly built models for the workers in the munitions plants; they were meant to be dismantled after the war and instead spread through the outer quarters. One storey, low pitch, a door between two windows of unequal width, and — after seventy years — a façade that may carry brick, sheet metal and artificial stone at once.

Where it comes from

The same object as the wartime housing this site catalogues at Lévis, at Arvida and in Verdun's voisinage Crawford, and the reason the canonical id is shared. What the Sud-Ouest fiche adds is the ordinary end of the story: not a prize-winning NHA neighbourhood but a few streets in Côte-Saint-Paul where the standard plan was dropped into an existing grid.

Notes on the approach

The boundary rule. The sharpest statement of typological thinking as regulation anywhere in this project is in the Sud-Ouest's design-review by-law, in the Nouveaux bâtiments fascicule, under Stratégies d'insertion relativement aux ensembles typologiques. The general objective is already typological — « Le nouveau bâtiment doit s’inspirer des caractéristiques typomorphologiques de l’unité de paysage dans lequel il se trouve et contribuer à l’évolution de son milieu d’insertion » — and a project inside a continuous ensemble must answer that ensemble's gauges, its horizontal and vertical divisions, its materials, openings, setbacks, projections and rear courettes. But then the by-law asks what happens at the seam, and answers it: « Si le site d’un bâtiment projeté est à la limite de deux ensembles typologiques différents (par exemple des triplex avec marge de recul avant et des duplex de la première période de développement, sans marge de recul ni escalier), le nouveau bâtiment doit choisir clairement une des deux typologies et reprendre l’ensemble de ses caractéristiques d’implantation tout en s’inspirant de ses caractéristiques architecturales. » Choose one, and take all of its siting characteristics — not a compromise, not an average, not a transition. The example it picks is exactly the before-and-after this borough is made of: the triplex with a setback on one side of the line, the pre-1880 duplex with no setback and no stair on the other. And where the fabric is genuinely heterogeneous, the rule inverts: the new building must reduce the heterogeneity by drawing on the borough's more traditional types. It is the only place on this site where a municipality writes down what a typology is for.

The code is 22, and the file is 12. The Évaluation du patrimoine urbain series numbers its files and its arrondissements on two different sequences, and they collide. This borough's cahier is file 12_evaluation_patrimoine_sud.pdf, and every one of the 540 sector codes printed inside it begins 22. Meanwhile file 22 in the same series is Saint-Laurent. A code inferred from a filename would be wrong here in both directions, which is why the code on this page was read from inside the document, by extracting every string matching the series' own grammar and counting the prefixes: 540 of 540 read 22.

Five code letters, not four. The methodology paragraph the series repeats in every cahier explains four — E for exceptional sectors, I for interesting ones, N for industrial sectors of interest, U for urban sectors of interest. The Sud-Ouest cahier prints a fifth: AP, secteur d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel, fourteen of them, in a section of their own that is about the ground rather than the buildings. They are an overlay, not a grade, and they are rendered here as one. Sector 22.AP.5 is encoded as the example; the borough's tallies are 15 E, 25 I, 2 N, 1 U and 14 AP.

Sixty-five units from fifty-nine names. The study's synthesis report names a landscape unit only when it lists that unit among some type's lieux d'occurrence, so its own pages yield 59 named units, not 65. The missing six are recoverable rather than lost: the numbering is dense inside each aire, and the highest number reached in each — 1.5, 2.14, 3.20, 4.17, 5.9 — sums to exactly 65, which is the figure the PIIA by-law states independently. The table on this page therefore carries all sixty-five rows, with 2.13, 3.18, 5.4, 5.5, 5.6 and 5.8 present as numbers without names. Four of those six are in Griffintown, which is the aire the postwar expropriations emptied.

Where the study disagrees with itself. Eight units carry two spellings of their name across the study's own cross-reference lists, and the disagreements are kept rather than resolved. Most are typographic (Parc Marguertie-Bourgeoys for Parc Marguerite-Bourgeoys; Rue Notre-Dame Ouest 2 where 4.2 is elsewhere Rue Notre-Dame Ouest 3). One is substantive: unit 3.2 appears as Avenue de l'Église Ouest on two fiches and as Rue De Roberval on a third, and there is no way to tell from the synthesis which is right. The unit fiches would settle it; they were not retrieved.

Two names for the same house. The 2013 study calls type 1.1 the maison villageoise. The PIIA by-law's own Encadré 2, summarising the same seven typologies for applicants, calls the same thing the maison « de faubourg ». Both documents belong to the same borough and the second sends readers to the first. The type card keeps the study's name and records the by-law's.

Two counts and no more. The study puts a number on exactly two of its nineteen types: 38 maisons villageoises, « ce qui lui donne une valeur de rareté », and 352 maisons « boomtown », « ce qui en fait une typologie relativement fréquente ». Both are carried as count_in_place. There is no count for any plex, which is worth stating plainly on the page where the plex is the subject: the density of the type is recorded qualitatively, through how many of the sixty-five units name it as principal or secondary, and that is what the unit table's columns show.

What is not here. The twenty-five 22.I.n secteurs de valeur patrimoniale intéressante, the two 22.N.n industrial sectors and the single 22.U.1 urban sector are counted but not encoded; only the E set and one AP sector are. The sixty-five individual unit fiches — with their limits, blocks, parcel dimensions and lists of immeubles d'intérêt patrimonial — are separate documents held at the borough office and were not retrieved. And the building-scale layer, the TYPOLOGIE_SPECIFIQUE field of the Ville's open data, would let every one of these fifteen types be counted address by address; it was not joined in this pass.
