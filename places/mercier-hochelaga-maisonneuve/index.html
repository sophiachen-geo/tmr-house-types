Residential Architectural Styles of Mercier–Hochelaga-Maisonneuve (borough of Montréal)

Mercier–Hochelaga-Maisonneuve is five annexed municipalities laid end to end along the eastern shore of the island: Longue-Pointe, a French-regime parish of 1722 that became a municipality in 1845; the village of Hochelaga, incorporated 1870 and annexed to Montréal in 1883 because it could not pay for its own sewers; Maisonneuve, created on 27 December 1883 by the Hochelaga landowners who refused to go with it; Beaurivage and Tétraultville, annexed with Longue-Pointe in 1910; and Saint-Jean-de-Dieu, the asylum's own territory, not fully annexed until 1963. The borough itself dates from 1 January 2002. It is one of the largest in Montréal and, with the Lachine canal, the largest concentration of old industrial land in the country.

Maisonneuve is why this page is here. It is the third planning exercise at company-town scale on this site, after Arvida and Témiscaming — and it is the one where the client was a town council rather than a corporation. The distinction runs all the way through the results, and this page is largely an argument about it. The two documents underneath the page are the Ville de Montréal's Évaluation du patrimoine urbain of 2005 and the borough's own Guide patrimonial. Neither is a typology; the first characterises sectors and lists individual buildings, the second regulates stairs, balconies, fascias, soffits, doors, windows, cladding and crowns. So the type records here are shorter than on the borough pages built from a Patri-Arch study, and each one says in its profile_note exactly which document each line came from.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

27.E.1 Le cœur du village d'Hochelaga exceptional This sector corresponds to the historic heart of the village of Hochelaga, with parc Dézéry at its centre. A first chapel was built there in 1833; the site went on to hold a town hall, the police and fire services, public baths, a public market and finally a park. The deep-water port and the railway lines along rue Notre-Dame are the real engines of the quarter's development: they drew in industry and workers' housing, and that housing settled first on rues Moreau, Dézéry and Saint-Germain. Around 1890 the village's links with the adjoining quartier Sainte-Marie were improved by cutting rue Sainte-Catherine through. Between 1972 and 1975 the demolitions for an east–west expressway, together with the laying out of the linear park, largely amputated the original village and changed the park's appearance. Even so, parc Dézéry is in itself a historic place, and the surrounding streets contain some of the oldest buildings in Hochelaga.
Ce secteur correspond au cœur historique du village d'Hochelaga et le parc Dézéry en est le centre. En 1833, on y construit une première chapelle, puis ce site sera occupé par un hôtel de ville, les services de police et de pompiers, des bains publics, un marché public et enfin un parc. Le port en eau profonde et les voies ferrées le long de la rue Notre-Dame sont les véritables moteurs de développement du quartier. Ils attirent des industries et des habitations ouvrières. Celles-ci s'implantent d'abord sur les rues Moreau, Dézéry et Saint-Germain. Vers 1890, le village d'Hochelaga voit ses liens avec le quartier Sainte-Marie, situé juste à côté, améliorés par le percement de la rue Sainte-Catherine. Entre 1972 et 1975, les démolitions en vue de l'aménagement d'une autoroute est-ouest ainsi que l'aménagement du parc linéaire ont largement amputé le village original et changé l'aspect du parc. Malgré tout, le parc Dézéry est en soi un lieu historique et les rues avoisinantes comprennent certains des bâtiments les plus anciens d'Hochelaga.
The three streets the fiche names as the first ground of workers' housing — Moreau, Dézéry and Saint-Germain — are the same three that carry the borough's two documented nineteenth-century workers' housing records: the Hudon mill's company row on Saint-Germain, and the plex alignments on Moreau and Dézéry. The 1972–1975 demolitions for the never-built east–west expressway removed more than 1,000 dwellings along rue Notre-Dame borough-wide, per the same document's chapter 2.

27.E.2 Maisonneuve exceptional This sector takes in the greater part of Maisonneuve, including Viauville (between rues Sicard and Viau). Maisonneuve was founded in 1883 by large landowners, financiers and industrialists who took a dim view of Hochelaga's annexation to Montréal. They therefore decided to create out of whole cloth a new municipality intended to become a paradise of industry and to attract a working population. This sector gathers the most eloquent built evidence of the cité-modèle of Maisonneuve: in particular the perspective of avenue Morgan, the square ornamented with a work by the sculptor Alfred Laliberté, the Maisonneuve market and public bath, and the three-storey workers' housing along tree-lined streets.
Ce secteur comprend la plus grande partie de Maisonneuve, dont Viauville (entre les rues Sicard et Viau). Maisonneuve a été fondé en 1883 par des grands propriétaires terriens, financiers et industriels qui voyaient d'un mauvais œil l'annexion d'Hochelaga à Montréal. Ils décident donc de créer de toute pièce une nouvelle municipalité appelée à devenir un paradis de l'industrie et à attirer une population ouvrière. Ce secteur regroupe les plus éloquents témoignages bâtis de la cité-modèle de Maisonneuve : notamment la perspective de l'avenue Morgan, la place ornée d'une œuvre du sculpteur Alfred Laliberté, le marché et le bain Maisonneuve, ainsi que les habitations ouvrières de trois étages le long de rues bordées d'arbres.
The document's phrase is « cité-modèle » — a model CITY, said of the municipality. It is not « maison-modèle », and neither this fiche nor any other part of the cahier documents a model house for Maisonneuve. The housing clause is the whole of what the fiche says about dwellings: « les habitations ouvrières de trois étages le long de rues bordées d'arbres ». Most of the built evidence it lists — avenue Morgan, the Laliberté sculpture, the market and the bath — was placed under provincial classement in 2022 as the Site patrimonial de l'Ancienne-Cité-de-Maisonneuve.
 avenue Morgan, rue Sicard, rue Viau, rue Ontario Est, rue Sainte-Catherine Est
27.E.3 Boulevard Pie-IX exceptional Pie-IX was one of Maisonneuve's major traffic axes; it was even foreseen that it would eventually link the river to the rivière des Prairies. The subdivision along this boulevard favoured rich constructions on larger lots than those found in the surrounding area. Even though the tree-lined central allée has disappeared, several buildings still bear witness to the splendour this thoroughfare once had.
Pie-IX était un axe de circulation majeur de Maisonneuve. On prévoyait même qu'à terme, il relierait le fleuve à la rivière des Prairies. Le lotissement le long de ce boulevard privilégiait de riches constructions sur des terrains plus grands que ce que l'on retrouve dans les environs. Même si l'allée centrale bordée d'arbres a disparu, plusieurs bâtiments témoignent encore du faste qu'avait autrefois cette voie.
This is the borough's one documented sector of large lots and prosperous houses, and the counterpart to the plex streets: the same city, laid out at a different grain. The lost « allée centrale bordée d'arbres » is the Park and Boulevard Movement half of Maisonneuve's 1910 planning ideas, and it is the piece that did not survive; the avenue Morgan terre-plein, which did, is the surviving example.

27.E.4 Saint-Jean-de-Dieu exceptional It was in 1874 that the Sœurs de la Providence had a first asylum built on this site. It was destroyed by fire in 1890 and a new hospital was built from 1895 to 1901 to the plans of the architect Hippolyte Bergeron. Onto this first building, which still survives, several pavilions were gradually grafted in 1926, 1935 and 1963, forming the immense institutional complex known today, the largest in the borough.
C'est en 1874 que les Sœurs de la Providence font construire sur ce site un premier asile. Il est détruit par le feu en 1890 et un nouvel hôpital est bâti de 1895 à 1901 selon les plans de l'architecte Hippolyte Bergeron. À ce premier immeuble, qui subsiste toujours, se greffent graduellement plusieurs pavillons en 1926, 1935 et 1963, formant l'immense complexe institutionnel que l'on connaît aujourd'hui, le plus important de l'arrondissement.
An institutional complex, not a residential sector; no type record attaches to it. The municipality of Saint-Jean-de-Dieu was the hospital's own territory, and it was not fully annexed to Montréal until 1963. The cahier's archaeological chapter dates the opening a year later than this fiche does — « L'hospice Saint-Jean-de-Dieu ouvre ses portes en 1875 » against « c'est en 1874 que … font construire » — the difference between commissioning and opening; both readings are the document's own.

27.E.5 Parc olympique exceptional Montréal's largest sports complex was built to host the 1976 Olympic Games. It includes, among other things, the Olympic stadium and its tower, a true icon of Montréal. The stadium quickly established itself as the place of choice for holding all of the metropolis's large-scale events: professional sport, rock concerts, exhibitions and great gatherings. The velodrome with its concrete vault and the stadium's leaning tower are genuine technological feats.
Le plus grand complexe sportif de Montréal a été construit pour accueillir les Jeux olympiques de 1976. Il comprend entre autres le stade olympique et sa tour, véritable icône de Montréal. Le stade s'est vite imposé comme lieu de prédilection pour la tenue de tous les événements à grand déploiement de la métropole : sport professionnel, concerts rock, expositions et grands rassemblements. Le vélodrome avec sa voûte de béton et la tour penchée du stade sont de véritables prouesses technologiques.
Not residential. It is kept because the 2005 document numbers it among the five sectors of exceptional value, and because it stands on the ground Maisonneuve had reserved for its own grand park: the cahier records that the projected parc Maisonneuve was to have carried a racetrack, an amphitheatre, an art gallery, a museum, a botanical garden and a zoo, and that « ce n'est que beaucoup plus tard que certains de ces projets se matérialiseront ».

1670–1845 · Longue-Pointe — the côtes, the redoubt and the village

1670 – 1845

The Sulpicians, seigneurs of the island since 1663, conceded these lands at the end of the seventeenth century, and a chain of forts went up around the shore against Iroquois raids; a wooden redoubt stood at Longue-Pointe by about 1670. A chapel and presbytery followed, and in 1722 Saint-François-d'Assise de la Longue Pointe became one of the few parishes erected on the island under the French regime. In 1825 the parish held 67 houses, occupied by artisans, innkeepers, rentiers, day labourers and, above all, farmers. Longue-Pointe became a municipality in 1845. Its houses were squared-timber boxes sheathed in board, shingle or brick, and a fragment of them still stands.

What was on the ground before any of the rest of it. Small squared-timber houses sheathed in board, shingle or brick, strung along the old chemin du Roy around a parish founded in 1722 — the oldest settlement layer in the borough, and the one most thoroughly cut apart, first by the tramway that reached the end of the island in 1897 and then by the bridge-tunnel approaches of the early 1960s.

Where it comes from

Longue-Pointe is the borough's French-regime layer. A wooden redoubt went up on the shore about 1670 in the chain of forts protecting Ville-Marie; a chapel and presbytery followed at the turn of the eighteenth century; Saint-François-d'Assise de la Longue Pointe became, in 1722, one of the few parishes erected on the island under the French regime, and the territory became a municipality in 1845. In 1825 the parish counted 67 houses, holding « de nombreux artisans, des aubergistes, des rentiers, des journaliers mais surtout des agriculteurs ». Its urbanisation came late precisely because it was far from Montréal's economy, and the cahier says so: development « se fera plus tardivement que celui des municipalités voisines (Hochelaga et Maisonneuve) puisqu'elle est située assez loin du centre des activités économiques de Montréal ». Longue-Pointe, Beaurivage and Tétraultville were all annexed to Montréal in 1910.

1845–1883 · Hochelaga industrialises and is annexed

1845 – 1883

Montréal became the metropolis of Canada in the second half of the nineteenth century, and Hochelaga was where the overflow went. Between 1871 and 1881 four large works arrived — the W.C. McDonald tobacco factory, the Abattoirs de l'Est, the V. Hudon cotton mills and the Filature Sainte-Anne, the last two merging in 1905 into Dominion Textile, which then held half the Canadian textile industry. They drew cheap labour off the farms, and, in the cahier's phrase, « les terrains sont lotis sans réglementation particulière ». The workers settled south of the tracks, on rues Moreau, Dézéry and Saint-Germain. On Saint-Germain the Hudon mill built its own workers' housing in 1881 — brick-faced wooden cubes in an unbroken run at the sidewalk edge, two dwellings each with the stair inside — and the Ville's MEM calls that row « le seul exemple encore existant d'habitations érigées au XIXe siècle par une compagnie à Montréal ». By 1883 Hochelaga's growth had outrun its ability to pay for sewers, water and street openings, and it asked to be annexed.

The corporate counterpart to Maisonneuve, built two years before Maisonneuve existed. Brick-faced wooden cubes running unbroken along the sidewalk on rue Saint-Germain, two dwellings each with the stair inside, put up in 1881 by the V. Hudon cotton mill for its own workers — and, according to the Ville's MEM, the only surviving nineteenth-century company-built housing in Montréal.

Where it comes from

Hochelaga industrialised faster than it could house anyone. Four large works arrived between 1871 and 1881 — the W.C. McDonald tobacco factory, the Abattoirs de l'Est, the Compagnie des moulins à coton V. Hudon and the Filature Sainte-Anne, the last two merging in 1905 into Dominion Textile, which then held half of the Canadian textile industry — and, in the Évaluation's words, « les terrains sont lotis sans réglementation particulière ». The Hudon mill announced its own workers' housing in early December 1881 and built this row; it was renovated in 1981, exactly a century later. Read this record against the plex on the next page and against Arvida and Témiscaming: an employer housing its workforce directly is what a company town does, and it is precisely what Maisonneuve two years later did not do. Maisonneuve's promoters sold lots and built civic monuments instead, and left the housing to the ordinary speculative builder.

1883–1918 · The cité de Maisonneuve

1883 – 1918

In December 1883 the large proprietors east of avenue Bourbonnière asked for, and got, a municipality of their own. Maisonneuve was proclaimed on 27 December 1883 by the lieutenant-governor, at the initiative of a group of French-Canadian industrialists and businessmen — Alphonse Desjardins, Hector Barsalou, Charles-Théodore Viau, William Bennett, Charles-Henri Létourneux, Raymond Préfontaine — who, the RPCQ says, wanted to make it « la "Westmount des francophones" ». Their method was straightforward: huge cheap lots, tax exemptions for incoming industry, and a site beside the new Sutherland (1891) and Tarte (1900) wharves and the rail lines. It worked. St. Lawrence Sugar came in 1887, Canadian Vickers in 1911; textiles, biscuits, confectionery, foundries, shipbuilding and shoes followed. The tramway arrived in 1892. Population went from about 350 at the founding to 2,000 in 1896 and roughly 18,000 in 1911, eighty per cent francophone and « composée surtout de travailleurs et de locataires ». On the industrial ranking, note that the two sources give two different readings and this page prints both: the RPCQ says that by 1910 Maisonneuve « figure au cinquième rang des villes industrielles du Canada » and that its industry was « principalement axée sur la fabrication de chaussures »; the Ville's cahier says that fifteen years after its creation the municipality « devient la cinquième ville industrielle au Canada et la deuxième au Québec » and that it advertised itself as la Pittsburgh canadienne. The popular claim that Maisonneuve was the world capital of the shoe industry appears in neither, and is not repeated here. Then, in 1910, the elected officials — who were also, as the cahier drily notes, the town's property developers — decided their city should become the garden of Montréal. Under maire Alexandre Michaud and échevin Oscar Dufresne, the municipal engineer Marius Dufresne (1883–1945) drew what the RPCQ calls « un plan d'aménagement ambitieux » and, flatly, « le plus ambitieux réalisé au Québec à cette époque »: monumental buildings, parks, urban boulevards, and a remedy for the ills of industrial cities. The inspiration is named — the American civic-embellishment movement City Beautiful, and the Park and Boulevard Movement — and so is its content: Beaux-Arts architecture, wide boulevards, planned vistas, parks, and the functional separation of space. Built: the hôtel de ville (1910–1912, Joseph-Cajetan Dufort), the marché public (1912–1914), the caserne de pompiers no 1, or caserne Letourneux (1914–1915, Marius Dufresne), and the bain et gymnase publics, the bain Morgan (1914–1916). Avenue Morgan, the plan's spine, was inaugurated in 1912 and drawn by the landscape architect Frederick Gage Todd — the same hand that laid out Town of Mount Royal for the Canadian Northern Railway in the same decade. Alfred Laliberté made the bronzes. A hippodrome was projected and never built. Marius Dufresne and his brother built themselves the Château Dufresne, 1915–1918, on twenty reinforced-concrete piers. Here is the comparison that matters. Arvida was Alcoa's, Témiscaming was Riordon's and then Canadian International Paper's; in both, one client owned the ground, hired one designer, and built the houses along with everything else — 270 houses in 135 days at Arvida, every dwelling of Témiscaming's lower town by Ross and Macdonald. Maisonneuve had the same ambition at the same scale and the opposite structure. Its client was a municipal corporation spending borrowed public money, and a municipal corporation can build a market, a bath and a boulevard but it cannot build anyone's house. So the plan produced monuments and left the housing to speculative builders, who put up the ordinary Montréal plex. That is the difference in one line, and it is visible on the ground: a Beaux-Arts civic core in cut limestone, and, three streets away, a rangée of three-storey brick and greystone plexes with iron stairs curving up out of the front yards. The city could not pay for the monuments. In the middle of the wartime economic crisis, deep in debt, the Cité de Maisonneuve was annexed to Montréal by provincial statute in 1918, after thirty-five years of autonomy.

The exception the record keeps insisting on. Two houses for two brothers built as one Beaux-Arts block in Indiana limestone — Marius Dufresne, who as municipal engineer designed Maisonneuve's public buildings, on one side, and Oscar Dufresne, who ran the family shoe factory, on the other — standing on twenty reinforced-concrete piers at a moment when almost no house in Montréal did. Forty rooms, a winter garden, garages underground.

Where it comes from

Maisonneuve was 80 % francophone and, in the Évaluation's phrase, « composée surtout de travailleurs et de locataires »; its promoters had founded the place in 1883 to be, as the RPCQ puts it, « la "Westmount des francophones" ». The bourgeois house is where that ambition became a building. The Dufresne brothers were both instruments of it: Oscar sat as échevin and ran Dufresne et Locke, the shoe manufactory established at Maisonneuve about 1900 and one of its largest employers; Marius was municipal engineer from 1910 to 1918 and drew the market (1912–1914), the fire station (1914–1915) and the public bath and gymnasium (1914–1916). They began their own house in 1915, the year the civic building programme was breaking the city's finances, and finished it in 1918, the year the province annexed Maisonneuve to Montréal. It was classé in 1976; the civic buildings had to wait until 2022.

The other half of Maisonneuve's zoning experiment. Where it comes from

The tramway is the cause. The Évaluation dates Hochelaga's commercial axes to the moment the streetcar arrived — rue Sainte-Catherine and the new Montreal Street Railway viaduct of 1892 « pour desservir les populations de l'est montréalais » — and dates Maisonneuve's own growth from the same year: « à partir de 1892, les tramways favorisent le développement rapide de Maisonneuve et placent la municipalité dans la zone d'influence de Montréal ». Shops followed the rails, and the corner block is what a builder puts on a lot that has to earn from both a shopfront and a stack of flats.

Three-storey workers' plex L'habitation ouvrière de trois étages (« plex »)

Style: Montréal faubourg vernacular
27.E.127.E.2

What Maisonneuve's workers actually lived in. Three storeys of grey limestone or clay brick under a flat roof, one dwelling to a floor, each with its own door off the street and the upper doors reached by an iron stair curving up out of the front yard — repeated down both sides of a street planted with trees. The Évaluation gives it one clause and no fiche, which is the point: the ambition at Maisonneuve went into the civic buildings and the boulevard, and the housing was the standard Montréal product.

Where it comes from

The form arrives with the industry. Hochelaga's mills — the W.C. McDonald tobacco works, the Abattoirs de l'Est, the V. Hudon cotton mills and the Filature Sainte-Anne, all established between 1871 and 1881 — drew what the cahier calls « une main-d'œuvre bon marché issue des populations agricoles qui convergent vers la ville », and « les terrains sont lotis sans réglementation particulière ». At Maisonneuve after 1883 the promoters offered huge cheap lots and tax exemptions to industry, and the population went from about 350 at the founding to 2,000 in 1896 and roughly 18,000 in 1911 — 80 % francophone and « composée surtout de travailleurs et de locataires ». Renters in a boom is what the plex is for. Maisonneuve also enacted a zoning by-law that the cahier calls « une nouveauté à l'époque », barring shops and industry from certain streets reserved for housing alone — one of the earliest use-separation controls in Québec, and the reason these streets read as purely residential while rue Ontario Est a block away does not.

1918–1945 · A Montréal quartier, building east and north

1918 – 1945

Annexation did not stop the building; it moved it. Development ran east and north through the former Longue-Pointe, Beaurivage and Tétraultville, which had come in as part of Montréal in 1910. Maisonneuve's ideals outlived its treasury: the RPCQ notes that they were « néanmoins perpétués dans les bâtiments institutionnels et les aménagements conçus au cours des années suivantes », and the clearest instance is parc Morgan itself, laid out in 1939 to Todd's plans on the old Milton Lodge estate the Morgan family had ceded to Maisonneuve in 1908 — twenty-one years after the city that commissioned it had ceased to exist. The old hôtel de ville, meanwhile, housed the Institut du Radium de Montréal from 1926 to 1967; La Bolduc, Mary Travers, was treated there and died there.

1945–1976 · Suburban Mercier — veterans' houses, bungalows, the Olympic Park

1945 – 1976

Mercier turned suburban. Around 1950, ensembles of veterans' houses went up north of rue de Rouen on the Wartime Housing Limited model, and the cahier records the mechanism exactly: a wartime emergency type that « fut adopté par la suite par plusieurs promoteurs » selling affordable detached houses. Four bungalow developments followed between about 1951 and 1955 — identical brick houses, one of them with no front door on the street face at all, another laid out on loop streets. Place de Léry, 1951–52, brought semi-detached two-storey houses with glass block, false tile roofs and porticos. And in 1976 the Olympic Park landed on the ground Maisonneuve had reserved for its own grand park.

The other post-war Mercier, and the odder one. Identical brick bungalows put up in whole streets in the early 1950s — in one development with no front door at all on the street face, just a band of windows pushed to one side — laid out with rows of trees along the sidewalk that the Ville's surveyors could still recognise fifty years later as part of the original plan.

Where it comes from

Mercier is where the borough turned suburban. The Évaluation notes that the criteria it used to find heritage sectors « n'a pas permis d'identifier beaucoup de secteurs dans la partie est du territoire », and answers itself: « cela ne signifie pas pour autant que cette partie de l'arrondissement ne présente pas d'intérêt. Les qualités d'ensemble de plusieurs rues devraient être maintenues. » That is why these four developments are recorded as ensembles urbains d'intérêt rather than as heritage sectors — the value is in the coherence of a whole street, not in any single house, and the document's own recommendation is to study such territories, « recenser les typologies des ensembles qui présentent une grande homogénéité », and build public awareness of them. It is a recommendation for exactly the kind of record this page is.

The federal minimal house, arriving in Mercier after the war it was invented for. A detached one-and-a-half-storey box under a two-slope roof, clad in pale clapboard or brick, built in whole streets to one volume so that the ensemble reads as a single gesture — around 1950 north of rue de Rouen, in 1953 on rue Curatteau.

Where it comes from

The Évaluation states the lineage exactly: these ensembles were built « autour de 1950 sur le modèle des maisons de la Wartime Housing Limited. Ce modèle a été développé durant la Deuxième Guerre mondiale pour répondre à la crise du logement qui sévissait alors et pour loger les ouvriers à proximité des entreprises consacrées à l'industrie de guerre. 1976–2022 · Demolition, reconstruction, and a provincial classement

1976 – 2022

Between 1972 and 1975 the widening of rue Notre-Dame into an urban boulevard demolished more than a thousand dwellings, and the works for the abandoned east–west expressway, together with the linear park that replaced it, amputated the old village of Hochelaga and changed parc Dézéry. Rebuilding on the cleared block-heads began in 1987. Then, at the very end of the sequence, the protection arrived — and from an unexpected direction. See the notes below.

Notes on the approach

The arrondissement code is 27, and the filename says 14. The Ville publishes this borough's cahier as 14_evaluation_patrimoine_mercier.pdf, but every sector code printed inside it begins with 27: 27.E.1–5, 27.I.1–6, 27.U.1–5, 27.N.1–2, and 27.AP.1–3 alongside 27.A.1–2 for archaeology. The file-number sequence and the arrondissement-number sequence are two different things and they collide across the series — the Sud-Ouest is code 22 in file 12, while file 22 is Saint-Laurent. A stray 14.AP.1 does appear on this cahier's archaeological synthesis map; it is a neighbouring borough's label bleeding across the sheet edge, not an MHM sector. The code was read from inside the document, as it must always be.

Only the five E sectors are encoded. The cahier's other twenty-one sectors are enumerated in the header of sectors.yaml so the count is not lost, but their fiches are Part 10b work. Two type records on this page therefore cite U-sector and I-sector codes in their notes rather than in their sector fields.

Maisonneuve is protected by a provincial classement, not a municipal citation — and that is unusual here. Every other heritage instrument across the five boroughs of this part is municipal: borough zoning by-laws, PIIA regulations, demolition control, cited sites. The Site patrimonial de l'Ancienne-Cité-de-Maisonneuve is not. There is one other provincial area protection touching a Montréal borough on this site — the site patrimonial du Mont-Royal, declared in 2005, which overlaps Outremont's southern flank — but that one is drawn around a mountain, and it caught the houses on its slope incidentally. This one was drawn around a town plan, and it caught exactly what the plan built. It was classed by the Minister of Culture and Communications under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel, and it carries two dates that look wrong and are not: Statut: Classement — Date: 2022-03-03, Prise d'effet: 2021-03-30, following an avis d'intention de classement of 2021-03-29. Protection in Québec bites from the minister's notice of intent, not from the decree that follows it, so the effective date precedes the classement by nearly a year; the same pattern governs Vieux-Terrebonne elsewhere on this site, and the glossary entry for prise d'effet explains it. The classement covers works of 1910 to 1939 — the former hôtel de ville, the former marché public, the bain et gymnase publics, the former caserne de pompiers, avenue Morgan and parc Morgan — together with Alfred Laliberté's two bronze fountain-sculptures, La Fermière and Les Petits baigneurs. Protection applies to building exteriors, to the sculptures, to the urban works and to the grounds. The Château Dufresne, classed on its own in 1976, sits inside the same story but under a separate instrument.

The province did what the city recommended, seventeen years late and with a stronger tool. The 2005 cahier's borough-specific recommendations include this one: « de constituer en site du patrimoine le centre-ville de Maisonneuve organisé autour de l'axe de l'avenue Morgan. Ses qualités urbaines certaines et la valeur patrimoniale de plusieurs bâtiments qui le bordent lui confèrent indéniablement une valeur à l'échelle montréalaise. » A site du patrimoine was the municipal instrument of the day. What eventually arrived, in 2021–2022, was a provincial classement instead. The same cahier also warned about what municipal instruments had failed to hold: several buildings selected by the CUM in 1982 had since been demolished — the asile Saint-Benoît-Joseph-Labre, the couvent Saint-Isidore, the Warden King and Son works, the Canadian Spool Cotton, the école Maisonneuve — and it drew the conclusion that this borough's heritage is fragile because it is made of modest things, « composé surtout de bâtiments modestes : écoles, maisons ouvrières, industries ».

There is no documented Maisonneuve "maison-modèle", and none has been invented here. The word modèle does occur in the cahier, twice, and neither use is a house type of the cité. Sector 27.E.2 calls the municipality itself « la cité-modèle de Maisonneuve » — a model city. Sector 27.U.1 speaks of « le modèle des maisons de la Wartime Housing Limited » — a federal house type of the 1940s, adopted in Mercier around 1950, thirty years after the cité had ceased to exist. What the Évaluation actually says about Maisonneuve's housing is one clause, and this page quotes it wherever it matters: « les habitations ouvrières de trois étages le long de rues bordées d'arbres », glossed in the same document's overview as « un patrimoine résidentiel composé de "plex" en pierre ou en brique le long de rues bordées d'arbres ». Three-storey plex and rangée, in other words; the standard Montréal product, and it is encoded as such.

One correction to the research memo. The RPCQ does not describe the Château Dufresne as the first reinforced-concrete private residence in Montréal. Its words are « l'une des premières utilisations de ce matériau pour la construction résidentielle à Montréal, voire au Québec » and « l'une des premières résidences à structure en béton armé à Montréal ». One of the first. The superlative is not published on this site. Similarly, the caserne Letourneux's frequently repeated debt to Frank Lloyd Wright's Unity Temple is not in the RPCQ fiche for that building, which gives only the dates 1914–1915 and the architect Marius Dufresne, and it is therefore not asserted here either.

What regulates the houses today. Not the classement, which stops at the civic ensemble. Ordinary houses are governed by the borough's zoning by-law 01-275 through two designations it carries directly — secteurs significatifs, where norms and criteria apply to any exterior work visible from the street, and immeubles significatifs — plus twelve PIIA areas, the demolition by-law RCA02-27006, and the lotissement by-law RCA04-27003. The borough's Guide patrimonial is the working manual, and it is unusually explicit about method: where a similar adjacent building still has its original components, « cet immeuble sert de référence pour les interventions ». The neighbour is the standard. That is typological regulation done without a typology.
