## intro

Verdun exists because of a wall. The land is flat, low and right against the Saint-Laurent at the foot of the Lachine rapids, and until the river was held back every spring flood made it unbuildable — the 2005 <em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em> says so in one sentence: residential development <em>« prend son envol au début du XX<sup>e</sup> siècle, après la mise en place de la digue protégeant le territoire des fortes inondations printanières »</em>. The dike went up from the 1890s; the town filled in behind it in about sixty years and then stopped, because it had run out of ground.

The corporate history is short and steep. The village of la Rivière-Saint-Pierre was detached from the paroisse Notre-Dame on 1 January 1875 and took the name Verdun the following year; it became a <em>ville</em> in 1907 and a <em>cité</em> in 1912, and it was a separate municipality until 1 January 2002, when it became a borough of Montréal. Its population was about 1,900 in 1901 and 25,000 twenty years later. By the 1920s, the cahier records, Verdun was <strong>the third-largest city in Québec</strong>, after Montréal and Québec, and it was extraordinarily dense for a place with no tall buildings. The housing shortage that followed became, in the document's word, <em>endémique</em>: by 1946 every one of the <strong>18,000 dwellings</strong> in the city was occupied.

What was built is plex, almost without exception, and the cahier's own sector fiches quietly divide it in two. North of avenue Desmarchais — the older half, built through <em>« la vague de 1900 à 1920 »</em> — the standard building is three storeys of brick with the stair to the upper flats climbing the outside, elaborate cornices, vertical windows with stained glass, and lanes laid out in an H behind. The document's verdict on it is five words: <em>« Ces constructions sont typiquement montréalaises. »</em> South of Desmarchais the same fabric changes register — two storeys, few exterior stairs, and a deep front balcony with woodwork the cahier twice pauses to admire. The two residential sectors it ranks as <em>exceptional</em> are both of the southern kind, and both are ranked for uniformity rather than for rarity.

Two things interrupt the plex. <strong>Crawford Park</strong>, laid out just before the Second World War under the federal National Housing Act, is a few hundred brick houses of a storey and a half built to award-winning plans, on streets that abandon the grid — the borough's one piece of planned suburb, and the same object as the wartime housing already recorded at Lévis and in the Sud-Ouest. And <strong>île des Sœurs</strong>, farmland belonging to the Congrégation de Notre-Dame from 1769 until it was annexed in 1956, was replanned in the 1960s in collaboration with <strong>Ludwig Mies van der Rohe</strong>, who left three residential slabs and a filling station on it. A city built out of one repeated building, with a garden suburb and a modernist new town stapled to either end.

## phase:p1

The côte de Verdun — <em>« ou des Argoulets »</em> — is the strip of first concessions south of Montréal at the foot of the Lachine rapids, occupied by soldiers in 1660 to hold the approach to the town against Iroquois raids. After the Grande paix of 1701 the land goes over to farming. Facing it in the river, île Saint-Paul is bought in part by the fur merchant Jacques LeBer in the last quarter of the seventeenth century, who builds a fortified farm on the north-east terrace; his daughter Jeanne passes the land to the Congrégation de Notre-Dame, and by the second half of the eighteenth century the sisters own the whole island, farming it from the ferme Saint-Paul and then, after a fire, the ferme Sainte-Famille. The aqueduct canal that will become Verdun's northern boundary is dug on Thomas C. Keefer's 1852 scheme and supplies Montréal with drinking water from 1856. Almost nothing residential survives from this phase; the cahier says plainly that <em>« il ne reste pratiquement aucune trace des premiers établissements dans Verdun »</em>, and names the maison Étienne-Nivard-de-Saint-Dizier on boulevard LaSalle as the one reminder of the road's age.

## phase:p2

The village is created in 1875 and renamed Verdun in 1876, and then almost nothing happens for twenty-five years, because the river will not let it. Spring floods make the banks unusable and stall the municipality; the council looks for a fix and builds a first dike in the 1890s. It works well enough to open the land, though the cahier notes that the flooding was only ended for good by the canalisation of the Saint-Laurent much later. The dike also produced the promenade along the river that is still the borough's front. The two founding routes are already in place: the chemin de la rivière Saint-Pierre or Lower Lachine Road, now boulevard LaSalle, and the chemin de la côte Saint-Paul, now rue de l'Église.

## phase:p3

The wave. Behind the dike the town fills up in a single generation, mostly between 1900 and 1920, with a working population employed in Montréal and — in 1915 — built for by small proprietors rather than by developers. The building is the three-storey plex with the exterior stair, repeated street after street on conventional lots with lanes; three separate sector fiches describe it in the same words. Verdun becomes a <em>ville</em> in 1907 and a <em>cité</em> in 1912, and by the 1920s it is the third city of Québec. On rue Wellington and rue de l'Église, built to the property line with no setback to project a balcony into, the same fabric produces the one invention the cahier calls Verdun's own: the <em>balcon en alcôve</em>, cut back into the wall. South of avenue Desmarchais the plex loses a storey and its outside stair and gains its deep balcony, and it is that southern version that will be ranked exceptional.

## phase:p4

The Depression hits hard and the housing shortage becomes chronic. Two federal instruments answer it. The National Housing Act, enacted a few years before the war, produces <em>« l'habitation unifamiliale de masse »</em>, and Verdun gets one of its best examples: Crawford Park, brick houses of a storey and a half to prize-winning plans, uniform in height, material, roof pitch, siting and planting, on curving streets that break with the grid. The cahier records the same house twice more in the borough on conventional street plans, so that the three occurrences differ only in layout. Meanwhile munitions plants operate in Verdun through both world wars, in a town the document otherwise calls almost exclusively residential, and wartime construction pushes the stock to its peak: in 1946 all 18,000 dwellings are occupied.

## phase:p5

The last mainland ground goes after 1950 — the strip between the aqueduct canal and rue Bannantyne, built up in row duplexes from a handful of repeated models — and by the end of the 1950s the territory is full. The expansion moves to the only virgin land left. Île des Sœurs is annexed in 1956, the sisters leave, and through the 1960s the island is replanned as, in the cahier's phrase, <em>« un vaste projet de développement urbain planifié »</em> worked out with Mies van der Rohe: build tall to free the ground, make the shore public, separate people from cars, and turn the blocks so the river shows between them. Three residential slabs and an Esso station by Mies survive from it. By 1968 the island holds over 800 dwellings and 3,000 residents; it now holds more than 13,000, out of about 60,000 in the borough. The pont Champlain, begun in 1957 and open in 1962, crosses at its edge. Verdun is merged into Montréal on 1 January 2002.

## notes

This page is built on a territorial characterisation, not a type catalogue, and six things about that need stating plainly.

<strong>The borough code is 21, and it was read from inside the document.</strong> The cahier is published by the Ville de Montréal as <code>24_evaluation_patrimoine_ver.pdf</code>, but every code printed inside it begins <code>21</code> — 21.E.1A to 21.E.7, 21.I.1 to 21.I.10, 21.U.1, and 21.AP.1 and 21.AP.2. The file prefix belongs to the numbering of the published volumes, not to the sectors, and the two sequences do not correspond: the Sud-Ouest is code 22 in file 12, while Saint-Laurent is file 22. Never infer one from the other.

<strong>Only the exceptional-value sectors are encoded.</strong> Nine records, covering the seven <em>secteurs de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle</em> — 21.E.1 is printed in three lettered parts. The cahier also carries ten <em>secteurs de valeur patrimoniale intéressante</em>, one <em>ensemble urbain d'intérêt</em> and two <em>secteurs d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel</em>, and they are not thin: 21.I.1 le Vieux-Verdun, 21.I.2 le voisinage Nord, 21.I.3 l'îlot ancien (a pre-lane subdivision where <em>« l'absence de ruelle a entraîné une typologie particulière donnant accès à la cour arrière par une porte cochère »</em>), 21.I.4 le boulevard LaSalle, 21.I.5 le secteur Rielle et de Verdun, 21.I.6 le secteur des Avenues, 21.I.7 le secteur Woodland et de Verdun, 21.I.8 le voisinage Ouest, 21.I.9 un projet « National Housing Act », 21.I.10 le voisinage Sud, 21.U.1 l'île des Sœurs, and 21.AP.1 île des Sœurs and 21.AP.2 zone côtière for archaeology. Four of the type records on this page are built out of those fiches even though the sectors themselves are not published here, and each says so in its <em>profile_note</em>. Enumerating the I, U and AP sectors is Part 10b.

<strong>The cahier disagrees with itself, and with the municipal record, about dates.</strong> Chapter 2 says the village of la Rivière-Saint-Pierre was created <em>« en 1874 »</em> and renamed Verdun <em>« en 1878 »</em>; the archaeology chapter of the same document says <em>« le village est créé en 1876 »</em>; the municipal-status record gives 1 January 1875 for the creation and 28 December 1876 for the renaming. Chapter 2 also says Verdun obtained <em>« son statut de ville en 1909 »</em>, where the municipal record gives 14 March 1907 for <em>ville</em> and 21 December 1912 for <em>cité</em>. The dike is dated 1895 in chapter 2 and <em>« dont la construction s'amorce en 1896 »</em> in the archaeology chapter. The corporate dates used on this page are the municipal ones and the phase boundaries avoid resting on any of the contested years; the cahier's own figures are kept wherever they are the thing being quoted. The superficie is a fourth case: the Part 10 memo gives 9.7 km², the fr.wikipedia infobox 983 ha, and no municipal source giving an area was retrieved — both figures are printed rather than averaged.

<strong>Two instruments could not be confirmed as the memo states them.</strong> The Part 10 memo lists a <em>« 987-series »</em> demolition by-law for Verdun. No municipal source found gives one; the borough's demolition regime is <strong>RCA21 210020</strong>, under which the members of the comité consultatif d'urbanisme sit as the comité de démolition, and that is what is recorded. Separately, the <strong>2020 fee schedule</strong> — <em>Règlement sur les tarifs (exercice financier 2020)</em>, RCA19 210007 — is indexed as carrying a PIIA study fee, $265, reserved for one operation and one building: enlarging the roof volume of a <strong>"wartime"-type house</strong> to add habitable space. That is the only fee schedule anywhere on this site that names a house type, and it is worth saying why it matters — a tariff line is the point at which a typological category stops being descriptive and starts costing money. But the by-law text itself could not be retrieved: the Ville's legacy document tree returns 404 for every path tried, the 311 information bank redirects in a loop, and the web archive is unreachable from here. The fee is therefore stated in English only, and no French is quoted for it. Getting the primary is a Part 10b task.

<strong>Mies van der Rohe is spelled two ways in the source.</strong> The body of fiche 21.E.6 and the list of <em>immeubles de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle</em> give <em>Mies van der Rohe</em>; the photo captions on the same page and the text of §3.1 give <em>Mies van de Rohe</em>. The correct form is used throughout this page and the slip is recorded rather than reproduced. The three residential slabs are at 100 and 200 rue De Gaspé and 201 rue Corot; the Esso station is at 201 rue Berlioz.

<strong>Verdun is also a French commune, and the two have nothing to do with each other.</strong> Verdun in the Meuse — the battlefield town — has its own heritage designation, a <em>site patrimonial remarquable</em> classed by <em>arrêté du 13 avril 2022</em>. It shares nothing with this borough but a name, taken here from the côte de Verdun, itself named for the first concessions on the ground. This belongs in the site glossary as well as here; it is recorded in this note because <code>data/canon/glossary.yaml</code> is owned by another part of the current work.

Finally, worth listing because the cahier singles them out and no type record on this page covers them: under <em>LES HABITATIONS</em> in its list of exceptionally valuable buildings it names four <em>maisons urbaines</em> — 703-707 rue Gordon, 3267, 3513 and 3523 rue Wellington — a <em>maison bourgeoise</em> at 5695 boulevard LaSalle, and the <strong>maison Nivard-de Saint-Dizier</strong> at 7244 boulevard LaSalle, <em>monument historique reconnu</em>, which the document elsewhere dates to <em>« possiblement au début du XVIII<sup>e</sup> siècle »</em> and attributes to the Sœurs de la Congrégation de Notre-Dame. It gives no typological description of any of them, so none has been turned into a type record here.
