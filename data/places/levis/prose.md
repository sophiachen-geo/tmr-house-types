## intro

Lévis is the place in this project with the best public catalogue of house types and the least legal
protection for the houses in it. The City publishes twenty architectural <em>courants</em>, one page
each, from the <em>Régime français</em> of 1620–1800 to <em>Modernisme</em> of 1950–1975, and every
one of them is set out in the same seven fields — Volumétrie, Plan, Toiture, Revêtements,
Ouvertures, Saillies, Ornementation — with a count of how many survive, a period, a line drawing, a
strip of historic photographs, and a before-and-after pair showing a real Lévis house restored. It
is searchable by street address. Nothing else in Québec except Gatineau's fiches comes close to it
as a public instrument.

What sits underneath it is thinner. Lévis has no site patrimonial déclaré. Its two cited sites are
both former village church cores on the outskirts. The historic core itself — Pointe-Lévy on the
promontory, the carré Déziel, the bourgeois streets of Wolfe and Guenette and Fraser — has no status
under the <em>Loi sur le patrimoine culturel</em> at all, and is covered only by a discretionary
municipal by-law. The City says so itself, in the plainest terms it could have chosen: <em>« Il y a
très peu de bâtiments qui sont visés par cette Loi sur le territoire lévisien. »</em>

The catalogue's own numbers are the argument for reading it as a whole rather than type by type. Of
the roughly four thousand heritage buildings the City counts, the <em>vernaculaire américain</em>
alone accounts for about 1,700 — more than a third — and the four industrial currents of 1880 to
1945 together account for well over three thousand. The Régime français accounts for fifteen. This
is a city whose heritage is overwhelmingly the plain wooden workers' house of the shipyard and
railway decades, and whose catalogue is honest enough to say so.

## phase:p1

The seigneurie de Lauzon is granted to Jean de Lauson in 1636 and Guillaume Couture settles at
Pointe-Lévy as its first colonist in 1647, but the south shore fills a century behind the north —
the brochure blames the Iroquois presence. Through the eighteenth century Pointe-Lévy is farms and
fishing, and in 1759 it is the back of the stage for the siege of Québec, with the English batteries
on the heights above. About fifteen buildings of this period survive across the whole of the merged
city, the smallest count in the catalogue.

## phase:p2

John Caldwell, who inherits the seigneurie, lays a street grid on the top of the cliff and calls it
Aubigny; the avenue Bégin was his Bergami, then his Eden Street. The côte du Passage carries
everyone coming from the Beauce or the United States down to the canotiers who ferried them across.
Allison Davie opens the shipyard at Lauzon in 1829, and from 1836 his widow Elizabeth Taylor Johnson
Davie runs it — the first woman to run a Canadian shipyard. The seigneury is dismantled in 1836.
The house of this half-century is the Québécois house, which at about seven hundred buildings is
still the second most numerous in the catalogue, and the City's own walking tour uses two of them on
rue Wolfe to narrate the change: the maison Lefrançois as the Norman house becoming Canadian, the
maison Pampalon as the same house with its floor lifted high enough to rent the basement.

## phase:p3

Ville de Lévis is incorporated on 18 May 1861, carved out of Mgr Joseph-David Déziel's parish of
Notre-Dame-de-la-Victoire; the brochure's verdict on Déziel is that a priest whose only wealth was
<em>« que son bréviaire et sa soutane »</em> built a city. The forts de Lévis go up in 1865–72
against a Fenian and American threat that never came. Timber and shipbuilding make the town a rival
to Québec across the water, and the promontory streets fill with the Second Empire and Victorian
houses of shipbuilders, railway contractors, mayors and notaries. The three houses at 20, 22-26 and
28 rue Guenette — <em>le trio victorien</em> — are the set piece.

## phase:p4

Alphonse Desjardins founds the first caisse populaire in his own Neo-Gothic house on 6 December
1900, and the industrial house forms arrive more or less together: Boomtown, the maison cubique, the
flat-roofed house, and the <em>vernaculaire américain</em> that outnumbers everything else in the
city. These four currents are the ones the catalogue exists to explain, because they are the ones a
Lévis homeowner is most likely to live in and least likely to think of as heritage.

## phase:p5

Wartime Housing Limited builds for the shipyard and munitions workers, and with it come the light
industrial claddings — asbestos-cement tile, aluminium, masonite — that the City lists without
disapproval as the type's proper materials. The catalogue's last current, <em>Modernisme</em>, is the
only one whose massing it refuses to fix at all: <em>« diversifiée selon l'usage »</em>. Lévis
published a separate Inventaire du patrimoine moderne in 2016, covering 79 buildings put up between
1946 and 1975.

## phase:p6

The catalogue stops at 1975 and says nothing about what came after. In 2002 ten municipalities are
merged into a single Ville de Lévis of three boroughs, which is why a catalogue of a river town's
house types has to carry an agricultural vernacular among its twenty currents, and why thirteen
separate village cores each need their own heritage-sector map sheet.

## notes

<strong>There is no declared site, and the two cited sites are not where you would expect them.</strong>
Lévis has no site patrimonial déclaré at all. It has exactly two cited sites, and both of them are
former village church cores on the periphery rather than the historic urban core: the Site du
patrimoine de Saint-Nicolas, eight buildings of 1760–1887 strung along the old chemin du Roy west of
the Saint-Nicolas village, and the Site du patrimoine de
l'Église-et-du-Presbytère-de-Saint-Jean-Chrysostome, a church, presbytery, cemetery and square in
what is now an inland borough. Vieux-Lévis itself — Pointe-Lévy, the carré Déziel, rue Wolfe, rue
Guenette — has no cited or declared status. Neither does Vieux-Lauzon, and neither does
Saint-Romuald. All three are covered only by the discretionary PIIA by-law, and the City warns
readers not to confuse the two things: <em>« Il est important de ne pas confondre le règlement
municipal (PIIA - volet patrimonial) avec les dispositions prévues dans cette Loi car les
implications ne sont pas les mêmes… Il y a très peu de bâtiments qui sont visés par cette Loi sur le
territoire lévisien. »</em> A 2005 request by the GIRAM to create heritage sites for
<em>« le Vieux-Lévis et le corridor de la rue Saint-Joseph à Lauzon »</em> was not acted on.

<strong>The four-thousand figure is the City's, and it covers the whole merged territory.</strong>
The catalogue's index reads <em>« La Ville de Lévis regroupe plus de 4000 bâtiments patrimoniaux qui
sont souvent associés à un style ou une typologie architecturale »</em>. That is a count for the
entire post-2002 city of ten former municipalities, not for any historic core, and it is not a
statutory inventory figure. The per-courant <em>« Nombre à Lévis »</em> counts on the twenty fiches
sum to roughly 4,050, which is consistent with it.

<strong>Twenty courants, counted on the crawl.</strong> The research memo behind this part said
twenty-one. The crawl of 16 August 2026 harvested the fiche links from the index rather than
guessing a range, and found exactly twenty distinct <code>?idfiche=</code> links, numbered 1 to 20,
each of which returned a page carrying all seven of the catalogue's field labels. Twenty is the
number published here. The idfiche numbers do not follow the index order — Boomtown is fiche 10 and
Vernaculaire américain is fiche 1 — so the ids have to be harvested, never iterated.

<strong>The statutory pre-1940 inventory: not established either way.</strong> Since the 2021
amendments to the <em>Loi sur le patrimoine culturel</em>, MRCs and equivalent territories have been
required to adopt an inventory of their pre-1940 buildings, with a deadline of 1 April 2026. As of
that deadline only 65 of the province's 106 MRCs and equivalent authorities had adopted one, and
Lévis's own position was not established by the sources consulted here. This record therefore
asserts nothing either way. What is documented is that Lévis has an Inventaire du patrimoine moderne
(2016, 79 buildings of 1946–1975, RPCQ inventory 1313) and that RPCQ building records for Lévis cite
an <em>Inventaire des bâtiments patrimoniaux significatifs de Lévis, mise à jour 2023</em>; whether
either satisfies the statutory obligation is unverified. If a statutory pre-1940 inventory exists it
would supersede the 4,000-building catalogue figure as the authoritative count.

<strong>The Saint-Nicolas citation date, resolved on the register.</strong> Two dates circulate for
the citation of the Site du patrimoine de Saint-Nicolas — 5 November 1987 and 5 October 1987. The
RPCQ statut block, consulted directly, reads <em>Citation, Site patrimonial, Municipalité (Lévis),
1987-10-05</em>, which is 5 October; it also lists two anterior statuses both dated 1987-07-06,
<em>« Avis de motion de citation »</em> and <em>« Citation »</em>. So the register supports 5 October
1987, not 5 November, and adds a third date that neither version of the conflict mentioned. The
5 November figure is recorded here as unsupported rather than simply dropped.

<strong>Two departures from the source material, both flagged.</strong> The Part 8 brief listed the
<em>Autre type : Bâtiment mixte ou à vocation commerciale</em> fiche as a dwelling type to render.
Its seven fields describe shopfronts and signage only — <em>vitrines</em>, <em>portes
commerciales</em>, <em>corniche d'affichage</em>, <em>enseignes</em>, <em>auvents rétractables</em> —
with no dwelling feature named, and it is one of only two of the twenty fiches that carry no
<em>« Nombre à Lévis »</em> count, the other being the accessory-building fiche. It is recorded and
exported here but not rendered as a card. Separately, the brief gave the maison Alphonse-Desjardins
at 6 rue du Mont-Marie; the RPCQ record for the classed building gives 8 rue du Mont-Marie, and that
is the address used.

<strong>No photographs are reproduced.</strong> The City's conditions d'utilisation, read before any
image was handled, state that <em>« Il vous est interdit de copier ou de publier, pour rediffusion à
des tiers ou à des fins commerciales, la moindre partie du contenu »</em>. Every fiche's
photographs — including the before-and-after restoration pairs that are unique to Lévis in this
project — are therefore recorded with their source URL and no file, and each type page carries a
placeholder naming the fiche and the terms. The full per-image list, with URLs, is in
<code>sources/levis/parsed.json</code>.
