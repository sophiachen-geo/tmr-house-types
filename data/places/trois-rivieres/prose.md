## intro

Trois-Rivières was founded on 4 July 1634, when Laviolette raised a fort at the mouth of the Saint-Maurice at Champlain's request, and it is the second oldest city in New France. Menaced by the Iroquois, the post became a walled bourg in 1650; under Pierre Boucher the inhabitants built their houses inside the palisade, and the line of that palisade, together with Jacques Levasseur de Neré's palisade project of 1704, still fixes the boundary of the declared heritage site. Rue des Ursulines runs the length of it, five streets traced from 1650 cross it, and the grid they make is the oldest thing on the ground.

The city that grew outward from that grid was made twice. The first making was industrial: sawmills at the mouth of the Saint-Maurice from 1854, the railway in the 1870s, and then the pulp and paper mills that earned Trois-Rivières the title of <em>capitale mondiale du papier journal</em>. The second was catastrophic. On 22 June 1908 a fire took the commercial centre; the reconstruction that followed was carried out inside four years by, in the City's phrase, <em>une poignée d'architectes et d'entrepreneurs</em>, and the quarter they left has a uniformity that is still legible. That uniformity is not incidental to the heritage claim — it <strong>is</strong> the heritage claim. A centre rebuilt at one moment, in one material, to one profile, is a rarer thing in Québec than a centre of mixed date.

What is easy to get wrong, and what the ministry's own documents make plain, is where the fire fell. The declared site was <em>peu affecté</em>. Inside it stand the Ursulines' conventual chapel of 1714–1716, the Récollets' presbytery of 1742 and their chapel of 1754 — now the anglican église Saint-James — a wooden house of about 1757, and three classed stone houses. Around it, and pressing on it from the south-west, is the rebuilt city: the widths of rues des Casernes and des Ursulines are themselves post-fire works, and thirteen of the site's forty-five buildings, close to a third, went up between 1908 and 1925.

## phase:p1

The lieu was frequented by Indigenous people long before Europeans arrived, and served as a meeting place between them and the fur traders; Jacques Cartier stopped in 1535 and François Gravé Du Pont came about 1599. The post was founded in 1634, a habitation built and a Jesuit mission established; the bourg was fortified in 1650. In 1663 Trois-Rivières was made the seat of a regional <em>gouvernement</em>, the equal of Québec and Montréal — the City's own history page dates this to 1665, one of several small disagreements between the sources noted below. The Ursulines arrived in 1697, moved into their monastery in 1700, and about 1714 raised a chapel and a small hospital. Part of the bourg burned in 1752 and the Ursulines' buildings burned with it; rebuilt the following year, they burned again in 1806. Growth was slow, checked by Montréal's rise as the hinge of the fur trade. Of the domestic architecture of this whole period only about a dozen buildings survive anywhere in the city, and the survivors that matter are concentrated in a few hundred metres of rue des Ursulines.

## phase:p2

After the Conquest the government of Trois-Rivières was abolished, and the town held its standing by other means: seat of a judicial district — the RPCQ's own fiche says 1792 in one section and 1793 in another — and seat of a bishopric in 1852. The Americans occupied the town briefly in 1776. Neoclassicism arrived with the British military engineers and the Scottish and English architects, and it took hold here more slowly and lasted longer than elsewhere: the City's fiche remarks that in Trois-Rivières <em>le néoclassicisme cédera plus lentement qu'ailleurs la place à l'éclectisme</em>. Its domestic form is the georgian house — two and a half storeys, symmetrical, brick, standing alone in a large lot, with cornice returns beginning a pediment. The maison Hertel-De La Fresnière of 1824 belongs to the seam between the two regimes: French in its siting on the ground, its steep roof and its fire-break walls, English in the symmetry of its openings and chimneys and in its carved window surrounds.

## phase:p3

Industrialisation in the second half of the nineteenth century made Trois-Rivières the centre of the Mauricie forest industry. Norcross and Philips built a sawmill at the mouth of the Saint-Maurice in 1854; the cité was constituted in 1857 and divided into four quarters, the old sector falling into the quartier Sainte-Ursule; the rail link came in the 1870s and the port was modernised. A new business bourgeoisie and a growing working population arrived together, and the housing divided accordingly. On the terrasse Turcotte and along rue des Ursulines the merchants built in stone and brick with copper roofs and elaborate trim; in the faubourgs, from the 1880s, the mansard house let workers inhabit the attic, and after it came the Boomtown house — balloon-framed, two storeys, flat or barely sloping to the rear, its ornament gathered in a cornice or a parapet at the top of the front. Trois-Rivières has enough of them, the City's fiche says, <em>pour la faire apparaître comme caractéristique de la ville</em>.

## phase:p4

The fire of 22 June 1908 began in a hay store and ran through the centre; the City's brigade of eight firefighters was pushed back within a quarter of an hour and help was called from Shawinigan, Grand-Mère, Québec and Montréal. How much burned depends entirely on whom you read, and the figures are set out with their attributions in the notes below. What is not in dispute is the shape of the loss — the whole quarter between the river and rues Bonaventure, Champlain and Saint-Georges, with the parish church and the maison des Gouverneurs among the buildings destroyed, several of them French-regime — and the shape of the recovery. By 1912 the centre was busy again. The streets were widened and in places redrawn, the city expanded west and north, and the new fabric was brick: a city still mostly of wood in 1900 was 55 % brick-housed by 1931. The Second Empire houses at 186-190 rue Bonaventure are post-fire; so are the wider carriageways of rues des Casernes and des Ursulines; so is the Appartements Laviolette of 1918, which replaced a building the flames had probably taken.

## phase:p5

The mills came in on the heels of the fire and did more to shape the city than the fire did. The Wabasso Cotton was built in 1907 on rue Saint-Paul; the Wayagamack Pulp and Paper and the St. Lawrence Pulp and Paper followed on the Saint-Maurice, and the Canadian International Paper mill of the 1920s was for a time the largest papermaking plant in the world. New parishes were erected to hold the workers — Saint-Philippe in 1909, Notre-Dame-des-Sept-Allégresses in 1911, Sainte-Cécile in 1912 — and each brought a church, a presbytery and a school with it. Between 1910 and 1940 the population tripled while the built-up territory only doubled. With no urban plan and heavy land speculation, the houses came closer together and then shared walls; large flats of seven and eight rooms were subdivided; stables, henhouses and sheds were converted to dwellings. The Boomtown house of the 1890s and 1900s gave way, from about 1910 and decisively in the 1920s, to the plex: two or three storeys of superposed flats, each with its own door reached by its own stair, under the flat roof that coal tar had made possible.

## phase:p6

After 1945 the bungalow took the ground that vernacular architecture had held, most of it built after the adoption of the 1957 urban plan, in the outer parts of the old quarters. The City's own fiche is unsentimental about it: <em>la production rapide, peu originale et parfois peu résistante, de maisons vendues</em>. The pont Laviolette opened in 1967. A large urban-renewal project gave the city a new hôtel de ville in 1968, a cultural centre, underground parking and the place de l'Hôtel-de-Ville, which won the Vincent Massey award in 1971. On 4 July 2001 the province decreed the merger of Trois-Rivières with Trois-Rivières-Ouest, Pointe-du-Lac, Cap-de-la-Madeleine, Sainte-Marthe-du-Cap and Saint-Louis-de-France; it took effect on 1 January 2002, and the inventory that followed in 2008–2010 covers all six.

## notes

<strong>Two scales, never to be conflated.</strong> There are two bodies of evidence here and they measure different things. The <em>declared site</em> is about 6.7 hectares and, depending which ministerial document you consult, 45 or about 50 buildings: the RPCQ fiche says <em>la cinquantaine de bâtiments</em>, the ministry's own 2014 Plan de conservation counts <em>45 bâtiments principaux</em>. The <em>city-wide inventory</em>, carried out by Patri-Arch in 2008–2010 across all six merged municipalities, covers 3,856 buildings, of which the City reports 250 retained for exceptional and superior heritage value. Those two numbers describe different territories at different resolutions and must never be added, averaged or substituted for one another. A third figure belongs to neither: the inventory's own Introduction et méthodologie says that "environ 200 bâtiments de valeur exceptionnelle ou supérieure ou situés à l'intérieur de l'arrondissement historique" received a written énoncé de valeur patrimoniale — a count of documents produced, not of buildings retained. The type records on this page come from the city-wide courants; each names in its sectors field whether the declared-site texts also describe it inside the perimeter.

<strong>The fire's contested fraction.</strong> The sources disagree, and the disagreement is not trivial, so the figures are given here with their attributions rather than resolved. The <em>Ville de Trois-Rivières</em>, on its "Histoire de Trois-Rivières" page, gives no fraction and no death toll: "Le 22 juin 1908, Trois-Rivières connaît l'incendie le plus dévastateur de son histoire… Près de 800 bâtiments sont rasés, ruinant et jetant des centaines de personnes à la rue." <em>Patri-Arch</em>, in the Synthèse the City itself commissioned, gives a much smaller count and a wider perimeter: "L'incendie du 22 juin 1908 détruit une grande part du centre-ville. Environ 200 bâtiments… sont incendiés dans le périmètre compris entre la terrasse Turcotte et la rue du Fleuve au sud, les rues René et Saint-George à l'ouest, les rues Champlain, Hart et des Prisons au nord et les rues Saint-Louis, Notre-Dame et des Champs à l'est." <em>Radio-Canada</em>, in "Un feu centenaire", gives the quarter and the larger count and denies any casualty: "un incendie qui a ravagé le quart de la ville"; "Quelque 800 bâtiments avaient été rasés par le feu"; "Il n'y avait eu aucun mort ni blessé". <em>Micheline Champoux, for the UQTR</em>, supplies what looks like the reconciliation: "En tout, 800 bâtiments, y compris hangars et entrepôts, ont brûlé", against "Plus de 200 résidences et presque tous les commerces de la ville". On that reading the ~200 and the ~800 are the same fire counted twice — principal buildings against every structure including sheds and warehouses. The figures widely repeated in popular accounts — a third of the city, 800 houses, 180 businesses, damage of $1,500,000, and a single victim named Joseph Métivier, killed by a collapsing wall the day after — could <strong>not</strong> be found on any page of the Ville de Trois-Rivières, nor in any of the City's own commissioned studies, and are recorded here as unattributed rather than repeated as municipal. The French Wikipedia article does report one death, from a wall collapse the day after rather than from the fire itself, which is consistent with Radio-Canada's "aucun mort" only if the two are counting different things.

<strong>The homogeneity is the heritage value.</strong> The reconstruction is not valuable in spite of being uniform. The City's own account rests the claim on the uniformity: "Rebâti par une poignée d'architectes et d'entrepreneurs, le quartier présente une grande homogénéité architecturale qui se constate encore aujourd'hui." Read that alongside the Plan de conservation's numbers for the declared site — 28 of 45 buildings in brick, 18 of them after 1908; 21 of 45 under flat roofs; 39 of 45 at two, two and a half or three storeys — and the same evenness is measurable a few streets away.

<strong>About a dozen French-regime buildings survive city-wide.</strong> The stock is thin and it is concentrated. Inside the declared site, three residential witnesses of the eighteenth century remain: the maison Georges-De Gannes (about 1756), the maison Saint-François (1757) and the manoir de Tonnancour (1795–1797), with the Ursulines' chapel of 1714–1716 and the Récollets' presbytery of 1742 and church of 1754. Outside it stand the manoir Boucher-De Niverville on rue Bonaventure, the moulin seigneurial de Tonnancour on rue Notre-Dame Ouest, the old windmill of 1781 — moved to the UQTR grounds in 1974, and the City's fiche is blunt that moving it cost it part of its meaning — and the Grande Maison des Forges, entirely rebuilt on its foundations in 1988–1989. The 1908 fire took several French-regime buildings with it, including the parish church and the maison des Gouverneurs.

<strong>Disagreements inside single documents, left as found.</strong> The RPCQ fiche dates the judicial district to 1792 in its <em>valeur patrimoniale</em> and to 1793 in its <em>informations historiques</em>. The same fiche dates the seat of regional government to 1663; the City's history page says 1665. The RPCQ dates the Wayagamack to 1911, the Canadian International Paper to 1922 and the St. Lawrence Pulp and Paper to 1923, while the research memo behind this record carried 1913, 1919 and 1907/1910 for a partly different set of mills; the City's 2024 industrial study dates the Wabasso Cotton to 1907. Where a date is quoted in a type record its source is named with it.

<strong>The Boomtown attic vent is a local signature.</strong> Worth stating plainly because it is the one point on which the trifluvien version of a continent-wide form is said to differ: "La section de la façade correspondant à l'espace de l'entretoit est souvent percée de petites fenêtres de forme particulière ou losangée. Ces ouvertures, servant à l'aération ou à l'éclairage du grenier, sont une particularité de l'architecture trifluvienne que l'on retrouve peu ailleurs au Québec."

<strong>What is not encoded.</strong> The 2005 Commission des biens culturels characterisation study by Nathalie Hamel is cited in the RPCQ bibliography but no copy could be reached, so nothing is quoted from it. The PIIA by-law and the demolition by-law are recorded in the instruments table as unverified, with their claimed chapter numbers marked as such. The per-secteur inventory volumes and the 2024 industrial study have been fetched and converted but are mined here only for the passages cited; the per-building data in them is left for a later pass.
