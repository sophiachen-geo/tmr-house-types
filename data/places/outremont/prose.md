## intro

Outremont began as farmland on the far side of the mountain — the <em>côte</em> Sainte-Catherine, whose first concessions date from 1694 and which became a municipality in its own right in 1855. The village of Outremont was detached from it on 23 February 1875, at the urging of Louis Beaubien, with something like three hundred residents; the story that barns had to be counted among the buildings to reach the legal threshold is repeated in every local history, and it gives the measure of the place at its start. For its first thirty-five years it was an anglophone suburb — roughly eighty per cent in the 1880s — and it did not elect a francophone mayor until 1910.

What turned the farms into streets was partly religious. The Clercs de Saint-Viateur arrived in 1887, and five more communities followed by 1920; their houses, colleges and churches fixed the axes along which the land was subdivided, and the église Saint-Viateur of 1910–13 — which carries the first work Guido Nincheri executed in Canada — still anchors the quarter that bears its name. The village became a <em>ville</em> in 1895 and a <em>cité</em> in 1915; parc Outremont was laid out in 1903, parc Pratt in 1931.

The result is a suburb built almost entirely in one generation. Between 1903 and 1920 the north and east filled with semi-detached single-family houses and paired duplexes with independent entrances — two storeys, brick, flat roofs, ground floors raised about a metre — and that short span is precisely why those streets are so homogeneous. The population, as the histories like to put it, doubled every fifteen years. The mountain flank went a different way: Édouard Gohier's Northmount Land Company subdivided it from 1906 into large irregular lots on the steepest ground in the borough, and through the 1920s and 1930s it filled with detached <em>résidences cossues</em> in every historicist idiom available — Beaux-Arts, château, Tudor, Arts and Crafts, neoclassical — and then, without pause, in the new ones: Art Deco, split-level, International Style. The Université de Montréal's expansion truncated what was left of the Northmount plan in 1961.

Outremont returned to <em>ville</em> status in 1970 and became a borough of Montréal on 1 January 2002. Its planning control today rests on two documents that describe territory rather than buildings: a 2020 typo-morphological study that cut the borough into four landscape areas and twenty-nine landscape units, and the 1992 Bisson inventory that graded every building and from which the list of <em>témoins architecturaux significatifs</em> was drawn.

## phase:p1

The côte Sainte-Catherine is conceded from 1694 and farmed for a century and a half — a strip of long lots running up the north-west flank of Mount Royal, with the Montréal side of the mountain between it and the city. The municipality of Côte-Sainte-Catherine is created in 1855. Nothing residential survives from this phase in any quantity; it is the land division itself, and the côte road that still runs across the borough, that carried forward.

## phase:p2

The village of Outremont is detached on 23 February 1875 with about three hundred residents, on Louis Beaubien's initiative. It is anglophone by a large majority, semi-rural, and slow: the first subdivisions go in along the eastern edge nearest the city. The Clercs de Saint-Viateur arrive in 1887 and are followed by five more religious communities to 1920, whose properties become the structuring elements of the street grid. Outremont becomes a <em>ville</em> in 1895.

## phase:p3

The build-out. Between 1903 and 1920 the greater part of the north and east is constructed, in a very short span and by a small number of builders, which is the whole explanation for the homogeneity the 2005 evaluation singles out: rows of semi-detached single-family houses and paired duplexes with independent entrances, two storeys, brick, flat-roofed. Parc Outremont opens in 1903, the église Saint-Viateur is built in 1910–13, the first francophone mayor is elected in 1910, and the town becomes a <em>cité</em> in 1915. Detached single-family houses appear in the same idiom, particularly on the ground that would become parc Pratt.

## phase:p4

Édouard Gohier's Northmount Land Company had subdivided the mountain flank from 1906; through the 1920s and 1930s it fills with the <em>résidences cossues</em> — detached, large, on the steepest and most expensive land in the borough, and drawing on every revival style then in circulation as well as the first modern ones. Parc Pratt is created in 1931. The Théâtre Outremont opens in 1928 to René Charbonneau's design with a Briffa interior, and is today the borough's only classed heritage immovable.

## phase:p5

Apartment buildings arrive — walk-ups in Art Deco and Streamline Moderne dress, and later modern blocks — and the remaining gaps on the flank are filled with International Style, split-level and Art Deco houses that the 2005 evaluation reads as one continuous sequence with the historicist houses beside them. The Université de Montréal's expansion in 1961 cuts off what was left of the Northmount plan, and the borough is essentially complete. Outremont reverts to <em>ville</em> in 1970 and is merged into Montréal as a borough in 2002; the redevelopment of the Outremont rail yard as the MIL campus is the one large change since.

## notes

This page is built on territorial characterisation, not on a type catalogue, and three things about that should be said plainly.

<strong>The landscape units are real but not named here.</strong> The 2020 study by L'Enclume divides the borough into four <em>aires</em> and twenty-nine <em>unités de paysage</em>, and the PIIA annexes carry a table of characteristic elements for each unit. Neither the study nor the annexes could be retrieved for this part, so the six type records below are drawn from the 2005 <em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em> alone and each carries a note saying so. When the unit tables are obtained they will supersede these records rather than supplement them.

<strong>The by-law number is unconfirmed.</strong> Legacy listings give Outremont's PIIA as by-law 1189; the 2020–21 consolidation appears to use an AO-### series, with AO-530 reported. Both are recorded in the instruments list with the uncertainty marked, and neither is asserted here.

<strong>The sector codes are the document's own.</strong> The fourteen sectors below are transcribed from the 2005 evaluation, which numbers them 16.E.1 to 16.E.8 (<em>valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle</em>) and 16.I.1 to 16.I.6 (<em>valeur patrimoniale intéressante</em>). The file as published by the Ville de Montréal is named with a <code>17_</code> prefix, which belongs to the series numbering of the evaluation volumes, not to the sectors; where the two disagree the codes printed inside the document have been followed.

Finally, unlike Gatineau, Outremont has <strong>no cited residential district</strong>. Protection runs through the PIIA and its list of <em>témoins architecturaux significatifs</em>, through the individual classement of the Théâtre Outremont, and through the <em>site patrimonial du Mont-Royal</em>, declared in 2005, which overlaps the borough's southern flank below avenue Maplewood.
