## intro

The Sud-Ouest is where the Montréal plex was made, and it is the one place on this site whose own planning documents explain how, in a sentence you can check. Everything else here follows from a canal. The first canal de Lachine opened in 1825 after four years' digging, was widened in the 1840s, and turned a stretch of Sulpician farmland into the industrial heart of the country: the Montreal &amp; Lachine Railway in 1847, the Victoria Bridge in 1859, and then tanneries, rolling mills, sugar, textiles, malt, rope and shoes. The people who worked in them had to be housed on the spot, quickly, on narrow lots, and by developers subdividing their own land village by village — Griffintown, Sainte-Cunégonde, Saint-Augustin, Saint-Henri, village Turcot, Pointe-Saint-Charles, Victoriatown, Côte-Saint-Paul. Each was constituted as a municipality in its own right in the 1870s and annexed to Montréal between 1887 and 1910, and each carries the rules that were in force when its blocks were cut.

That is why the borough's housing is legible as a sequence rather than a style. Patri-Arch's <em>Étude typomorphologique</em>, made for the borough in 2005 and updated in 2013, reads the territory as five <em>aires de paysage</em> — La Petite-Bourgogne, Pointe-Saint-Charles, Côte-Saint-Paul, Saint-Henri, Griffintown — cut into sixty-five <em>unités de paysage</em>, and reads the buildings as nineteen architectural types on one template. Seven of the nineteen are plexes, and the study defines the whole family in a single line that the borough's design-review by-law repeats: <em>« Ces immeubles possèdent de deux à six logements. »</em> Three more are apartment buildings, and the line between the two families is drawn just as sharply — an <em>immeuble d'appartements</em> has <em>« un hall d’entrée commun qui dessert de six à douze logements »</em>, and once you have a common hall you have left the plex behind.

The exterior stair, which is the thing everyone knows about Montréal, arrives in this document as a consequence rather than a trait. Montréal began regulating residential construction around 1880. The new rules produced a new kind of lotting — ruelles, first simple or in an <em>« I »</em>, then in an <em>« H »</em>, giving regular blocks with four built sides — and they required buildings to stand behind a front setback. The study draws the conclusion in one clause: <em>« l’obligation de l’implantation avec une marge de recul avant amènera la translation de l’escalier d’accès aux logements des étages supérieurs vers l’extérieur, ce qui donnera la caractéristique si typique des bâtiments des quartiers montréalais de cette époque »</em>. Put the stair outside and you keep the floor area inside and stop heating a shared hall. Lotting without a ruelle and building without a setback <em>« ne sera abandonnée que vers la fin des années 1880 »</em>; the triplex with exterior stair <em>« fait son apparition principalement à partir de 1900 »</em>; the duplex with exterior stair and the multiplex follow immediately. Which means this borough contains the before and the after side by side, and its type list — duplex with interior stair, triplex with interior stair, three-storey duplex, then duplex, triplex and multiplex with exterior stairs — is a record of one regulatory change working itself through a housing stock.

The one other thing that makes this record unusual is the shape of the fiche. Section C.4, <em>Traitement de la façade</em>, divides every façade into three horizontal parts and names them: <em>le socle</em>, the low part, generally the foundation but sometimes taking in the ground floor; <em>le corps</em>, the central part; <em>le couronnement</em>, the high part, which absorbs the roof whenever the roof is visible. No other source on this site does this. Twelve of the fifteen residential types are described in all three divisions, and reading down them is the fastest way to see what actually changes between the plexes: the socle goes from rusticated stone to exposed concrete, the corps from two asymmetrical bays to three symmetrical ones, and the couronnement from a moulded wood cornice on consoles to a strip of metal flashing.

## phase:p1

Before the canal, farmland. The territory was first cut in 1663, when Dollier de Casson conceded the seigneurie of the island of Montréal to the Sulpicians, who divided it progressively into <em>côtes</em> whose lots were then granted for settlement. The earliest côtes run perpendicular to the St Lawrence; later ones follow topography and water instead, which is why the côte Saint-Paul, tracing the old shores of Lac Saint-Louis, sits at an angle to the côte des Argoulets, and why the street grid still changes direction where they meet. The roads of this phase are the roads that structured everything after: the Upper Lachine Road (rue Notre-Dame Ouest, continuing as rue Saint-Jacques west of rue du Couvent), the Lower Lachine Road (rue Wellington), the Middle Lachine Road (rue Saint-Patrick), and the links between them — the chemin de la Côte-Saint-Paul and the avenue de l'Église. One building survives from it, and it is one of the oldest in the city: the maison Saint-Gabriel, built in 1698 on the foundations of an older building that had housed the Filles du roi, classed a monument historique and now a museum. There were tanneries at Saint-Henri-des-Tanneries by about 1770, which is why the archaeological overlay 22.AP.5 exists.

## phase:p2

The canal opens in 1825 and the faubourgs begin. The widening of the 1840s is the real trigger, because it made the canal's hydraulic potential usable by industry; the Montreal &amp; Lachine Railway followed in 1847 and the Victoria Bridge in 1859. Residential lotting is done village by village and mostly by individual promoters subdividing their own land. Griffintown is first, on the fief Nazareth: leased by the Sœurs hospitalières to Thomas McCord in 1792 on a ninety-nine-year emphyteutic lease, sold on by his associates to Robert and Mary Griffin in 1796 without his agreement, subdivided, and left largely unbuilt until the canal was dug. Sainte-Cunégonde follows the building of Benjamin Brewster's basin in 1840 and the mills that came with it. Saint-Augustin is subdivided out of Arthur Webster's farm in 1855. The housing of this phase is the housing of a city with no setback rule: the maison villageoise where the old villages survived, and, where density was wanted, the duplex and triplex with the stair inside and the three-storey duplex under a false mansard.

## phase:p3

The villages become municipalities and then parts of Montréal, and municipal power arrives with them: Saint-Gabriel constituted 1874 and annexed 1887; Sainte-Cunégonde a parish in 1875, a village in 1876, annexed 1906; Saint-Henri constituted 1875, annexed 1905; Côte-Saint-Paul constituted 1875, with Émard detaching in 1878 and constituted in 1902, both annexed 1910. What municipal power buys is public works, and the most visible of them are open spaces: parc Saint-Gabriel in 1874, place Saint-Henri about 1880, squares Alma and Saint-Patrick in 1880, square Iberville in 1886, square Saint-Henri in 1892, square Gallery in 1898. With them come the aqueduct, post offices, fire stations, town halls, public baths and markets. This is also the phase in which standardised housing is born, and in which the building rules that produce the plex come into force. By its end almost the whole borough is lotted and built, with free land left mainly in Côte-Saint-Paul and Ville-Émard.

## phase:p4

The exterior stair. This is not a heading in the study — it is the sub-period the study describes inside the third, and it is given a phase of its own here because half the borough's residential types belong to it and none of them exists before it. The regulatory sequence is the point. Setback requirements push the stair out of the building; the ruelle in an <em>« H »</em> gives the block four built sides and a service lane; the triplex with exterior stair appears from 1900 and becomes the base type of that fabric; the duplex with exterior stair follows in the parts of the borough that needed less density, and the multiplex — three storeys, two dwellings a floor, one shared access at the top — follows on wider lots from the First World War. Eleven of the sixty-five landscape units name the triplex with exterior stair as their principal type — behind only the two interior-stair plexes it displaced, at thirteen and twelve, which is the arithmetic of a form that arrived fifty years later and still caught them. The raised duplex, which arrives at the very end of the period with a garage under it, is the first sign of what comes next.

## phase:p5

After 1945 the borough comes apart and is put back together. Trucking and the St Lawrence Seaway destroy the canal's reason to exist; navigation on it stops in 1970. Firms close or move, manufacturing declines, and the population falls from 94,833 in 1971 to 66,474 in 2001. The Ville intervenes on the fabric itself, and the interventions are what most of this phase's housing records. The Ville-Marie, Décarie and Bonaventure expressways take out residential blocks. Victoriatown, at the foot of the Victoria Bridge, is levelled outright for the Bonaventure. Griffintown is emptied by a wave of municipal expropriations and then cut off from downtown by the Bonaventure and the rail viaduct into Central Station. In Sainte-Cunégonde the work is done block by block, so the street grid survives but the two sides of a street can now carry different types — which is a fair description of what typological analysis is for. The community networks that formed to fight the renewal programmes turn into rehabilitation strategies, and the recognition of the canal de Lachine as a national historic site turns its banks into a recreational corridor; it reopened to pleasure craft in 2002, and the mills along it have been converted to housing. The types of this phase are the veterans' house from the 1940s, the town house from the 1960s and especially the 1980s, and the two apartment forms — the conciergerie and the lift-served immeuble d'appartements — which the study describes plainly as <em>bâtiments de substitution</em>.

## notes

<strong>The boundary rule.</strong> The sharpest statement of typological thinking as regulation anywhere in this project is in the Sud-Ouest's design-review by-law, in the <em>Nouveaux bâtiments</em> fascicule, under <em>Stratégies d'insertion relativement aux ensembles typologiques</em>. The general objective is already typological — <em>« Le nouveau bâtiment doit s’inspirer des caractéristiques typomorphologiques de l’unité de paysage dans lequel il se trouve et contribuer à l’évolution de son milieu d’insertion »</em> — and a project inside a continuous ensemble must answer that ensemble's gauges, its horizontal and vertical divisions, its materials, openings, setbacks, projections and rear courettes. But then the by-law asks what happens at the seam, and answers it: <em>« Si le site d’un bâtiment projeté est à la limite de deux ensembles typologiques différents (par exemple des triplex avec marge de recul avant et des duplex de la première période de développement, sans marge de recul ni escalier), le nouveau bâtiment doit choisir clairement une des deux typologies et reprendre l’ensemble de ses caractéristiques d’implantation tout en s’inspirant de ses caractéristiques architecturales. »</em> Choose one, and take all of its siting characteristics — not a compromise, not an average, not a transition. The example it picks is exactly the before-and-after this borough is made of: the triplex with a setback on one side of the line, the pre-1880 duplex with no setback and no stair on the other. And where the fabric is genuinely heterogeneous, the rule inverts: the new building must <em>reduce</em> the heterogeneity by drawing on the borough's more traditional types. It is the only place on this site where a municipality writes down what a typology is <em>for</em>.

<strong>The code is 22, and the file is 12.</strong> The Évaluation du patrimoine urbain series numbers its files and its arrondissements on two different sequences, and they collide. This borough's cahier is file <code>12_evaluation_patrimoine_sud.pdf</code>, and every one of the 540 sector codes printed inside it begins 22. Meanwhile file 22 in the same series is Saint-Laurent. A code inferred from a filename would be wrong here in both directions, which is why the code on this page was read from inside the document, by extracting every string matching the series' own grammar and counting the prefixes: 540 of 540 read 22.

<strong>Five code letters, not four.</strong> The methodology paragraph the series repeats in every cahier explains four — E for exceptional sectors, I for interesting ones, N for industrial sectors of interest, U for urban sectors of interest. The Sud-Ouest cahier prints a fifth: AP, <em>secteur d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel</em>, fourteen of them, in a section of their own that is about the ground rather than the buildings. They are an overlay, not a grade, and they are rendered here as one. Sector 22.AP.5 is encoded as the example; the borough's tallies are 15 E, 25 I, 2 N, 1 U and 14 AP.

<strong>Sixty-five units from fifty-nine names.</strong> The study's synthesis report names a landscape unit only when it lists that unit among some type's <em>lieux d'occurrence</em>, so its own pages yield 59 named units, not 65. The missing six are recoverable rather than lost: the numbering is dense inside each aire, and the highest number reached in each — 1.5, 2.14, 3.20, 4.17, 5.9 — sums to exactly 65, which is the figure the PIIA by-law states independently. The table on this page therefore carries all sixty-five rows, with 2.13, 3.18, 5.4, 5.5, 5.6 and 5.8 present as numbers without names. Four of those six are in Griffintown, which is the aire the postwar expropriations emptied.

<strong>Where the study disagrees with itself.</strong> Eight units carry two spellings of their name across the study's own cross-reference lists, and the disagreements are kept rather than resolved. Most are typographic (<em>Parc Marguertie-Bourgeoys</em> for <em>Parc Marguerite-Bourgeoys</em>; <em>Rue Notre-Dame Ouest 2</em> where 4.2 is elsewhere <em>Rue Notre-Dame Ouest 3</em>). One is substantive: unit 3.2 appears as <em>Avenue de l'Église Ouest</em> on two fiches and as <em>Rue De Roberval</em> on a third, and there is no way to tell from the synthesis which is right. The unit fiches would settle it; they were not retrieved.

<strong>Two names for the same house.</strong> The 2013 study calls type 1.1 the <em>maison villageoise</em>. The PIIA by-law's own Encadré 2, summarising the same seven typologies for applicants, calls the same thing the <em>maison « de faubourg »</em>. Both documents belong to the same borough and the second sends readers to the first. The type card keeps the study's name and records the by-law's.

<strong>Two counts and no more.</strong> The study puts a number on exactly two of its nineteen types: 38 maisons villageoises, <em>« ce qui lui donne une valeur de rareté »</em>, and 352 maisons « boomtown », <em>« ce qui en fait une typologie relativement fréquente »</em>. Both are carried as <code>count_in_place</code>. There is no count for any plex, which is worth stating plainly on the page where the plex is the subject: the density of the type is recorded qualitatively, through how many of the sixty-five units name it as principal or secondary, and that is what the unit table's columns show.

<strong>What is not here.</strong> The twenty-five 22.I.n secteurs de valeur patrimoniale intéressante, the two 22.N.n industrial sectors and the single 22.U.1 urban sector are counted but not encoded; only the E set and one AP sector are. The sixty-five individual unit fiches — with their limits, blocks, parcel dimensions and lists of <em>immeubles d'intérêt patrimonial</em> — are separate documents held at the borough office and were not retrieved. And the building-scale layer, the <code>TYPOLOGIE_SPECIFIQUE</code> field of the Ville's open data, would let every one of these fifteen types be counted address by address; it was not joined in this pass.
