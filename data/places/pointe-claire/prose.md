## intro

The pointe Claire is a flat spit of land running south into Lac Saint-Louis, with a small presqu'île
hanging off it on a neck of ground. Everything institutional this parish ever built stands on it. The
Sulpicians, seigneurs of the island since 1663, reserved the point in 1698 to put up a windmill, and
the mill went up in 1709–1710 — a fieldstone <em>moulin-tour</em> with two opposed doors, a conical
roof and a tail to turn it into the wind, built by the carpenters Léonard and Charles Paillé and the
master mason Jean Mars. A presbytère-chapelle came in 1705 and the first church in 1713. Around 1729,
with Franco-Iroquois relations deteriorating, a stockade of stakes went up around church and
presbytery and the mill, loopholed, served as the redoubt. What stands today is the fourth church, of
1882–1885, by Victor Bourgeau and his associate Alcibiade Leprohon; the convent of 1867–1868 by
Henri-Maurice Perrault; the presbytery of 1913 by Théodose Daoust, built on the masonry body of its
1848 predecessor; and the école Marguerite-Bourgeoys, whose oldest wing dates from 1928.

On 2 April 2013 the city cited the whole of it — about 2.7 hectares — as a <em>site patrimonial</em>
under by-law PC-2808, and the protection it enacted is not cosmetic. It covers the ground as well as
the buildings: <em>« La protection s'applique aux terrains ainsi qu'à l'enveloppe extérieure des
constructions qui s'y élèvent. »</em> Subdivision, new building, alteration, repair, signage, street
furniture, landscaping, excavation and the felling of a mature tree all need forty-five days' notice
and may be made subject to conditions; demolition needs council authorisation; every excavation needs
archaeological supervision; and three named public artworks must stay where they are.

<strong>Hold that beside the Mille carré doré.</strong> Two point seven hectares of parish
infrastructure on a point in the West Island are formally designated, with the ground included, by a
by-law with a number and a date. The <a href="/places/ville-marie/">Mille carré doré</a> — the
richest concentration of domestic architecture ever built on the island of Montréal — has no ensemble
protection at all. Designation in Québec tracks legibility and local politics, not architectural
quality, and this page is one half of the demonstration.

Behind the point the village itself runs back along the chemin du Bord-du-Lac in an L, and the city
governs it through something unusual: a <em>Code villageois</em>, a form-based code that replaces
zoning, subdivision, PIIA and conditional uses for the village sector with one document organised
around ten <em>unités paysagères</em>. It claims, in its own preamble, to be the first of its kind in
Québec. Its third chapter is the only text anywhere in Pointe-Claire that names house types, and it
names three: the traditional Québec architecture of the oldest sectors, the Boomtown, and the Cubique.

Beyond the village the ground was subdivided in a chain of garden suburbs laid out parallel to the
shore road: Cedar Park from 1893, the Bowling Green by Frederick G. Todd in 1905 for the Canadian
Nursery Company, Upper Bowling Green and Killarney Gardens after 1912, and the houses on the avenue du
Golf, built by the founding members of the Beaconsfield Golf Club under a design committee and a
minimum spend. Then the resorts around the baie de Valois; then, after 1947 and north of the railway,
a veterans' sector on the Wartime Housing Limited model. One document describes all of that and
nothing else does — the Ville de Montréal's 2005 <em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em>, made while
Pointe-Claire was a borough and left without force when it demerged.

## phase:p1

In 1698 the priests of the Séminaire de Saint-Sulpice, seigneurs of the island of Montréal since 1663,
reserve a piece of the ground called the pointe Claire for a windmill, which the seigneurial regime
obliges them to build for their censitaires. The mill goes up in 1709–1710 and is leased to millers
who work, maintain and repair it against the annual dues. Settlement follows the mill: a
presbytère-chapelle in 1705, the first church in 1713, all at the end of the point. Around 1729 a
stockade is thrown round church and presbytery and the mill becomes the redoubt; the same defensive
use is recorded at Senneville and Châteauguay, on the same lake. Over the next three decades a handful
of emplacements conceded behind the first cemetery form the embryo of a bourg.

## phase:p2

The bourg becomes real a few years after the Conquest. The village core is surveyed for the first time
in 1756, and in 1765 the Sulpicians concede a good part of its parcels. With the core formed, the
Sœurs de la Congrégation de Notre-Dame build a convent behind the cemetery in 1787, and a boys' school
opens on what is now avenue Sainte-Anne towards the end of the 1810s. Inland, on the côte Saint-Jean,
Hyacinthe Jamme-dit-Carrière acquires in 1769 a farm conceded in 1722 and builds in fieldstone
sometime before 1791 — the house that is now, classed since 1964, one of the rare late-18th-century
farmhouses left inside the lands of Pointe-Claire. The presbytery is rebuilt in masonry in 1848.

## phase:p3

In 1867 the Fabrique wants a bigger church and a bigger cemetery and has no room, so it takes the
ground behind the church where the convent stands and gives the sisters the <em>pointe du Moulin</em>
just south in exchange. Their new convent, to plans by the architect and surveyor Henri-Maurice
Perrault, is finished in 1868. The unfinished new church burns in 1881; a fourth, monumental,
néogothique church is built between 1882 and 1885 to plans by Victor Bourgeau and Alcibiade Leprohon,
and the new cemetery behind it swallows the rue du Couvent. At that point the village has about a
hundred houses — three times as many as a century before — and about five hundred inhabitants, and it
forms an L with the chemin du Bord-du-Lac as its main street, lined with hotels and shops. In 1896 the
Fabrique buys the last property on the south of the point, an inn, and turns it into a school. The
mill falls out of use between 1880 and 1885, loses its conical roof to a terrace carrying a wind pump
for the convent's private aqueduct, and becomes an observatory. A fire — dated 1900 twice and 1901
once in the Code villageois — takes out much of the village, and the two styles that rebuild it are
the Boomtown and the Cubique. On the avenue du Golf, meanwhile, a country suburb of Shingle, Arts and
Crafts and Craftsman houses goes up on very large treed lots in a single short campaign.

## phase:p4

The Frères des Écoles chrétiennes replace the Frères de Saint-Joseph and move in 1928 into a new
building — the south-west section of today's école Marguerite-Bourgeoys — attached to the old inn. A
further section is built on rue Sainte-Anne in 1954 and joined to it, and another annexe to the east
in 1957 on the site of the old inn. The cemetery, outgrown, is relocated in 1946, and the lawn behind
the church still marks where it was. The mill's restoration begins in 1954, its conical roof is put
back in 1962 and its sails in 1967; it is classed an immeuble patrimonial on 21 March 1983. Between
these dates the rest of the territory suburbanises: the Code villageois records bungalows of one
storey on avenue Lanthier, and the shopping and industrial belt along the autoroute is built. In 2002
Pointe-Claire is merged into the Ville de Montréal.

## phase:p5

Pointe-Claire demerges. The referendums of 20 June 2004 and Bill 9 reconstitute it as a city on 1
January 2006, and it uses the municipal power that comes back with reconstitution: a notice of motion
on 3 December 2012, and on 2 April 2013 the adoption of by-law PC-2808 citing the institutional core
as a site patrimonial. In the decade after, the city rewrites its urbanism instruments around the
village: the PIIA by-law PC-2787 grows a chapter listing some three hundred buildings of heritage
interest by civic address and another making the chemin du Bord-du-Lac a protected panoramic route,
and by-law PC-2880, the Code villageois, replaces the zoning of the village sector with a form-based
code of ten landscape units, amended three times between November 2024 and October 2025.

## notes

<strong>The by-law number, which Part 11 listed as outstanding, is PC-2808.</strong> It is
« Règlement sur la citation du noyau institutionnel de Pointe-Claire comme site patrimonial », carried
by resolution 2013-123 at the ordinary sitting of the council of Tuesday 2 April 2013 at 19h30, moved
by conseiller Geller and seconded by conseiller Smith, and signed by mayor Bill McMurchie and the
greffier Jean-Denis Jacob. It is listed on the city's own by-law index and the full text is published.
Two details are worth recording. First, the title cites the <em>noyau institutionnel</em>, not the
pointe: the RPCQ carries both names, and the by-law's annexed <em>énoncé d'importance</em> calls the
place « le site du patrimoine du Noyau-Institutionnel-de-Pointe-Claire » throughout. Second, the
by-law's cover carries a line that reads oddly beside the adoption date — <em>« En vigueur le 12
décembre 2012 / Publication le 10 avril 2013 »</em> — so the instrument is in force from a date four
months before the council adopted it, nine days after the notice of motion of 3 December 2012. That is
the same shape as the Vieux-Terrebonne declaration on this site, where protection also runs from the
ministerial notice rather than from the decree that concludes the process; but this site has not
retrieved the provision that produces the 12 December date, and does not assert one.

<strong>Where the RPCQ fiche garbles its own source.</strong> The site fiche says of the mill:
<em>« Après une restauration dans les années 1960, deux ailes sont ajoutées à l'école, et le toit
conique est rétabli. »</em> Two sails added to the <em>school</em>, in a sentence that is otherwise
about the mill — and two, when the mill has four. The building-level fiche for the mill, id 93473,
gives the sequence cleanly: <em>« Sa restauration est amorcée en 1954. Le toit conique est rétabli en
1962 et les ailes sont ajoutées en 1967. »</em> Four sails, on a propeller at the centre of a gable at
the base of the roof. This page follows the building fiche. The same pair of fiches also disagrees
about the mill's date: the site fiche and by-law PC-2808 both say <em>1709-1710</em>, the building
fiche says <em>construit en 1709</em> three times. Both are published here; neither is corrected.

<strong>The Code villageois cannot decide what year the village burned — but another document
can.</strong> Chapter 3 of the Code says the traditional Québec architecture recalls the traces of the
village's establishment <em>« jusqu'à l'incendie de 1901 »</em>, and then, four lines later on the
same page, <em>« Après l'incendie de 1900, deux styles principaux créent une certaine homogénéité
»</em>. The description of unité paysagère UP3 says that unit was <em>« Épargnée par la déflagration
de 1900 »</em>. Two occurrences of 1900 against one of 1901, in one by-law, about one fire. The Ville
de Montréal's 2005 cahier settles it independently and adds a detail: <em>« En mai 1900, un feu
prenant son origine dans une boutique de ferblanterie détruit une partie du noyau villageois. Malgré
tout, Pointe-Claire se reconstruit en conservant la même trame de rues. »</em> May 1900, from a
tinsmith's shop, and the street plan kept. This site prints 1900 and records the Code's 1901 as an
error in it.

<strong>Three documents, three geometries, and a fourth layer that has no map at all.</strong> This
page carries thirty-three sector rows because Pointe-Claire has been surveyed three times by three
bodies for three purposes, and none of the three covers the same ground. The 2013 citation covers 2.7
hectares and is the only one with statutory force under the <em>Loi sur le patrimoine culturel</em>.
The Code villageois of 2024–2025 covers the village sector in ten landscape units and is a zoning
instrument, not a heritage one. The Ville de Montréal's 2005 <em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em>
covers the whole municipal territory in twenty-two sectors and lost whatever force it had when
Pointe-Claire demerged in 2006 — and it is the only document that describes Cedar Park, the Bowling
Green, the Beaconsfield golf-club houses, the Valois bay resorts or the postwar quarters at all. On
top of those three sits chapter 13 of the PIIA by-law, which protects roughly three hundred buildings
one address at a time, with no sector boundary anywhere in it.

<strong>The 2005 cahier gives the plainest statement in this whole part of how a place gets its
looks.</strong> Of the houses on the avenue du Golf it says: most were built by the founding members
of the Beaconsfield Golf Club, <em>« Leur design devait être approuvé par un comité formé par le Club
et leur coût devait être supérieur à un minimum prédéterminé. Ces restrictions ont contribué à créer
un ensemble harmonieux de paysage naturel et bâti. »</em> A design committee and a minimum spend, run
by a private club in 1904, produced the coherence that a heritage instrument would later be asked to
protect. The Bowling Green next door is the same story in another key: laid out in 1905 by Frederick
G. Todd, at the start of his career and trained by the Olmsted firm, for the Canadian Nursery Company,
which subdivided its own nursery ground when the nursery stopped paying.

<strong>Three things the two Pointe-Claire sources disagree about, and one the brief gets wrong.
</strong> The <em>mill's date</em>: the RPCQ site fiche and by-law PC-2808 say 1709-1710, the RPCQ
building fiche says 1709 three times, the 2005 cahier says 1710 in its sector text and 1709-1710 in
its overview. The <em>church's dates</em>: 1882-1885 on the RPCQ fiche and in the by-law, 1881-1885 in
the cahier, which also calls the present church a replica of a first Bourgeau church begun in 1868 and
burnt in 1881 — where the RPCQ dates the Fabrique's decision to 1867 and says only that the unfinished
church burned in 1881. And the <em>mill's ownership</em>, which is where the brief for this part is
wrong: it describes the mill as owned by the Sulpicians. The RPCQ says the Sulpicians built it in 1709
and leased it to millers, and says nothing about who owns it now; the 2005 cahier says the
Congrégation de Notre-Dame acquired it — in 1866 according to its sector text, in 1845 according to
its own historique twenty pages earlier. Three published dates for one transfer of ownership, and no
current owner asserted anywhere on this page. The cahier also calls the mill <em>« classé bien
archéologique en 1983 »</em>; the RPCQ statut block says <em>Classement, Immeuble patrimonial</em>,
21 March 1983, which is a different category, and the RPCQ is followed here.

<strong>A form-based code is not a heritage instrument, and its style rows are prescriptions.</strong>
The ten unités paysagères below come from by-law PC-2880, whose purpose is to determine in advance
« le style et les caractéristiques du développement immobilier ». Its tables therefore say what
belongs in each unit as much as what stands there, and the two can differ visibly: unit UP7 is
described as being made of one-storey bungalows and its table prescribes contemporary blocks of three
to six storeys. Read the description and the table together, and the direction of travel is legible in
a way that a conventional zoning by-law would hide. The building-by-building heritage protection in
the village comes from somewhere else — chapter 13 of the PIIA by-law PC-2787, which lists roughly
three hundred addresses and applies to them wherever they are.

<strong>What Pointe-Claire contributes to the shared West Island type records.</strong> Four of the
five shared records touch this place, and the evidence for each is different in kind. The
<em>maison de tradition québécoise</em> is chapter 3 of the Code villageois, verbatim: a one-storey
carré under a pitched roof, present in the oldest sectors, "rappelle les traces de l'établissement du
village". The <em>maison de pierre du Régime français</em> is a single classed building, the maison
Hyacinthe-Jamme-Dit-Carrière of 1769–1791, whose RPCQ fiche gives a full five-column description. The
<em>bungalow d'après-guerre</em> is named in the Code's description of unit UP7. And the
<em>villa anglaise de villégiature</em> is unit UP9, the avenue du Golf nord, which Part 11 did not
list Pointe-Claire under and which the source supports better than almost anything else in this part:
the Code attributes the sector explicitly to the British Arts and Crafts movement reaching Québec at
the start of the 20th century "dans des déclinaisons issues des adaptations stylistiques américaines
ayant donné naissance aux maisons de style Shingle et Craftsman", on lots the table calls "très larges
et profonds" at a coverage of about 20 per cent behind a "très profonde" front margin. Its two own
type records — the Boomtown and the Cubique — are the two styles chapter 3 describes that no shared
record covers.

<strong>What is not claimed here.</strong> No date, architect or description is asserted for any of
the roughly three hundred addresses on the PIIA heritage list beyond the names the list itself prints;
it gives addresses and names only. The individual RPCQ records for the church, the convent, the
presbytery, the school and the mission cross were not retrieved, so everything said about them comes
from the site fiche and from by-law PC-2808. And no free photograph identified as a Pointe-Claire
Cubique was found, so that card carries a placeholder rather than a lookalike from somewhere else.
