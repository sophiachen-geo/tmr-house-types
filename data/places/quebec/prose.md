## intro

Québec City is the only place on this site whose types were named before we came looking. The
Ville de Québec publishes a *thésaurus des styles architecturaux* inside its Répertoire du
patrimoine bâti, and that thesaurus — not our reading of a by-law, not a consultant's
typomorphological study — is the spine of this page. Every card below is one of its nodes, carries
the node's own id as a `tid` chip linking back to the City's fiche, and reproduces the fiche's
"Éléments caractéristiques" list verbatim beneath the English translation.

The thesaurus sorts the whole built stock into nine currents. **Influences françaises**
(1608–1800) covers what the first colonists built from French models adapted to the climate and the
materials of Nouvelle-France — the one-storey rural house of stacked timber under an eaveless hipped
roof, and the stone house of the walled town whose party walls climb past the roof as fire-breaks.
**Influences britanniques** (1790–1880) arrives with the new administration and its wish to be seen:
Palladian symmetry, the neoclassical, and the narrow deep London terrace with its door at one end.
**Milieu québécois** (1770–1900) is what happened when the two traditions met and settled — the
Franco-Québécois transition house, then the néoclassique québécois with its bell-cast eaves over a
front gallery, and its stripped-down copy, the maison de faubourg. **Influence des styles
historiques** (1830–1930) is the Victorian revival machine: neo-Gothic villas, Second Empire and its
vernacular mansard, neo-Queen Anne, neo-Tudor, neo-Georgian. **Influences américaines** (1875–1950)
is the industrial vernacular that actually built modern Québec — Boomtown, the flat-roofed faubourg
house, the plex, the cubique, the immeuble à logements. **Influences marginales** (1910–1960) is the
reaction against all of that, Arts and Crafts and régionalisme. **Influences traditionnelles et
modernes** (1900–1950) holds the Cape Cod and the Wartime Housing programme, and the camp and chalet.
**Influences modernes** (1940–1980) is the bungalow, the Prairie house and the International Style.
**Influences contemporaines** (1970– ) is everything since, and almost none of it residential.

What makes the housing history legible is that the city burned, repeatedly, and wrote the results
into its by-laws. The maison de faubourg is the clearest case: the thesaurus's own bullet list
describes it twice over, once as it was built and once as the fire regulations left it — boards
replaced by brick or roughcast, cedar shingle replaced by tin, and the eave taken away altogether.
That is a type record doing the work of a legal history.

Québec is also where several of the canonical forms this site uses elsewhere were first named. The
mansard house, the cubique and the plex all appear here as thesaurus nodes with fiches of their own,
and the cross-links on each card run out to their counterparts in Saint-Lambert, Gatineau, Outremont
and the Plateau. The words do not always mean the same thing in each place, which is the subject of
note 6 below.

## phase:p1

1608 to 1760, the French regime. Two house types and one style current: the rural house of
pièce-sur-pièce timber or rubble stone, one level, no cellar, a hipped roof pulled tight to the wall
with no eaves and an attic used for grain rather than people; and the urban house of the walled town,
one or two levels over a vaulted cellar in its wealthier version, with the party walls carried up
past the roofline as fire-breaks and the chimneys buried inside them. That second detail is already a
response to fire: the Basse-Ville was largely destroyed on 4 August 1682, and in the early 18th
century the intendants' edicts and ordinances normalised how the house was to be built.

## phase:p2

1760 to 1840. The Conquest changed the rulers before it changed the buildings — the tradesmen and
their French habits stayed, and the house evolved only at the turn of the 19th century, when English
immigration brought other ways of building. The Franco-Québécois transition house is the record of
that decade or two: French footing, steep roof and massive chimney stacks, with the British
contribution visible in how regularly the openings are now placed and how much larger the panes have
grown. Alongside it the Palladian arrives from about the end of the 18th century to 1830, and the
London-type terrace house marks the urban fabric mainly from 1815 to 1870.

## phase:p3

1820 to 1880. Two things at once. On the edges of the city, villégiature: the Regency villa and the
Regency cottage go up on large properties along the Grande Allée and the chemins Saint-Louis and
Sainte-Foy, and out through Sillery, Beauport and Sainte-Foy, the cottage under a low four-slope roof
whose eaves run out far enough to cover a veranda all the way round. In the villages and faubourgs,
the synthesis: the maison néoclassique québécoise, the vernacular house of Québécois residential
architecture, a storey and a half under a 45-degree roof with bell-cast eaves over a front gallery
and a summer kitchen trailing off the end. Thomas Baillairgé (1791–1859), who also drew the standard
two-storey brick house the insurers promoted after the fires, is the figure usually credited with
pulling the neoclassical into a Québécois idiom.

## phase:p4

1840 to 1890 — the faubourgs and the great fires, and the one phase where the type records read as
consequences. On **28 May 1845** a fire beginning "à la tannerie d'Osborne L. Richardson située sur
la rue Arago" destroyed "1 630 résidences et 3 000 bâtiments non résidentiels", left about 12,000
homeless and killed roughly 50. On **28 June 1845** the Saint-Jean fire destroyed nearly 1,300 more
dwellings. On **14 October 1866** *Le Canadien* reported the next day: "2,500 à 3,000 maisons
détruites… Environ 20,000 personnes sur le pavé… St. Sauveur entièrement en cendres." Then **1876**
in the faubourg Saint-Louis, **1881** in the faubourg Saint-Jean, and **1889** in Saint-Sauveur,
reported variously as 600 or as 1,000 houses.

The consequences are visible in the records themselves. Fire by-laws required brick or roughcast
walls and tin roofs *without eaves*; the boulevard Langelier was opened as a fire-break; and the
insurers promoted a standard two-storey brick house designed by Thomas Baillairgé. The maison de
faubourg fiche states the change in its own bullets — "suite à la règlementation pour lutter contre
les incendies" — twice, once for the walls and once for the roof. The mansard house of the same
decades did the other half of the work, densifying the faubourgs by turning a two-slope roof into a
full inhabited storey.

## phase:p5

1875 to 1930, the industrial vernacular, and the phase that built most of the housing standing
today. The balloon frame arrives from the United States and Boomtown with it — a simple plan, cheap
cladding, and a roof that slopes gently back or lies flat, so that the whole architectural argument
moves up to the cornice and the parapet. The flat-roofed faubourg house replaces the gables of
Saint-Roch, Saint-Sauveur and Saint-Jean-Baptiste, filling its lot from party wall to party wall with
a carriage arch punched through it. From about 1910 the plex supplants Boomtown in Montcalm and
Limoilou, stacking two, three or four dwellings with independent entries reached by stairs that at
Québec may be interior as readily as exterior. The cubique, devised by Frank Kidder in 1891 and sold
through catalogues, and the industrial-vernacular cottage fill the quarters and the near suburbs.
Above them all the revival styles run on — neo-Queen Anne in vogue between 1880 and 1915 with the
anglophone community, neo-Tudor between 1890 and 1920, neo-Renaissance between 1850 and 1920.

## phase:p6

1920 to 1970. The immeuble à logements takes hold from 1930 in Montcalm and Saint-Sacrement, denser
than the plex: a square block, a symmetrical tripartite front, one monumental door and a central
stairwell serving four, six, eight or more flats behind a column of glass block. Between the wars the
Dutch Colonial Revival house arrives through the sales catalogues, concentrated in Sillery, known by
its bell-shaped gambrel roof. Modernism lands early here for a house — the first International Style
houses appear at Québec from 1935, at Sillery, by Robert Blatter and Charles A. Jean. Then the war:
Wartime Housing Limited, created in 1942, put up prefabricated single-family houses in tightly
subdivided sectors between 1942 and 1945, and carried on as CMHC after 1946. And then the suburban
belt and the bungalow, which the City files as a déclinaison of Prairie, and which by the 1950s was a
consumer product manufactured in series.

Meanwhile the protective apparatus arrives: **1963/1964** Vieux-Québec declared, **1964** Sillery and
Beauport, **1965** Charlesbourg, **1985** Beauport enlarged and Vieux-Québec inscribed on the UNESCO
World Heritage List on 3 December — the first urban agglomeration north of Mexico to be so listed.
The municipal merger follows in **2002**.

## notes

1. **The thesaurus is descriptive, not regulatory, and carries no conservation guidance at all.**
Every type record on this page therefore has an empty `conservation` list, and that emptiness is a
finding rather than an omission: there is nothing in the source to put there. The rules that actually
govern work on these houses live in the CUCQ guides d'intervention ("Conserver et mettre en valeur…",
Vieux-Québec 1998, Beauport, Charlesbourg) and in the ministerial plans de conservation, and they
attach by geography — by which declared site or CUCQ sector a building stands in — not by type. Part
7b joins them spatially.

2. **Types you might expect and will not find.** There is no standalone "maison monumentale
néoclassique anglaise"; the phrase belongs to the third-party site *maisons-anciennes.qc.ca*, and the
thesaurus's nearest equivalent is Néo-georgien (tid 410). There is no separate **split-level** page —
it is a variant named inside Bungalow (tid 801). There is no **Italianate** page; it is subsumed
under Éclectisme (tid 403) and Second Empire (tid 401). And tid 204, the Regency parent node that
should sit above the cottage and the villa, returns a 404: the two children exist, the parent does
not.

3. **Where the dates come from.** The thesaurus puts its dates in the prose rather than in a header,
so `phase_confidence` is `verified` only where the fiche itself states a range — "principalement de
1815 à 1870", "en vogue entre 1880 et 1915", "entre 1942-1945" — and `provisional` where the phase
rests on a period band or on the current's own dates instead.

4. **The fire figures differ by source and are given here as ranges.** The 1889 Saint-Sauveur fire is
reported as 600 houses by some sources and 1,000 by others; the October 1866 figure of 2,500 to 3,000
houses is the period press's own range.

5. **The Charlesbourg declaration year (1965) is secondary-source.** It should be verified against
the RPCQ legal field before it is relied on; the sector record carries the caution.

6. **Same-word warnings, for the glossary.** Québec's **maison de faubourg** (tid 303) is not Mount
Royal's "maison faubourienne" and not the Plateau's "maison de faubourg" family — three different
objects under one phrase, now all on this site and cross-linked. Québec's **plex** (tid 503) and
Montréal's differ in stair placement, Québec's fiche allowing interior stairs as readily as exterior
ones, and in whether the ground floor is raised. Québec's **cottage** is neither the OQLF's "any
two-storey detached house" nor Saint-Lambert's King Cottage: here it names two unrelated things, the
Regency villégiature cottage (tid 205) and the industrial-vernacular cottage (tid 505).

7. **Scope and what is recorded but not shown.** The thesaurus covers every building category,
including the religious and institutional. Only residential and mixed residential-commercial types
get cards. The nine currents and the parent nodes below them are recorded as *courants* and render as
entries in the table above rather than as cards; the nineteen style nodes whose fiches describe
churches, banks and office blocks rather than dwellings are recorded as non-residential. Nothing that
was crawled has been discarded — all 53 thesaurus nodes and all nine landing pages are in the data
exports, each saying which of the three it is and why.

8. **No images from the City are reproduced here.** The thesaurus drawings ("Illustration :
Charles-Étienne Brochu, 2022") and the Répertoire photographs are © Ville de Québec, and the City's
own copyright page states that "toute utilisation, reproduction, diffusion, traduction, publication
ou retransmission du contenu, en tout ou en partie, est strictement interdite sans l'autorisation
écrite et préalable de la Ville de Québec", adding that "les photographies illustrant ce site ne
peuvent pas être reproduites". Every photo slot is therefore a placeholder carrying the illustration's
URL and its credit, and each card instead links out to the fiche it was read from and lists the
buildings the City names as exemplars, as text links.
