## intro

Hampstead was incorporated on 19 February 1914, detached from Côte-Saint-Luc by a private bill; its first council met on 7 April 1914 under the founding mayor James Ballie, and the land it governed was held by a single company, Côte St. Luc Realties. It was named for the garden suburb in London and laid out on the same principles: streets that curve so that traffic slows, large single-family lots with room to garden, and no commerce or industry anywhere inside the town. Growth was slow — 53 residents in 1921, about a thousand people and a hundred houses by 1944 — and then the postwar decades filled the plan, taking the town past 6,500 by the early 2000s and to roughly 7,000 today. The Town borrowed Westmount's building standards until it adopted its own in 1923, and set up a Commission consultative d'architecture in 1926. Montréal's 2005 heritage evaluation divides it into three sectors, and its interwar core, Vieux-Hampstead, is a secteur de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle of two-storey stone houses "fortement inspirés des styles anglais aussi présents en grand nombre à Westmount".

## phase:p1

Incorporation and the first lots. Construction begins in 1916 but stays sparse; the town applies building standards borrowed from Westmount, having none of its own.

## phase:p2

Real development from 1920, and the majority of Vieux-Hampstead is standing by 1940: two-storey stone and brick houses in English-revival idioms on the curving streets of the original plan. The Town adopts its own building standards in 1923 and creates a Commission consultative d'architecture in 1926.

## phase:p3

Postwar expansion continues the 1913 street pattern rather than replacing it: single-family houses on large lots, many of the 1940s and later, with less of the old stone concentration. Architect-designed houses of the 1960s and 70s, "maisons qui affichent une grande individualité", form a recognised urban ensemble.

## phase:p4

Rebuilding on the garden-suburb lots. The phenomenon is visible on the ground but is not characterised in any retrievable source, and no demolition or rebuild figures were located.

## notes

Two things this page deliberately does not say. First, **the planner is unattributed**: the plan is credited to the provisional council and to the land company, Côte St. Luc Realties, and no primary source ties Leonard E. Schlemm — who built Westmount's Priests' Farm and is associated with regional garden-suburb work — or Frederick Todd, or any other named designer, to Hampstead's plan. Second, there is no established local type name: nothing in the sources supports a "Hampstead cottage" or any equivalent, so the types here are named for what the sector fiches describe. Protection is discretionary — the PIIA (règlement 775) and the demolition by-law (1009) — not provincial; no part of Hampstead is a site patrimonial or a classified immeuble. The three sectors are the whole of the published characterisation: Montréal's 2005 evaluation covers Hampstead in an arrondissement volume shared with Côte-Saint-Luc and Montréal-Ouest, and no per-type catalogue exists.
