## intro

Hampstead was incorporated on 19 February 1914, detached from Côte-Saint-Luc by a private bill; its first council met on 7 April 1914 under the founding mayor James Ballie. Its founder was Sir Herbert Holt, the engineer who was first president of the Hampstead Land and Construction Company and who, in the Town's own account, played a central role in the initial planning and development. The physical plan itself was drawn during the 1920s by the landscape architect Leonard E. Schlemm. The town was named for the garden suburb in London and laid out on the same principles: streets that curve so that traffic slows, large single-family lots with room to garden, and no commerce or industry anywhere inside the town. Growth was slow — 53 residents in 1921, about a thousand people and a hundred houses by 1944 — and then the postwar decades filled the plan, taking the town past 6,500 by the early 2000s and to roughly 7,000 today. The Town borrowed Westmount's building standards until it adopted its own in 1923, and set up a Commission consultative d'architecture in 1926. Montréal's 2005 heritage evaluation divides it into three sectors, and its interwar core, Vieux-Hampstead, is a secteur de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle of two-storey stone houses "fortement inspirés des styles anglais aussi présents en grand nombre à Westmount".

## phase:p1

Incorporation and the first lots. Construction begins in 1916 but stays sparse; the town applies building standards borrowed from Westmount, having none of its own.

## phase:p2

Real development from 1920 on the plan Leonard E. Schlemm drew during this decade, and the majority of Vieux-Hampstead is standing by 1940: two-storey stone and brick houses in English-revival idioms on the curving streets of that plan. The Town adopts its own building standards in 1923 and creates a Commission consultative d'architecture in 1926.

## phase:p3

Postwar expansion continues the original street pattern rather than replacing it: single-family houses on large lots, many of the 1940s and later, with less of the old stone concentration. Architect-designed houses of the 1960s and 70s, "maisons qui affichent une grande individualité", form a recognised urban ensemble.

## phase:p4

Rebuilding on the garden-suburb lots. The phenomenon is visible on the ground and turns up case by case in council decisions, but the Town publishes no demolition or rebuild figure and no characterisation of the replacement houses.

## notes

<strong>Designer and founder are two different people, and this page keeps them apart.</strong> The plan was drawn during the 1920s by <strong>Leonard E. Schlemm</strong>, landscape architect; <strong>Sir Herbert Holt</strong>, engineer, was the town's founder and the first president of the Hampstead Land and Construction Company, credited by the Town with a central role in the initial planning and development but not with authorship of the physical plan. <strong>Frederick G. Todd is not the designer of Hampstead and must not be credited</strong> — the attribution is rejected here explicitly, because it circulates. The Schlemm attribution rests on two UQAM scholarly histories of Montréal's garden suburbs, one saying the plan was <em>« confié à Leonard E. Schlemm »</em> and the other that the town was founded in 1914 but <em>« dessinée par Leonard E. Schlemm durant les années 1920 »</em>; their full bibliographic details are still to be completed, so the attribution is reported here rather than footnoted, and no document held in this project's source folder confirms it.

<strong>Schlemm links Hampstead to Westmount.</strong> The same landscape architect laid out Priest's Farm in Westmount between 1926 and 1930 — Westmount's sector 12 — which is why the two enclaves read alike on the ground and why this site cross-references them.

Two company names circulate and both are recorded: the <strong>Hampstead Land and Construction Company</strong>, named in the Town's official history, and <strong>Côte St. Luc Realties</strong>, the landholding syndicate cited in the 2005 <em>Évaluation</em>. They are not collapsed into one here.

There is no established local type name: nothing in the sources supports a "Hampstead cottage" or any equivalent, so the types on this page are named for what the sector fiches describe. <strong>No demolition or rebuild statistic is published.</strong> The Town issues individual demolition notices and council decisions — 5851 Ferncroft; 74 Stratford, approved August 2024; 29 Lyncroft, approved February 2025, with the replacement single-family house considered separately; 54 Place Heath — and its fee by-law treats demolition permits one at a time. Those four decisions are recorded here as evidence of practice, not as a series; a count assembled from council records would be this project's figure, not the Town's, and none is published here. Protection is discretionary — the PIIA (règlement 775) and the demolition by-law (1009) — not provincial; no part of Hampstead is a site patrimonial or a classified immeuble. The three sectors are the whole of the published characterisation: Montréal's 2005 evaluation covers Hampstead in an arrondissement volume shared with Côte-Saint-Luc and Montréal-Ouest, and no per-type catalogue exists.
