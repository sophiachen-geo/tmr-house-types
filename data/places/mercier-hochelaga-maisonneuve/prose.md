## intro

Mercier–Hochelaga-Maisonneuve is five annexed municipalities laid end to end along the eastern shore of the island: Longue-Pointe, a French-regime parish of 1722 that became a municipality in 1845; the village of Hochelaga, incorporated 1870 and annexed to Montréal in 1883 because it could not pay for its own sewers; Maisonneuve, created on 27 December 1883 by the Hochelaga landowners who refused to go with it; Beaurivage and Tétraultville, annexed with Longue-Pointe in 1910; and Saint-Jean-de-Dieu, the asylum's own territory, not fully annexed until 1963. The borough itself dates from 1 January 2002. It is one of the largest in Montréal and, with the Lachine canal, the largest concentration of old industrial land in the country.

<strong>Maisonneuve is why this page is here.</strong> It is the third planning exercise at company-town scale on this site, after Arvida and Témiscaming — and it is the one where the client was a town council rather than a corporation. The distinction runs all the way through the results, and this page is largely an argument about it. The two documents underneath the page are the Ville de Montréal's <em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em> of 2005 and the borough's own <em>Guide patrimonial</em>. Neither is a typology; the first characterises sectors and lists individual buildings, the second regulates stairs, balconies, fascias, soffits, doors, windows, cladding and crowns. So the type records here are shorter than on the borough pages built from a Patri-Arch study, and each one says in its <em>profile_note</em> exactly which document each line came from.

## phase:p1

The Sulpicians, seigneurs of the island since 1663, conceded these lands at the end of the seventeenth century, and a chain of forts went up around the shore against Iroquois raids; a wooden redoubt stood at Longue-Pointe by about 1670. A chapel and presbytery followed, and in 1722 Saint-François-d'Assise de la Longue Pointe became one of the few parishes erected on the island under the French regime. In 1825 the parish held 67 houses, occupied by artisans, innkeepers, rentiers, day labourers and, above all, farmers. Longue-Pointe became a municipality in 1845. Its houses were squared-timber boxes sheathed in board, shingle or brick, and a fragment of them still stands.

## phase:p2

Montréal became the metropolis of Canada in the second half of the nineteenth century, and Hochelaga was where the overflow went. Between 1871 and 1881 four large works arrived — the W.C. McDonald tobacco factory, the Abattoirs de l'Est, the V. Hudon cotton mills and the Filature Sainte-Anne, the last two merging in 1905 into Dominion Textile, which then held half the Canadian textile industry. They drew cheap labour off the farms, and, in the cahier's phrase, <em>« les terrains sont lotis sans réglementation particulière »</em>. The workers settled south of the tracks, on rues Moreau, Dézéry and Saint-Germain. On Saint-Germain the Hudon mill built its own workers' housing in 1881 — brick-faced wooden cubes in an unbroken run at the sidewalk edge, two dwellings each with the stair inside — and the Ville's MEM calls that row <em>« le seul exemple encore existant d'habitations érigées au XIX<sup>e</sup> siècle par une compagnie à Montréal »</em>. By 1883 Hochelaga's growth had outrun its ability to pay for sewers, water and street openings, and it asked to be annexed.

## phase:p3

In December 1883 the large proprietors east of avenue Bourbonnière asked for, and got, a municipality of their own. Maisonneuve was proclaimed on 27 December 1883 by the lieutenant-governor, at the initiative of a group of French-Canadian industrialists and businessmen — Alphonse Desjardins, Hector Barsalou, Charles-Théodore Viau, William Bennett, Charles-Henri Létourneux, Raymond Préfontaine — who, the RPCQ says, wanted to make it <em>« la "Westmount des francophones" »</em>. Their method was straightforward: huge cheap lots, tax exemptions for incoming industry, and a site beside the new Sutherland (1891) and Tarte (1900) wharves and the rail lines. It worked. St. Lawrence Sugar came in 1887, Canadian Vickers in 1911; textiles, biscuits, confectionery, foundries, shipbuilding and shoes followed. The tramway arrived in 1892. Population went from about 350 at the founding to 2,000 in 1896 and roughly 18,000 in 1911, eighty per cent francophone and <em>« composée surtout de travailleurs et de locataires »</em>. On the industrial ranking, note that the two sources give two different readings and this page prints both: the RPCQ says that by 1910 Maisonneuve <em>« figure au cinquième rang des villes industrielles du Canada »</em> and that its industry was <em>« principalement axée sur la fabrication de chaussures »</em>; the Ville's cahier says that fifteen years after its creation the municipality <em>« devient la cinquième ville industrielle au Canada et la deuxième au Québec »</em> and that it advertised itself as <em>la Pittsburgh canadienne</em>. <strong>The popular claim that Maisonneuve was the world capital of the shoe industry appears in neither, and is not repeated here.</strong>

Then, in 1910, the elected officials — who were also, as the cahier drily notes, the town's property developers — decided their city should become the garden of Montréal. Under maire Alexandre Michaud and échevin Oscar Dufresne, the municipal engineer <strong>Marius Dufresne (1883–1945)</strong> drew what the RPCQ calls <em>« un plan d'aménagement ambitieux »</em> and, flatly, <em>« le plus ambitieux réalisé au Québec à cette époque »</em>: monumental buildings, parks, urban boulevards, and a remedy for the ills of industrial cities. The inspiration is named — the American civic-embellishment movement <em>City Beautiful</em>, and the Park and Boulevard Movement — and so is its content: Beaux-Arts architecture, wide boulevards, planned vistas, parks, and the functional separation of space. Built: the hôtel de ville (1910–1912, Joseph-Cajetan Dufort), the marché public (1912–1914), the caserne de pompiers no 1, or caserne Letourneux (1914–1915, Marius Dufresne), and the bain et gymnase publics, the bain Morgan (1914–1916). Avenue Morgan, the plan's spine, was inaugurated in 1912 and drawn by the landscape architect <strong>Frederick Gage Todd</strong> — the same hand that laid out Town of Mount Royal for the Canadian Northern Railway in the same decade. Alfred Laliberté made the bronzes. A hippodrome was projected and never built. Marius Dufresne and his brother built themselves the Château Dufresne, 1915–1918, on twenty reinforced-concrete piers.

<strong>Here is the comparison that matters.</strong> Arvida was Alcoa's, Témiscaming was Riordon's and then Canadian International Paper's; in both, one client owned the ground, hired one designer, and built the houses along with everything else — 270 houses in 135 days at Arvida, every dwelling of Témiscaming's lower town by Ross and Macdonald. Maisonneuve had the same ambition at the same scale and the opposite structure. Its client was a municipal corporation spending borrowed public money, and a municipal corporation can build a market, a bath and a boulevard but it cannot build anyone's house. So the plan produced monuments and left the housing to speculative builders, who put up the ordinary Montréal plex. That is the difference in one line, and it is visible on the ground: a Beaux-Arts civic core in cut limestone, and, three streets away, a rangée of three-storey brick and greystone plexes with iron stairs curving up out of the front yards. The city could not pay for the monuments. In the middle of the wartime economic crisis, deep in debt, the Cité de Maisonneuve was annexed to Montréal by provincial statute in 1918, after thirty-five years of autonomy.

## phase:p4

Annexation did not stop the building; it moved it. Development ran east and north through the former Longue-Pointe, Beaurivage and Tétraultville, which had come in as part of Montréal in 1910. Maisonneuve's ideals outlived its treasury: the RPCQ notes that they were <em>« néanmoins perpétués dans les bâtiments institutionnels et les aménagements conçus au cours des années suivantes »</em>, and the clearest instance is parc Morgan itself, laid out in 1939 to Todd's plans on the old Milton Lodge estate the Morgan family had ceded to Maisonneuve in 1908 — twenty-one years after the city that commissioned it had ceased to exist. The old hôtel de ville, meanwhile, housed the Institut du Radium de Montréal from 1926 to 1967; La Bolduc, Mary Travers, was treated there and died there.

## phase:p5

Mercier turned suburban. Around 1950, ensembles of veterans' houses went up north of rue de Rouen on the Wartime Housing Limited model, and the cahier records the mechanism exactly: a wartime emergency type that <em>« fut adopté par la suite par plusieurs promoteurs »</em> selling affordable detached houses. Four bungalow developments followed between about 1951 and 1955 — identical brick houses, one of them with no front door on the street face at all, another laid out on loop streets. Place de Léry, 1951–52, brought semi-detached two-storey houses with glass block, false tile roofs and porticos. And in 1976 the Olympic Park landed on the ground Maisonneuve had reserved for its own grand park.

## phase:p6

Between 1972 and 1975 the widening of rue Notre-Dame into an urban boulevard demolished more than a thousand dwellings, and the works for the abandoned east–west expressway, together with the linear park that replaced it, amputated the old village of Hochelaga and changed parc Dézéry. Rebuilding on the cleared block-heads began in 1987. Then, at the very end of the sequence, the protection arrived — and from an unexpected direction. See the notes below.

## notes

<strong>The arrondissement code is 27, and the filename says 14.</strong> The Ville publishes this borough's cahier as <code>14_evaluation_patrimoine_mercier.pdf</code>, but every sector code printed inside it begins with 27: 27.E.1–5, 27.I.1–6, 27.U.1–5, 27.N.1–2, and 27.AP.1–3 alongside 27.A.1–2 for archaeology. The file-number sequence and the arrondissement-number sequence are two different things and they collide across the series — the Sud-Ouest is code 22 in file 12, while file 22 is Saint-Laurent. A stray <code>14.AP.1</code> does appear on this cahier's archaeological synthesis map; it is a neighbouring borough's label bleeding across the sheet edge, not an MHM sector. The code was read from inside the document, as it must always be.

<strong>Only the five E sectors are encoded.</strong> The cahier's other twenty-one sectors are enumerated in the header of <code>sectors.yaml</code> so the count is not lost, but their fiches are Part 10b work. Two type records on this page therefore cite U-sector and I-sector codes in their notes rather than in their sector fields.

<strong>Maisonneuve is protected by a provincial classement, not a municipal citation — and that is unusual here.</strong> Every other heritage instrument across the five boroughs of this part is municipal: borough zoning by-laws, PIIA regulations, demolition control, cited sites. The <em>Site patrimonial de l'Ancienne-Cité-de-Maisonneuve</em> is not. There is one other provincial area protection touching a Montréal borough on this site — the <em>site patrimonial du Mont-Royal</em>, declared in 2005, which overlaps Outremont's southern flank — but that one is drawn around a mountain, and it caught the houses on its slope incidentally. This one was drawn around a town plan, and it caught exactly what the plan built. It was classed by the Minister of Culture and Communications under the <em>Loi sur le patrimoine culturel</em>, and it carries two dates that look wrong and are not: <strong>Statut: Classement — Date: 2022-03-03, Prise d'effet: 2021-03-30</strong>, following an <em>avis d'intention de classement</em> of 2021-03-29. Protection in Québec bites from the minister's notice of intent, not from the decree that follows it, so the effective date precedes the classement by nearly a year; the same pattern governs Vieux-Terrebonne elsewhere on this site, and the glossary entry for <em>prise d'effet</em> explains it. The classement covers works of 1910 to 1939 — the former hôtel de ville, the former marché public, the bain et gymnase publics, the former caserne de pompiers, avenue Morgan and parc Morgan — together with Alfred Laliberté's two bronze fountain-sculptures, <em>La Fermière</em> and <em>Les Petits baigneurs</em>. Protection applies to building exteriors, to the sculptures, to the urban works and to the grounds. The Château Dufresne, classed on its own in 1976, sits inside the same story but under a separate instrument.

<strong>The province did what the city recommended, seventeen years late and with a stronger tool.</strong> The 2005 cahier's borough-specific recommendations include this one: <em>« de constituer en site du patrimoine le centre-ville de Maisonneuve organisé autour de l'axe de l'avenue Morgan. Ses qualités urbaines certaines et la valeur patrimoniale de plusieurs bâtiments qui le bordent lui confèrent indéniablement une valeur à l'échelle montréalaise. »</em> A <em>site du patrimoine</em> was the municipal instrument of the day. What eventually arrived, in 2021–2022, was a provincial classement instead. The same cahier also warned about what municipal instruments had failed to hold: several buildings selected by the CUM in 1982 had since been demolished — the asile Saint-Benoît-Joseph-Labre, the couvent Saint-Isidore, the Warden King and Son works, the Canadian Spool Cotton, the école Maisonneuve — and it drew the conclusion that this borough's heritage is fragile because it is made of modest things, <em>« composé surtout de bâtiments modestes : écoles, maisons ouvrières, industries »</em>.

<strong>There is no documented Maisonneuve "maison-modèle", and none has been invented here.</strong> The word <em>modèle</em> does occur in the cahier, twice, and neither use is a house type of the cité. Sector 27.E.2 calls the municipality itself <em>« la cité-modèle de Maisonneuve »</em> — a model city. Sector 27.U.1 speaks of <em>« le modèle des maisons de la Wartime Housing Limited »</em> — a federal house type of the 1940s, adopted in Mercier around 1950, thirty years after the cité had ceased to exist. What the Évaluation actually says about Maisonneuve's housing is one clause, and this page quotes it wherever it matters: <em>« les habitations ouvrières de trois étages le long de rues bordées d'arbres »</em>, glossed in the same document's overview as <em>« un patrimoine résidentiel composé de "plex" en pierre ou en brique le long de rues bordées d'arbres »</em>. Three-storey plex and rangée, in other words; the standard Montréal product, and it is encoded as such.

<strong>One correction to the research memo.</strong> The RPCQ does not describe the Château Dufresne as the first reinforced-concrete private residence in Montréal. Its words are <em>« l'une des premières utilisations de ce matériau pour la construction résidentielle à Montréal, voire au Québec »</em> and <em>« l'une des premières résidences à structure en béton armé à Montréal »</em>. One of the first. The superlative is not published on this site. Similarly, the caserne Letourneux's frequently repeated debt to Frank Lloyd Wright's Unity Temple is not in the RPCQ fiche for that building, which gives only the dates 1914–1915 and the architect Marius Dufresne, and it is therefore not asserted here either.

<strong>What regulates the houses today.</strong> Not the classement, which stops at the civic ensemble. Ordinary houses are governed by the borough's zoning by-law 01-275 through two designations it carries directly — <em>secteurs significatifs</em>, where norms and criteria apply to any exterior work visible from the street, and <em>immeubles significatifs</em> — plus twelve PIIA areas, the demolition by-law RCA02-27006, and the lotissement by-law RCA04-27003. The borough's <em>Guide patrimonial</em> is the working manual, and it is unusually explicit about method: where a similar adjacent building still has its original components, <em>« cet immeuble sert de référence pour les interventions »</em>. The neighbour is the standard. That is typological regulation done without a typology.
