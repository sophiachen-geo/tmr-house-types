## intro

Baie-D'Urfé is a shore, a road and a set of farm lots that never became a village. The chemin du Roy — later the Queen's Highway, now the chemin du Lakeshore — runs along the north bank of the lac Saint-Louis, and the Sulpicians conceded the land behind it in narrow strips reaching back from the water. The 2005 heritage evaluation of the merged arrondissement of Beaconsfield–Baie d'Urfé says that this road holds <em>« la plus grande densité de bâtiments d'intérêt patrimonial de l'arrondissement »</em>, and describes what is on it in one clause that is the closest thing this town has to a typology: <em>« d'anciennes maisons de ferme, des maisons bourgeoises et des villas d'été de la fin du XIXe siècle et du début du XXe siècle »</em>, standing well back from a winding road with mature trees and no sidewalks.

The oldest thing here is a farmhouse of about 1700 at 20122 chemin Lakeshore, built by Hubert Rangé-Dit-Laviolette on a lot the Sulpicians had conceded in 1686 and which he bought in 1695. It sits at the back of its lot, a hundred metres from the modern road, because the chemin du Roy used to pass between it and the water — the paving stones are said to survive under the lawn — and its main front faces south to the lake and the îles Sainte-Geneviève for the same reason. It is the only protected building in the town.

Almost everything else is postwar. The town was chartered in 1911 with fewer than two hundred inhabitants, and its own history records that in the 1950s the population increased fivefold. In the 1950s, the 2005 cahier notes, the only drivable streets in the whole municipality were the rues Oxford and Cambridge, the avenue St. Andrew, the chemin du Lakeshore and the rues Morgan and Lakeview — six roads. What has been built since covers the rest, and the heritage argument here is entirely about the shore road.

## phase:p1

The Sulpicians become seigneurs of the island of Montréal in 1663 and begin conceding land at the western end. In 1687 an Iroquois raid kills settlers on the Pointe-à-Caron, the chapel is burned and the population falls back on Montréal; the colonists return after the peace of 1701 and farm. The house that survives from this phase — the only one — is the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette, built about 1700 by Hubert Rangé-Dit-Laviolette (1661 – vers 1727) on the concession he had bought in 1695. The RPCQ reads its oldest part as belonging to the French building tradition: a stone <em>carré</em> barely raised off the ground, an asymmetrical arrangement of openings, an almost square plan of small dimensions, and chimney stacks carried up in the line of the gable walls, which it says are <em>« plus particulièrement associés aux maisons de l'île de Montréal et des régions environnantes »</em>.

## phase:p2

Farming, and then the Anglo-Saxon retrofit. A census of 1781 records a house, a barn and a stable on the Rangé property. Charles Lenoir buys it after 1781 and may have run it as an inn for some fifteen years at the turn of the nineteenth century; it then passes to Gabriel Pilon, who farms it and returns it to a dwelling, and stays in the Pilon family until 1874. Between 1839 and 1874 the Pilons raise the stone walls, slacken the pitch of the roof, and add moulded wooden window casings, overhanging <em>larmiers</em> and a covered gallery on the south side — elements the RPCQ attributes to the Anglo-Saxon architectural tradition introduced after the Conquest, and to the picturesque taste of the period, <em>« une sensibilité nouvelle par rapport à la nature »</em>. In 1874 André Perrier buys the property and gives the central pavilion neo-Gothic attributes and the old south front its three dormers. This is a phase with no village in it: no parish church, no core, only farms on a road.

## phase:p3

The Grand Trunk reaches the West Island in 1855, and a railway station close to the shore opens the lac Saint-Louis to summer people. The cahier describes the pattern: at the end of the nineteenth century the lakeshore is taken up for <em>villégiature</em>, and in summer <em>« les abords du lac sont envahis par une population aisée d'entrepreneurs et de professionnels »</em>; residents then settle permanently and commute by train. Beaconsfield is incorporated in 1910 with fewer than 400 permanent residents; a year later Baie-d'Urfé is incorporated with fewer than 200, its territory detached from Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue. The charter was granted in March 1911 and the first council met on 10 July, with no election: the first mayor, Vivian de Vere Dowker, and the councillors were acclaimed. At that first meeting James Morgan announced that he would give the town a waterfront property for a town hall, a park for all the citizens, and a street that still bears his name; in October 1912 he gave the white-frame farmhouse that was converted into the town hall, and in 1913 the architects Edward and William Maxwell renovated and enlarged it. Edward Maxwell also built the large bourgeois villa at 211 rue Oakridge.

## phase:p4

Farming holds on into the early twentieth century while the summer people animate the shore; streets are opened and subdivided but development stays south of the railway and moves far more slowly than in Pointe-Claire. Then, in the 1950s and 1960s, the Metropolitan expressway and the postwar demographic boom accelerate a suburb that is essentially residential, and building crosses to the north side of the tracks. The town's own account is blunt about the scale of it: the population increased fivefold in the 1950s. The cahier's verdict on the result is that the rural character disappears but the tree-lined streets keep a country look, and that the parks and the lac Saint-Louis go on attracting the more comfortable classes of Montréal society. In 2001 the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette is cited by the municipality. On 1 January 2002 Baie-d'Urfé is merged into the new ville de Montréal.

## phase:p5

The town is reconstituted on 1 January 2006 under Bill 9, after the referendums of 20 June 2004, with effect from 1 January. In 2024 the agglomeration of Montréal begins the inventory of pre-1940 buildings that the 2021 <em>Loi sur le patrimoine culturel</em> requires it to finish by 1 April 2026, using the ministry's methodology and grading on historical, architectural and social value, the survival of original characteristics, and state of maintenance. Baie-D'Urfé publishes its share of the result as annexe C of a new plan d'urbanisme, règlement 1108, adopted on 17 June 2025 and in force on 20 October 2025: forty-eight records, thirty-nine addresses, four grades, and not one line of description. The same by-law sets out, for the first time in a public document, exactly what protects them.

## notes

<strong>Baie-D'Urfé has no protected ensemble, and its own plan d'urbanisme says what it has instead.</strong> The RPCQ holds exactly one immovable record for the entire municipality — the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette — and a search of the RPCQ filtered to catégorie de bien <em>« Site patrimonial »</em> and municipality Baie-D'Urfé returns <em>« Aucun résultat »</em>; the same search across the Montréal region returns forty site records, none of them here. That is the provincial side. The municipal side is stated by the town itself in section 2.2.3.1 of règlement 1108, which is worth reading closely because it is an unusually honest piece of drafting. The exceptional sector — the parc Fritz and the hôtel de ville site — is safe, it says, <em>« du fait qu'il est entièrement propriété municipale »</em>. Every other heritage building, including the temple Dao En, is protected <em>« par une application stricte du règlement sur les démolitions »</em>. Infill is managed by the sheer size of the lots — 2,000 and 4,000 m² minimums, with the required front and side margins and the abundance of planting — and the by-law notes with satisfaction that those minimums <em>« présentent aussi l'avantage d'exclure pratiquement toute possibilité de morcellement des propriétés »</em>. Finally the PIIA. Ownership, a demolition permit, big lots and design review: four instruments, none of them a designation.

<strong>The one designation there is, is narrower than it looks.</strong> The maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette was <em>citée</em> by the municipality on 11 December 2001, and the RPCQ states the limit precisely: <em>« La protection s'applique uniquement à l'enveloppe extérieure de la partie la plus ancienne de l'ensemble, soit la section est du bâtiment. »</em> The house today is three volumes in an L — the c. 1700 east section, a central pavilion the Pilons are thought to have raised between 1839 and 1874 and Perrier gave neo-Gothic attributes in 1874, and a west pavilion built in 1949 by William Lawrence Glen. Only the first is cited, and only its skin. It is still a private dwelling.

<strong>The brief that commissioned this page counted two buildings where the sources have one.</strong> It listed <em>Maison Rangé-dit-Laviolette (c. 1700)</em> and, separately, <em>a 1703 stone building at 20122 Lakeshore</em>. The RPCQ gives 20122 chemin Lakeshore as the address of the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette and dates its construction <em>« vers 1700 – 1700 »</em>; the 2005 cahier lists 20122 chemin du Lakeshore as <em>« Maison Rangé (maison Lenoir), monument historique cité »</em>; the town's annexe C has one principal and one secondary building at that address. There is one house, and no source this project read gives it the date 1703. The brief also spelled the name with a lower-case <em>dit</em>; the RPCQ writes <em>Maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette</em>, and that spelling is used here.

<strong>The fiche disagrees with itself about the windows.</strong> Its <em>valeur patrimoniale</em> lists <em>« la disposition asymétrique des ouvertures »</em> among the elements that tie the house to the French tradition; its <em>éléments caractéristiques</em> list <em>« les ouvertures, dont leur disposition symétrique »</em>. Both statements are in the same record. They are reproduced here as they stand, and neither is used to fill a column.

<strong>The fiche is also out of date about where the house is.</strong> Its description ends <em>« La maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette est située dans l'arrondissement municipal de Beaconsfield-Baie-D'Urfé de la ville de Montréal »</em>, and its source line reads <em>Ville de Montréal, 2005</em>. That arrondissement was dissolved when Baie-D'Urfé was reconstituted on 1 January 2006, and the RPCQ has not revised the sentence.

<strong>Three dates circulate for the Sulpician concession, and they are not the same event.</strong> The 2005 cahier says the first lands here were conceded by the Sulpicians in <strong>1678</strong>. The RPCQ says <em>« une première concession est accordée en 1677 »</em> and that the lot under the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette was conceded by the Sulpicians in <strong>1686</strong>, the year the abbé Lascaris d'Urfé became the first resident curé of Saint-Louis-du-Bout-de-l'Île. The place record uses 1686 because that is the date attached to the one surviving building, and the others are recorded here rather than reconciled.

<strong>The town hall's dates come from two different sources and both are kept.</strong> The town says James Morgan gave the waterfront land at the first council meeting of 10 July 1911 and gave <em>« the white-frame farmhouse for conversion to a Town Hall »</em> in October 1912. The 2005 cahier and the 2025 plan d'urbanisme both say the building was renovated and enlarged in <strong>1913</strong> by the Maxwells — the plan naming them as Edward and William Maxwell. A farmhouse given in 1912 and enlarged by Montréal's most fashionable architects in 1913 is one story, not two.

<strong>The two lists of heritage buildings do not match, and the mismatch is published above.</strong> The Ville de Montréal named seventeen Baie-D'Urfé addresses as <em>immeubles de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle</em> in 2005. The town's own annexe C of 2025 grades thirty-nine addresses. Sixteen appear on both. Twenty-three are new in 2025. And one — 20658 chemin du Lakeshore, exceptional in 2005 — is simply not in the 2025 inventory, with no explanation in either document. The full row-by-row comparison is in the published CSV.

<strong>What could not be obtained.</strong> The <em>Inventaire des bâtiments patrimoniaux du secteur Baie-d'Urfé de l'arrondissement Beaconsfield–Baie d'Urfé de la Ville de Montréal</em> by Pierre Beaupré and Josette Michaud — cited by the 2005 cahier as <em>Cahier 1 – Textes préliminaires, sommaire de l'inventaire et liste des bâtiments</em>, a <em>rapport d'étape</em> dated 7 April 2003, and by the RPCQ in its completed 2004 form — is the one document that would turn this page's address lists into building descriptions. It is cited twice by institutional sources: in the 2005 cahier's bibliography, and in the RPCQ's own reference list for the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette, together with the same firm's 19-page <em>Évaluation de l'intérêt patrimonial de l'édifice situé au 20 122, chemin Lakeshore</em> of 2000. A second and separate 2004 document, <em>Inventaire des bâtiments patrimoniaux du secteur Baie D'Urfé, cahier des fiches</em>, published by the Ville de Montréal in the summer of 2004 and not the work of Beaupré and Michaud, is reported to exist but has not been seen either. Neither is published by the town; neither is on the architects' site, which lists the inventory only as a completed project; and no repository copy was found. Behind all of them stands the <em>Répertoire d'architecture traditionnelle de la Communauté urbaine de Montréal</em>, the 1980s survey that both towns' later documents draw on, likewise not obtained. Until one of them is read, the columns on this page's type cards stay empty.
