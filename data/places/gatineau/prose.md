## intro

Gatineau is five cities in one. Since 1 January 2002 the name has covered Hull, Aylmer, Gatineau, Buckingham and Masson-Angers, each with its own founding story and its own housing stock, and the result is a city whose residential landscape can only be read as five overlapping ones. It begins in 1800, when Philemon Wright brought a party of settlers from Massachusetts to the Chaudière falls and founded Wright's Town; his first timber raft went down the Ottawa in 1806, and the square-timber trade made the place. Downriver, Charles Symmes managed the Deschênes farm from 1821, bought and subdivided it in 1828, and gave Aylmer its Regency-era beginning — the auberge Charles-Symmes of 1831, the district court in 1842, the palais de justice in 1852 — a resort and administrative town of stone and clapboard, quite unlike the mill town at the falls.

The house that dominates this page came out of the second of those places. E. B. Eddy arrived in Hull in 1854, and the match works he built there gave the *maison allumette* — the match house — its name: the gable-front worker's dwelling, wood or brick, one or two storeys, that housed the families of the match and pulp mills through the demographic explosion of 1871–91. Its narrowness is not a style choice but a survey line. In 1864 W. A. Austin resubdivided the old Wright lots into the thin strips that still govern the Hull core, and every house built on them afterwards had to be narrow, deep and gable-end to the street.

Twice the stock burned. The Great Hull Fire of 1900 destroyed a large part of the town in the same year the Hull Electric Railway opened, and the Aylmer fire of 1921 took about a hundred residences; both places rebuilt quickly, which is why so much of what looks like nineteenth-century Gatineau is in fact early-twentieth-century replacement in the same forms. Then came a fourth foundation: the Canadian International Paper Company established at Templeton-Ouest in 1925–27 and built the Quartier-du-Moulin around 1926–33 — a company garden suburb of fifty-five houses, probably by William Lyon Somerville, in the town that incorporated as the village of Gatineau in 1933.

In 2023 the City published fourteen explanatory fiches, one for each residential type that dominates the Gatineau landscape. They are the reason this page looks the way it does: each fiche gives historical context, geographic distribution, volumetry, openings, cladding and ornament, with paired "interventions to favour / to avoid" and credited photographs of real addresses. Apart from Mount Royal's by-law tables, no other place on this site publishes anything as complete.

## phase:p1

Philemon Wright's party reaches the Chaudière in 1800 and the first raft of squared timber leaves for Québec in 1806. Charles Symmes manages the Deschênes farm from 1821, buys and subdivides it in 1828, and the first Aylmer houses go up about 1830 — the auberge Charles-Symmes of 1831 among them. The district court moves to Aylmer in 1842 and the palais de justice follows in 1852. What survives from this half-century is a thin layer: L-plan and gable-roofed houses in the Québec tradition, and the stone and clapboard of Aylmer's Regency-era main street.

## phase:p2

E. B. Eddy arrives in Hull in 1854, and match and pulp works turn the settlement into an industrial town. In 1864 W. A. Austin's survey cuts the old Wright lots into narrow strips, fixing the plan of the Hull core for good. Between 1871 and 1891 the population explodes, and the *maison allumette* — narrow, gable-front, wood-framed, one or two storeys — is built by the hundred to house the mill families. Aylmer meanwhile keeps its slower rhythm and its mansard-roofed and Second Empire houses.

## phase:p3

The Great Hull Fire of 1900 destroys much of the town in the same year the Hull Electric Railway opens; the Aylmer fire of 1921 takes about a hundred residences. Both are rebuilt at speed and largely in the same types, so this is the period that actually produced most of the surviving stock: brick *maisons allumettes*, flat-roofed wood and brick houses in the Boomtown manner, mixed-use blocks with shops below and dwellings above, and the *maison cubique* that arrived through American plan catalogues and settled in Hull, Aylmer and Buckingham alike.

## phase:p4

The Canadian International Paper Company establishes at Templeton-Ouest in 1925–27 and builds the Quartier-du-Moulin about 1926–33: fifty-five houses on three blocks, set back from the street behind a double row of trees, with asymmetrical L- and T-plans, tall gables over low ground floors, small-paned windows with shutters, and a deliberate variety of brick, stucco, shingle and clapboard. The village of Gatineau incorporates in 1933. Elsewhere in the city the interwar years bring central-gable and complex-gable-roofed houses in the vernacular tradition.

## phase:p5

The federal capital's growth reaches across the river. Post-war Gatineau fills with the North American bungalow — low, one-storey, wide-fronted, on suburban lots that owe nothing to the Austin survey — and the older cores absorb duplexes with the gable wall to the side, two dwellings superposed. It is the period the City's separate modern-heritage inventory covers: 164 buildings from 1937 to 1980, classified by function of origin crossed with morphology rather than by type.

## phase:p6

The first amalgamation, in 1975, gathers the Outaouais municipalities into a smaller set; the second, on 1 January 2002, produces the present city of Gatineau out of Hull, Aylmer, Gatineau, Buckingham and Masson-Angers. Aylmer's historic core had been cited as a heritage site in 1990 and the Quartier-du-Moulin in 1996, so both entered the merged city already protected — and the 2023 fiches are the first document to describe all five former cities' housing in one vocabulary.

## notes

**Two phases carry no card, and that is the fiches' answer, not an omission.** The fourteen types cluster in 1850–1900 and 1900–1925 — five records and seven — with one each for the CIP quarter and the post-war bungalow, and none at all before 1850 or after 1980. The earliest type the fiches describe, the L-plan house with a two-slope roof, is dated in its own text « vers la fin du 19e siècle », which is why it sits in the second phase and not the first, against the provisional reading this page started from. Two further assignments were corrected the same way when the fiche text contradicted them: that record now carries no style, because its fiche attributes none, and the CIP executive's house carries Arts and Crafts alone, because that is the only movement its fiche names.

Beyond that, three cautions about what this page is showing. First, **the fiches are advisory**. The fourteen documents of 2023 are explanatory tools that support the PIIA — the site-planning and architectural-integration programme, by-law R-505-2005 with 505.1-2011 for the Habitation and Commercial categories — and the "interventions to favour / to avoid" they set out are guidance for applicants and reviewers, not by-law text. The obligation comes from the PIIA and from the *Loi sur le patrimoine culturel* where a cited site applies; the fiche explains what good practice looks like.

Second, **the modern inventory is not encoded here**. EVOQ Stratégies delivered a separate *Inventaire du patrimoine bâti moderne* on 10 November 2023 covering 164 buildings from 1937 to 1980. It uses an entirely different classification — *familles typologiques* defined as function of origin (residential, industrial, commercial, institutional, parks and public spaces) crossed with morphology (volumetry, siting, plan) — and grades its buildings on five levels, of which 4 are *supérieure*, 65 *forte* and 93 *moyenne*. Mapping those families onto the fourteen fiche types would be an invention, so it is left out; the inventory is cited in the sources for anyone who needs it.

Third, **the Quartier-du-Moulin is Gatineau's, not Témiscaming's**. Canadian International Paper built two company towns in Québec, and both appear on this site. The Quartier-du-Moulin, cited in 1996, is here in Gatineau; Témiscaming, four hundred kilometres north-west, is the other, and its upper district was designed by the same architect, William Lyon Somerville, in the same years. The RPCQ's Gatineau entry explicitly links the two developments, which is exactly how the confusion starts. On this site they are connected in the honest way — through the shared canonical form `company-house-executive-1925` — and nothing more is claimed.
