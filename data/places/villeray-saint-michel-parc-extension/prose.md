## intro

Three settlements became one borough by annexation, and none of them began as a plan. Villeray was
made by a tramway: the Montreal Park and Island Railway ran an electric line north through the fields
in 1893 along what is now rue Lajeunesse, land promoters subdivided either side of it, and the people
who moved in were, in the cahier's phrase, <em>surtout composée d'ouvriers-locataires</em>. The
village was erected in 1896, had 800 inhabitants when Montréal annexed it in 1905, and then filled
very fast — a housing boom between 1925 and 1931 covering everything west of rue Saint-Hubert.
Parc-Extension was a speculation at the end of avenue du Parc, mounted by promoters competing with
the men building the model city next door at Ville Mont-Royal; lots went on sale in 1912 and the
buildings came decades later. Ville Saint-Michel was a quarry hamlet on the côte Saint-Michel with
lime kilns Pehr Kalm noticed in 1749; it was given a garden-city street plan in 1912, on the model
Ebenezer Howard had published in 1898, and did not build it out until after 1945.

What holds the borough together is not its history but its by-law. Règlement 01-283-124, Annexe F
sets out thirteen residential types over 109 pages, in three families — seven single-family houses,
three plex, three apartment buildings — and gives each of them the same eight-page treatment: a page
of historical context, an elevation, a description under three headings, a page or two of lettered
variants each with its own elevation, and three pages tabulating the original components and the
replacement components a permit may allow. <strong>No other borough on this site regulates at that
grain.</strong> Several of the variants name the zones they apply in, and one of them, variant B of
the post-war house, sets a different list of permitted claddings for zone H02-096 than for zones
H03-081, H03-085, H03-125 and H03-131. That is a typology annexe operating as a material schedule
keyed to a map.

The 2005 <em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em> cahier sits under all of it and does something
different: it grades territory. It publishes one exceptional sector, five interesting ones, three
ensembles urbains d'intérêt and two archaeological records, and it is candid about how modest the
material is — <em>Son bâti date principalement de la première moitié du XXe siècle. Les bâtiments et
secteurs d'intérêt patrimonial sont donc plutôt récents et modestes.</em> The single exceptional
sector is a street, rue Saint-Denis, and it is exceptional for its plex. Fifteen years later the
by-law answered it directly by naming a type variant after it: <em>plex de la rue Saint-Denis</em>,
the only variant in the whole annexe named after a street.

## notes

<strong>The shoebox, and a disagreement between two boroughs of the same city.</strong>
Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension splits the shoebox into two numbered types. Fiche 1.2 is the
<em>shoebox du début du 20e siècle</em>: <em>« La première phase de développement de cette typologie
s'étend des années 1900 à 1940. Ce sont des constructions simples, réalisées à faible coût à partir
des matériaux disponibles à l'époque. […] On les trouve principalement dans Villeray et
Parc-Extension. »</em> Fiche 1.3 is the <em>shoebox du milieu du 20e siècle</em>: <em>« À partir de
1940 jusqu'en 1960, le traitement architectural des shoebox se simplifie peu à peu. Le couronnement
est sobre et le rez-de-chaussée est graduellement surélevé pour permettre l'aménagement d'un
sous-sol. L'implantation majoritairement contiguë sera peu à peu remplacée par une implantation
jumelée ou isolée, présentant une marge de recul plus importante afin de dégager l'espace pour garer
une voiture. »</em> Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie, immediately to the south-east, treats the shoebox as
one type. Both boroughs are in the same city, both are regulating the same object, and their
documents are roughly contemporary. <strong>This is a genuine typological disagreement, not a
difference of detail, and this site records it as one rather than resolving it.</strong> That is
also the honest position: this record was written without reading Rosemont's, and it states only
this borough's case.

The case is stronger than it first looks, because this borough counts. Its own 2019 guide inventories
1 159 shoebox houses and tabulates them by decade: 16 in 1900-1909, 144 in 1910-1919, 136 in
1920-1929, 128 in 1930-1939, 191 in 1940-1949, and <strong>544 in the eleven years 1950-1960</strong>
— 46.9 % of the whole stock in the last decade of its life. Add the 1940s and 63.4 % of the borough's
shoeboxes postdate the moment fiche 1.2 stops. A borough whose shoebox population is mostly post-war
has a practical reason to describe the post-war shoebox separately, and a borough whose shoeboxes are
mostly older does not. The same table also shows where they are: François-Perrault 660, Saint-Michel
256, Villeray 153, Parc-Extension 90 — so the fiche's own claim that the early type is found
<em>principalement dans Villeray et Parc-Extension</em> is a statement about the early type, not
about the borough, whose shoebox mass sits in the two northern districts.

What actually differs between the two fiches is worth stating precisely, because it is not
ornament alone. Four things change together between 1940 and 1960: the crown goes from a cornice or
a pedimented parapet to something sober; the ground floor rises to make a real basement out of what
had been a crawl space; the party walls are given up for a gap on each side; and the front margin
deepens, expressly to park a car. Those are bungalow properties — this same by-law files the bungalow
six fiches later, at 1.7, with a deep setback, a raised floor over a basement garage, detached siting
and horizontal windows — arriving inside a flat-roofed rectangular envelope that does not change at
all. Two of fiche 1.3's five variants build on the raised floor: variant D puts a garage under it,
reached by a sunken drive, and variant E puts a second dwelling under it, reached independently
through an areaway beneath the front perron.

<strong>« Uniplex ».</strong> Variant E is where the borough's proposed alternative name becomes
legible. The 2018 heritage study behind the borough's shoebox guidance proposes that these houses
could as well be called <em>uniplex</em>, on the ground that they are generally single-family
dwellings that belong to the plex family, some of them with a dwelling in the basement — which would
make the shoebox the family's member of one. <strong>That claim is recorded here in English only,
and deliberately.</strong> The study itself could not be retrieved in this pass: its address on the
Ville's legacy portal now redirects to a dead page, and no mirror was found. No French quotation for
« uniplex » is published anywhere in this record, because the sentence was never read in the
original. The name is worth keeping in view all the same, because the by-law's own family logic
supports it: family 2.0 defines the plex as <em>« de deux à six logements »</em> whose distinguishing
feature is that <em>« chaque logement présente habituellement une entrée distincte en façade »</em>,
and a shoebox with a basement flat entered through its own areaway satisfies that description at
n = 2.

<strong>What the Part 10 brief did not have.</strong> The brief flagged that the plex extraction
might have been truncated after fiche 2.2, and asked for a check. It was truncated, and by more than
one fiche. <strong>Fiche 2.3, <em>plex de la seconde moitié du 20e siècle</em>, exists</strong> — and
the by-law has a third family after it, <strong>3.0 Immeubles d'appartements</strong>, holding fiches
3.1, 3.2 and 3.3. Thirteen fiches, not nine. Fiche 2.3 is the most quotable of the three plex: from
the 1960s, <em>« aussi connue sous le nom "plex italien" en raison de l'origine de ses
constructeurs »</em>, with pale brick, white or light yellow and sometimes glazed, laid at times in
coloured patterns the by-law calls <em>fantaisistes</em>, and its most homogeneous groups in
Saint-Michel. It is the only type on this site named for the community that built it, and the name
is the municipality's own — the borough's public guidance uses it too.

Family 3.0 matters for a different reason: it is where this by-law draws the outer boundary of the
plex, and it draws it in an unusual place. <em>« Ils se distinguent des bâtiments de type plex par
l'intégration, à l'intérieur du plan de l'édifice, d'un escalier permettant l'accès aux logements et
qui rompt avec la tradition montréalaise des escaliers extérieurs. En raison de leur configuration,
ces immeubles peuvent comporter significativement plus de logements que les plex. »</em> The line is
the stair, and the dwelling count follows from it — where the Sud-Ouest typo-morphological study
draws the same line at a shared hall serving six to twelve dwellings. Both tests belong on the
exterior-stair topic page, and this one is the sharper, because it says outright that putting the
stair inside breaks with a Montréal tradition.

<strong>The code, and why the filename cannot be trusted.</strong> This borough is arrondissement
<strong>14</strong>. That was read from inside the cahier, where all 121 sector-code occurrences
carry the prefix 14, in a file published as <code>26_evaluation_patrimoine_villeray.pdf</code>. The
Part 10 memo's expectation was correct and the filename is not evidence of anything. The cahier also
uses a code letter this site's schema does not carry: alongside <code>14.AP.1</code>, a
<em>secteur d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel</em>, it prints <code>14.A.1</code> with a bare
A, for the lower rank of <em>secteur d'intérêt archéologique</em>. The difference is substantive —
for the AP class the cahier says protective measures are necessary, for the A class it says further
study is needed first — and both are filed here under one value with the distinction recorded on each
sector.

<strong>Parc-Extension, and what could not be confirmed.</strong> The cahier gives the quarter's name
from <em>« le projet de développement connu sous le nom de Park Avenue Extension »</em>, near Ville
Mont-Royal and a Canadian Pacific line, and dates the sale of lots to 1912: <em>« Sur le territoire
de Parc Extension, les lots sont à vendre dès 1912, mais il faudra attendre beaucoup plus tard pour
voir une véritable urbanisation. »</em> The Ville's MEM encyclopedia dates the quarter's beginning to
1910 and gives its area as 1.6 km². Two further claims in circulation could not be sourced here and
are flagged rather than asserted: that the lots were Park Realty's and were laid out in
<strong>1907</strong> — a date the cahier's own « dès 1912 » does not support, and which no municipal
document consulted in this pass carries — and that <em>69 % des immeubles du quartier sont des
duplex et des multiplex (4 à 12 logements)</em>, which comes from a Ville de Montréal quarter
portrait that returned 404 on every route tried. The same gap leaves the wartime-house quadrilatère
bounded by Bloomfield, Jarry, Wiseman and de Liège unconfirmed; it is encoded as a sector under a
deliberately non-conforming code, <code>PE-WH-1</code>, with the doubt written into the record, so it
can be verified or deleted rather than quietly inherited.

<strong>A note on the extraction.</strong> The by-law PDF sets some of its body text in a font whose
glyphs <code>pdftotext</code> cannot map, and it drops those lines silently — including, on the
shoebox page, the clause that says the façade is symmetrical. Every French sentence quoted in this
record was therefore checked against a rendered image of its page, not against the text layer alone.
The pages this affected are listed in <code>sources/vsp/MANIFEST.md</code>.

## footer

Encoded from règlement 01-283-124, Annexe F and the 2005 <em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em>
cahier, both read in full; the borough's 2019 shoebox guide supplies the counts. Thirteen type
records, one per fiche, with the by-law's own numbering, its own French, and its own lettered
variants. Where a claim could not be traced to a municipal document it is labelled as unverified on
the record that carries it rather than left to look sourced.
