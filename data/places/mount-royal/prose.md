## intro

Town of Mount Royal is the only place in Québec built in its entirety to a single garden-city plan, drawn by Frederick Gage Todd in 1912 for the Canadian Northern Railway to pay for the tunnel under the mountain. It filled in over three periods, and each period left its own family of houses. The town's design-review by-law (PIIA By-law No. 1449, 2018) names them, photographs them and lists what makes each one itself. This page is that catalogue, in English.

The Town publishes By-law 1449 in French only. Photos below are the by-law's own; the “What the by-law says” lists are direct translations of its tables; the short descriptions are ours.

## phase:p1

The first houses rose near the station and along the diagonal boulevards while the tunnel was still being bored (1910–1918); the town's first mayor, Thomas S. Darling, was the railway land company's man on the ground. Two lineages meet in this phase, one imported and one local.

## phase:p2

Building slowed in the Depression and resumed in the late 1930s and after the war, when the historical revivals then dominant across anglophone North America set the tone. Lots widened to 15–22 m and the garage attached itself to the house. Four revivals and one apartment idiom account for nearly everything.

## phase:p3

The widest lots (20–25 m) were the last to fill, and they filled under the car. Roofs flatten to 30° or less, windows lose their muntins and turn horizontal, the garage moves into the façade. Héritage Montréal lists Mount Royal among the Montreal-area districts where the postwar house is common, and notes that some such ensembles now carry real heritage value. Four house types and one apartment type carry the period.

## notes

By-law 1449 sorts the town's houses into thirteen <em>types</em>, named for period, lot and massing rather than for the movements that produced them. This section restores the conventional style names and says where each came from. The frame is the town's own history: a speculative railway suburb laid out in 1912 by landscape architect Frederick Gage Todd for the Canadian Northern Railway, whose lot sales were meant to pay for the tunnel under the mountain; a plan mixing Garden City, City Beautiful and urban-park ideas that Parks Canada recognised in 2008 as a National Historic Site; and a build-out over roughly six decades that tracked, phase by phase, the shift in North American suburban taste from English cottage to historical revival to postwar modern.

Two styles that visitors expect to find by name, <strong>Art Deco</strong> and <strong>Streamline Moderne</strong>, are present in TMR mainly as ornament on Phase 1 walk-ups and as a minority of Phase 3 cottages and early apartment blocks. Neither dominates a zone, so neither receives a table in the by-law; such buildings are reviewed under the general criteria of Chapter III instead. <strong>Tudor Revival</strong> is fully present, but named by its massing ("English manor") rather than its style. The by-law's own count has also grown: the version in force through the 2000s recognised eight house styles across the same three phases; the 2018 by-law splits out the apartment types and adds Georgian and Prairie, for thirteen.

By-law 1449 is a discretionary review instrument. Québec's Act respecting land use planning and development has allowed municipalities to adopt PIIA by-laws since 1989 (sections 145.15 to 145.20.1); the provincial planning guide is explicit that a PIIA does not judge whether a project is warranted, only its appearance and its integration into its setting, and that approval adds a condition to the permit without changing zoning. Council decides by resolution on the recommendation of the Planning Advisory Committee. Because approvals and refusals must be reasoned and can be litigated (TMR's PIIA survived a Court of Appeal challenge over a Kindersley Avenue addition in the early 2000s), the by-law describes buildings in observable, measurable terms: setback, lot width, roof pitch, window proportion, brick module. "Not Tudor enough" is not a finding; "roof pitch under 45° and no small-paned glazing" is.

The thirteen categories follow the typo-morphological method used across Québec heritage characterisation studies and municipal PIIAs: period of formation crossed with lot, volume, roof, openings and material. Montreal's Plateau by-law and Québec City's built-heritage thesaurus classify their housing the same way, and use some of the same words. Chapter II of By-law 1449 is that kind of inventory, condensed. Each type is assigned to zones through the zoning grid and mapped in Annex A, which means only house forms frequent enough to dominate a zone become named types. Frequency, not significance, is the filter. That is why Tudor Revival appears under the name of its massing, why Art Deco is absorbed into the Faubourg apartment block, and why single Deco, Moderne or hybrid houses have no table of their own.

The by-law is candid that its tables are references, not templates. Article 18 states that the PIIA "does not aim to restrict creativity and originality by imposing solutions copied from the architecture of the past, but rather to support the development of an architecture adapted to today's needs, in harmony with the specific character that stands as heritage value." In practice a house that fits no type is judged against the dominant type of its zone plus the general criteria of Chapter III: composition, subordination and legibility of additions, preservation of original components, materials, landscape. That is the approach's weakness as well as its strength. It is consistent and defensible, and a minority house is always read through the majority's lens.

The style names on this page are ours, added so the town's houses can be read in the vocabulary of architectural history as well as in the by-law's own. Where the two disagree, the by-law's tables govern what the Town will approve.

## footer

<strong>A note on the by-law's intent.</strong> Its own words, translated: the PIIA “does not aim to restrict creativity and originality by imposing solutions copied from the architecture of the past, but rather to support the development of an architecture adapted to today's needs, in harmony with the specific character that stands as heritage value.” The characteristics above are guidance the Planning Advisory Committee weighs; the by-law's evaluation criteria (siting, massing, composition, materials, details, landscaping) apply on top of them.

<strong>Vocabulary.</strong> <em>Articulation</em> translates <em>découpage</em>, the by-law's word for how the façade is broken into base, body and crown. <em>Larmier</em> is the flared drip edge of a Québec roof. <em>Chamois</em> is buff-coloured brick. A <em>cottage</em>, in Québec French, is any two-storey detached house.
