## intro

Île d'Orléans is the clearest surviving sequence of the Québec rural canon anywhere. Six parishes
sit at roughly even intervals around a single ring road, the <em>chemin Royal</em>, which has
circled the island since 1744; behind them the seigneurial <em>côtes</em> run in long narrow
rectangles from the river up to the wooded crest at the centre, the <em>Mitan</em>. About 95 % of
the territory is still farmed. Declared a heritage site on 11 March 1970 — the second in Québec
after the Vieux-Québec — it holds some 3,600 buildings, nineteen of them classed immeubles
patrimoniaux, and its houses can be read in order: the low stone house of French inspiration with
its steep short-eaved roof; the neoclassical Québec house raised just enough off the ground to need
a gallery, its eaves curving out to cover it; the mansard; the Regency cottage of the
Sainte-Pétronille resort; then the American vernacular, the cubique and the Boomtown house arriving
by catalogue.

The instrument that makes all this legible is an inventory. In 2013 and 2014 the firm Patri-Arch
surveyed the island for the MRC and the ministère, defined <strong>eleven courants
architecturaux</strong>, gave each one a page of narrative closing in a bulleted list of
<em>principaux éléments caractéristiques</em>, and put a plate of captioned example addresses
opposite. It then did something the earlier inventories had never done: it rated every one of the
659 buildings on a five-level scale, the <strong>cote A–E</strong>, computed from five weighted
criteria. Since August 2019 that rating has legal force — the ministry's relaxation measures allow
asphalt shingle only on buildings rated C, D or E, and only outside the village sectors, and only
where the roof is already non-traditional. A heritage rating that decides a permitted material is
rare, and worth stating plainly.

The inventory is careful about its own categories in a way that is worth repeating: <em>«
l'architecture de l'île d'Orléans est parfois métissée et … un bâtiment peut parfois être à cheval
entre deux courants architecturaux. C'est pourquoi on parle plutôt d'influences stylistiques ou
d'emprunts d'éléments à une typologie donnée plutôt qu'à des styles "purs". »</em> Eleven courants
are a finding aid, not a taxonomy of eleven kinds of thing.

## phase:p1

Indigenous presence on the island goes back roughly five thousand years. Jacques Cartier reaches it
in 1535 and names it the Île de Bacchus, renaming it the following year for the duc d'Orléans;
Champlain visits in 1603. In <strong>1636</strong> the Compagnie des Cent-Associés concedes the
island <em>en seigneurie</em> to the Compagnie de Beaupré, and the first colonists arrive in
<strong>1648</strong>, making this one of the earliest settlement hearths of the St Lawrence valley
— some three hundred families from France in the 17th century, with more than a hundred thousand
descendants in North America today. The Hurons-Wendat occupy the anse du Fort from 1651 to 1656 and
leave after an Iroquois attack. The parish of l'Île is founded in <strong>1661</strong> and its
church built about eight years later in what becomes Sainte-Famille; in <strong>1679</strong>
Saint-François, Saint-Pierre and Saint-Paul — renamed Saint-Laurent in 1698 — are detached, and the
Saint-Jean mission opens the year after. The ancienne église de Saint-Pierre of 1717–1719 is the
oldest rural church in Québec. The chemin Royal is completed around the island in
<strong>1744</strong>. In 1759 the western sector is sacked during the siege of Québec. The house of
this period is the <em>maison d'inspiration française</em>: a low stone box, a battered wall, one
massive central chimney, a roof over 45° in cedar shingle, and as few openings as the climate
allows.

## phase:p2

The Conquest changes the buildings slowly. Tradesmen stay and French practice holds for decades;
what arrives early in the 19th century is English classical architecture, carried by military
engineers, British architects and pattern books. Out of the two comes the <em>maison traditionnelle
québécoise d'influence néoclassique</em> — the island's most numerous 19th-century house, and,
according to the plan de conservation, the type that shaped the identity of the village sectors,
particularly Saint-Laurent and Saint-Jean. The carré is lifted just enough for a rudimentary cellar,
which produces a gallery and steps; the eaves curve outward as <em>larmiers incurvés</em> to cover
them, or an independent lean-to awning does the same work where the slopes are straight. A summer
kitchen often extends the house at one end, repeating its shape at smaller scale. The route du Mitan
across the island opens in <strong>1830</strong>.

## phase:p3

Specialised market agriculture, then maritime work on the south shore, then visitors. A ferry runs
from Québec from <strong>1855</strong>, and Sainte-Pétronille becomes one of the region's principal
<em>villégiature</em> stations, with beaches, summer hotels and pensions, and a golf club at 8170
chemin Royal — Bonjour Québec describes the course as the oldest in North America, a claim recorded
here with its attribution and not asserted. The parish of Sainte-Pétronille is erected in
<strong>1870</strong>. Three courants belong to these decades. The <em>Second Empire</em> mansard
arrives in the last quarter of the century as a popular, modest version of a monumental style,
keeping the broken roof because it turns the attic into a full second storey. The <em>cottage
Regency</em>, English in origin and romantic in temper, is built for a large wooded lot and wraps
itself in a veranda under low, wide, sometimes upturned eaves; it is rare on the island and almost
entirely concentrated at Sainte-Pétronille. <em>Victorian eclecticism</em> barely lands here at all
— the inventory finds very few real examples, and mostly ordinary house types wearing an elaborate
decor.

## phase:p4

Standardisation reaches the countryside. Milled lumber, catalogue doors and windows, and the balloon
frame produce three courants that the inventory treats as separate and equal: the <em>cottage
vernaculaire américain</em>, which is the traditional Québec house simplified and made more vertical,
with straight slopes and no curved larmier; the <em>maison cubique</em>, an American Four Square
model credited to the architect Frank Kidder in 1891, two full floors under a shallow pavilion roof,
its ornament varying frankly with the owner's social standing; and the <em>maison Boomtown</em>,
flat-roofed and two-storeyed, spending everything it has on a cornice or a stepped parapet at the top
of the façade. All three are thin on this island — <em>assez peu représentée</em>, <em>peu
représenté</em>, <em>quelques exemplaires</em> — which is itself the finding. Alongside them, and
almost entirely at Sainte-Pétronille, comes <em>Arts and Crafts</em>: articulated plans, deep
overhanging eaves, exposed rafters and half-timbering, of which the best examples are the house and
studio of the painter Horatio Walker at 11 and 13 chemin Horatio-Walker.

## phase:p5

The <em>pont de l'Île</em> opens to traffic on 6 July <strong>1935</strong>, and the <em>Loi
concernant l'Île d'Orléans</em> follows the same year. The bridge strengthens the island's resort
role: small chalets go up on tight lots between the chemin Royal and the foreshore through the
1940s, 1950s and 1960s, and the first suburban estates appear on subdivided farmland near the bridge
at Sainte-Pétronille, Saint-Pierre and Saint-Laurent — the landscape unit the plan de conservation
says frankly is <em>« pas lié au passé rural et villageois de l'île d'Orléans »</em>. Two courants
sit in this phase and pull in opposite directions. <em>Régionalisme québécois</em>, 1910 to the end
of the Second World War, deliberately copies the buildings of the French regime — high shingled
roof, fire-break gable walls, stone or stucco — so faithfully that the inventory warns its houses
can be mistaken for far older ones; the examples here are dated 1939, 1940, 1944 and 1945.
<em>Modernisme</em> is represented by exactly one house.

## phase:p6

The declaration of <strong>11 March 1970</strong> made the whole island a protected site, and every
significant intervention now needs authorisation under arts. 64–65 of the Loi sur le patrimoine
culturel. The 2017 plan de conservation set out the ministry's orientations — three that govern
every kind of intervention, and 239 numbered ones below them, the last grouped by <em>unité de
paysage</em>. The 2019 relaxation measures answered residents' complaints by loosening the rules on
roofing, windows, colour, patio doors, garages and suburban new-build, and tied several of those
loosenings to the inventory's own cote. Comparing the old photographs with the new, the inventory
reports something genuinely encouraging: many buildings disfigured in the 1950s and 1960s, before the
arrondissement historique existed, have since been restored with appropriate materials and have got
their heritage value back.

## notes

<strong>(a) Four building counts, four scopes.</strong> The RPCQ says the declared site holds
<em>« quelque 3 600 bâtiments »</em> and lists 3,492 associated records; the Patri-Arch built-heritage
inventory contains 659 buildings; nineteen are classed immeubles patrimoniaux, of which thirteen were
already classed when the 2013–14 survey was evaluated. These measure different things. The 3,600 is
everything inside the perimeter. The 659 is the built-heritage inventory alone and excludes the
separate religious, agricultural and Boomtown/flat-roof inventories, so it is not "the heritage
buildings of the island". No single number should be published for this place.

<strong>(b) A rating that decides a material.</strong> The cote A–E is assigned across five criteria
— intérêt historique, intérêt ethnologique (authenticité), intérêt paysager, intérêt artistique et
architectural, valeur d'usage — each rated separately in PIMIQ, and the report is explicit that the
global cote is a weighted judgement rather than an average: <em>« Ceci n'est pas un exercice
mathématique ; il faut prendre en compte que les critères n'ont pas tous la même importance ou portée.
»</em> Since August 2019 the cote does regulatory work: asphalt shingle is permitted only on houses
rated C, D or E, only outside the village sectors, and only where the roof already carries asphalt or
another non-traditional material. This is the only place on this site where a heritage
<em>rating</em> directly determines a permitted <em>material</em>, and it is worth noticing as a
general mechanism — a descriptive instrument built for research becomes, a decade later, the trigger
for a permission. The parsed distribution shows what that means in practice: 537 of the 659
inventoried buildings are rated C, D or E, so the relaxation reaches most of the inventory, while the
122 rated A or B stay under the stricter rule.

<strong>(c) Three families or eleven courants.</strong> The RPCQ fiche for the site groups the
housing into only three families — maisons québécoises d'esprit français in stone or wood, maisons
québécoises d'inspiration néoclassique, and maisons de villégiature de facture éclectique gathering
Regency, Second Empire and Victorian together. The MRC inventory splits the same fabric eleven ways.
The plan de conservation uses a third, intermediate reading, and folds the cubique, the cottage
vernaculaire and the Boomtown house into a single <em>maison vernaculaire industrielle</em>. This
site encodes the eleven, because they are the finest instrument and the only one with a per-type
characteristics list, and records the others as parallel readings in /methods/.

<strong>(d) The inventory's date.</strong> The file is served as
<code>rapport_synthese_io_050919_maj2025.pdf</code> and its PDF ModDate is 20 June 2025, but its
cover reads <em>« Août 2014 »</em> and its methodology section says the fieldwork ran from October to
December 2013 and resumed in May 2014. Treat the content as the 2014 Patri-Arch synthesis reissued in
2025. Nothing in the parsed text indicates a substantive update; the type records carry the 2014 date
with the reissue noted in <code>source_generation</code>.

<strong>(e) "Maison de colonisation" is not a courant here.</strong> It does not appear anywhere in
the eleven-type list, nor in the inventory's vocabulary. Any such label applied to an Île d'Orléans
house would be inference, not a source term. The contrast is with Part 5 of this project, where the
MRC de Témiscamingue does use the term for Latulipe-et-Gaboury and it is therefore encoded — the
label travels with the source that uses it, not with the building shape.

<strong>(f) The seigneurial founders.</strong> The 1636 concession of the island <em>en
seigneurie</em> to the Compagnie de Beaupré by the Compagnie des Cent-Associés is on the RPCQ fiche
and is published here. The names commonly attached to that grant in the secondary literature —
Jacques Castillon, Éléonore de Grandmaison — are not in the RPCQ fiche, are not verified here, and
are marked as secondary literature rather than stated as fact.

<strong>(g) Dates that do not agree, and ids that were dropped.</strong> Church dates come from
mixed sources: Sainte-Famille 1743–1747, the ancienne église de Saint-Pierre 1717–1719 and Saint-Jean
1734–1737 are widely given, but the Saint-Laurent 1860 and Sainte-Pétronille 1871 dates come from
tourism material and a parish-erection date and have not been checked against the individual RPCQ
church fiches — treat them as provisional. Two other checks were made and did change what is
published. The maison Morisset's build date is given as 1678 in secondary sources; its RPCQ fiche
(92475) says <em>« construite avant 1699 et agrandie avant 1727 »</em>, and the fiche is what is
encoded, along with its classement of 7 June 1962. And three anchor houses named in the research
were dropped outright: no <strong>maison Ferland</strong> or <strong>maison Beaulieu</strong> on this
island carries an RPCQ record — the only Maison Ferland in the register is at Sorel-Tracy, and
"chemin Ferland" is simply a street in Saint-Laurent, which is exactly how the name-matching went
wrong — and both <strong>Canac-Marquis</strong> records belong elsewhere, 92660 in Québec City and
235048 in Saint-André in the Bas-Saint-Laurent. None of the three appears among the nineteen classed
buildings the RPCQ lists for the site. Finally, the golf course at Sainte-Pétronille: Bonjour Québec
describes it as the oldest in North America and GolfPass gives 1868. The plan de conservation
mentions only <em>« un terrain de golf, qui est toujours présent »</em>. The claim is recorded here
with its attribution and is not asserted.
