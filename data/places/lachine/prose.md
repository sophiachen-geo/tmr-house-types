## intro

<strong>Lachine is a borough of Montréal, and that is the first thing to say about it.</strong> Every
other place in this section of the site — Pointe-Claire, Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue, Dorval, Senneville,
Baie-D'Urfé — was merged into Montréal in 2002 and reconstituted as a city or a village on 1 January
2006 after the referendums of 20 June 2004. Lachine was not. It went into the merger and stayed, and
it is governed today by a borough council under the Ville de Montréal, with a borough zoning by-law
and a borough PIIA, while its neighbours along the same lakeshore run their own. The consequence for
this site is direct: Lachine's heritage sectors come from the Ville de Montréal's own borough-by-
borough <em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em> series, the same instrument that governs Verdun, the
Sud-Ouest and Rosemont, and not from a municipal inventory of its own.

Geography made the place before politics did. The rapides de Lachine stop everything coming up the
St Lawrence, and the canoe routes from the <em>pays d'en Haut</em> converge just above them; the
ground here is where cargo had to come out of the water. In 1667 the Sulpicians, seigneurs of the
island since 1663, conceded an arrière-fief called the côte Saint-Sulpice to René-Robert Cavelier de
La Salle, who sold part of it in 1669 to the merchants Charles Le Moyne and Jacques Le Ber. Between
1669 and 1671 they built a stone house there to serve as a trading post, a warehouse for outfitting
the <em>coureurs des bois</em>, and a dwelling. It is still standing, it is classed, and it is the
oldest building on this page by a century and a half.

What came after arrived in two waves that never really mixed. The canal, opened in 1825 and enlarged
twice, and the railway that reached the Lachine quay in 1847, made the east end an industrial town:
Dominion Bridge from 1883, the Dawes brewery from 1811, and behind them a dense grid of village
houses, one-storey brick houses and brick duplexes and triplexes. The west end went the other way.
The 1855 railway brought wealthy anglophone families out to the lake, Summerlea was created in 1895
and annexed in 1912, and the ground west of the 32nd Avenue filled with Shingle Style and Arts and
Crafts summer houses on very large, very deep, well-planted lots. The cahier's own summary of the
split is blunt: the east is dense, mixed and short of green space, "surtout du logement ouvrier"; the
west is strictly residential, "moins dense, beaucoup plus verdoyante et habitée par une population
économiquement plus à l'aise qui en fait très tôt un lieu de villégiature".

## phase:p1

The rapids are the reason. The navigable routes from the west converge at the head of a five-kilometre
barrier, and traffic between Montréal and the interior has to stop and portage. Traces of Indigenous
occupation going back to the early Middle Woodland have been found on the point. In 1667 La Salle is
conceded the arrière-fief of the côte Saint-Sulpice by the Sulpicians, seigneurs of the island since
1663; in 1669 he gives the fief back to its first owners and sells a share to Charles Le Moyne
(1626–1685) and Jacques Le Ber (vers 1633–1706), fur-trade partners who see a hinge of the trade in
it. Between 1669 and 1671 they build in stone: a cellar, an attic, two rooms and a chimney. The post
is a secondary one and lasts about fifteen years; in 1687 it is sold to François Guillemot dit
Lalande. On the morning of 5 August 1689 the colony at Lachine is attacked by the Iroquois, settlers
are killed and many buildings destroyed — the Lachine massacre — and the fur trade and settlement
both stall until the Grande Paix of 1701. Historians long assumed the Le Ber-Le Moyne house burned
that morning; the excavations begun in 1998 have found no trace of fire. The site is abandoned for a
few years and then laid out as a farm by Marguerite Chorel (vers 1670–1736) and her husband Guillaume
de Lorimier des Bordes (1657–1709), who settle there in 1695 and whose descendants stay until 1765.
Registers for the parish of Les Saints-Anges-de-Lachine open in 1676, over a territory then running
from the côte des Argoulets, now Verdun, all the way to the pointe Claire.

## phase:p2

Most of the population lives on land conceded by the Sulpicians and stays agricultural through the
18th century, and the number of inhabitants barely moves. Two things change that. In 1811 the Dawes
brewery is established on the shore of Lac Saint-Louis and becomes the third largest on the island;
workers and shops gather round it, more than half of Lachine's working population concentrates there,
and the farms around convert to barley and hops for it. And in 1825 the canal is dug. The idea is old
— Dollier de Casson pushed the Sulpicians to attempt it in the 17th century — but it takes Canadian
government money in the early 19th to carry it through. A new urban and economic pole grows at the
canal mouth: innkeepers, hoteliers, artisans and small traders living off the traffic. A large enough
Anglo-Protestant population supports two churches of different denominations in the 1830s. In 1845
the parish municipality is created and in 1848 the village municipality. In 1847 the first railway on
the island, linking Montréal to the Lachine quay, sharpens the trade again. In 1861–1865 the église
des Saints-Anges is built on its present site next to the two Protestant churches, and the
institutional core takes shape.

## phase:p3

In 1872 Lachine becomes a ville with water, sewers and street lighting, well ahead of the other lake
municipalities. At the end of the century the big firms arrive at the canal: Dominion Bridge, a maker
of steel structures, settles near the locks in 1883 and goes on to build bridges and buildings across
Québec and Canada; one of its first contracts is the Canadian Pacific railway bridge nearby. Workers
arrive in numbers and settle in the east end, which densifies and spills across the railway; the
firms also employ skilled tradesmen and professionals, who go to the west end instead. In 1912 Lachine
loses its eastern territory, which becomes the municipality of LaSalle, and gains Summerlea in the far
west, created in 1895 — a place of summer houses, chalets and large bourgeois residences near a golf
course. Ville Saint-Pierre, created as the village of Saint-Pierre-aux-Liens in 1894 and a ville in
1908, is meanwhile drawing industry of its own with tax exemptions and cheap land: Canadian Car and
Foundry in 1905, Consumers Glass in 1917.

## phase:p4

The municipal policy of attracting industry keeps the population climbing until the 1930 crisis
levels it off. Then, like other towns, Lachine answers the postwar boom by turning its unbuilt
sectors into dormitory quarters. The mass urbanisation of the 1950s to 1970s fills the north and west
of the territory and works its way into the older fabric as well, and the forms it produces — the
cahier says so plainly — are the ones that testify to the emergence of a middle class, individual
ownership and a way of living organised around the car. By the 1970s the population is over 50,000.
That is also when the canal, after more than a century of intense traffic, closes to through
navigation; it passes to Parks Canada in 1978. In 2000 Ville Saint-Pierre is annexed to Lachine, and
in 2002 the merged municipality becomes a borough of the Ville de Montréal.

## phase:p5

Lachine does not demerge. The referendums of 20 June 2004 and Bill 9 give six of the seven places in
this part of the site their independence back on 1 January 2006; Lachine remains an arrondissement.
The same year the merger took effect, two other things happen on its ground: the canal reopens to
pleasure craft — the dignitaries gathered at lock no. 5 on 17 May 2002 — and the Le Ber-Le Moyne site,
classed by Québec in 2001, is designated a national historic site by the Historic Sites and Monuments
Board of Canada on 1 November 2002. A transfer of responsibility registered 21 September 2017 and
effective 21 September 2018 moves authorisation of work inside the classed site from the ministère to
the Ville de Montréal. In 2025 the borough council takes up a wholesale replacement of its urbanism
by-laws, extending the PIIA to the entire territory, and Parks Canada marks the canal's bicentenary.

## notes

<strong>Lachine never demerged, and this page says so twice on purpose.</strong> The other six places
of Part 11 are either a central borough (Ville-Marie) or reconstituted municipalities. Lachine is the
only West Island core that went into the 2002 merger and stayed in it. It matters for how the page is
built: Lachine has no municipal heritage inventory, no citation by-law and no local <em>site
patrimonial cité</em>. What it has instead is a chapter of the Ville de Montréal's twenty-seven-volume
<em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em>, and the borough-level zoning and PIIA by-laws that any
Montréal borough has. Compare Pointe-Claire, five kilometres west, which used its restored municipal
powers to cite a 2.7-hectare site in 2013.

<strong>Two instruments over one house, one year apart.</strong> The Le Ber-Le Moyne site carries a
provincial <em>classement</em> as a <em>site patrimonial</em> dated 26 April 2001 and a federal
designation as a <em>lieu historique national du Canada</em> dated 1 November 2002. They are not the
same thing and they do not do the same work. The provincial classement is what makes work on the site
subject to authorisation under the <em>Loi sur le patrimoine culturel</em> — and since 21 September
2018, to authorisation by the Ville de Montréal rather than by the ministère, under a transfer of
powers registered a year earlier. The federal designation is commemorative. The RPCQ fiche states the
reach of the provincial protection exactly: <em>« La protection s'applique à l'extérieur des bâtiments
et au terrain. »</em> Exterior and ground, not interiors. The excavated collection was classed
separately, as an <em>objet patrimonial</em>, at the same time as the site.

<strong>What the sources will and will not say about the age of the Le Ber-Le Moyne house.</strong>
The RPCQ says the two stone buildings are contemporary, that masonry analysis confirmed it, and that
the house, "érigée à la fin du XVIIe siècle, […] est l'un des rares témoins de l'architecture rurale
de cette époque". The 2005 cahier says it dates from the French regime and is "l'une des plus
anciennes au Canada". Neither says it is the oldest complete building on the island of Montréal, and
this site does not say so either. What both do say is that a major restoration between 1981 and 1985
returned the house to its 17th-century appearance and that the museum reopened in 1985 — so what
stands is a restored 17th-century fabric, not an untouched one, and the 1768 rebuilding of the front
façade by the innkeeper Hugh Heney is part of its history whether or not it is now visible.

<strong>Parks Canada contradicts itself about the canal, and the contradiction is worth printing.
</strong> The Directory of Federal Heritage Designations record for the canal (id 627) gives the
designation date 1929-05-17 and a construction period of 1821 to 1825. The commemorative plaque text
carried on the same record says the canal <em>« fut inauguré en 1824 »</em> and <em>« Il fut fermé en
1971, 12 ans après l'ouverture de la Voie maritime du Saint-Laurent »</em>. The French statement of
value on that record says the canal was built <em>« à des fins commerciales en 1821-1825 »</em>; the
English version of the same paragraph says "built as a commercial canal in 1825-26". Parks Canada's
current public pages, meanwhile, say the works began in 1821 and the canal was finished in 1825, that
it closed in 1970, that it reopened to pleasure craft in 2002, and that 2025 is its bicentenary. This
site follows the current pages for 1825, 1970 and 2002, records 1824 and 1971 as the plaque's
readings, and notes that the 1907 Pinsoneault atlas and the 2005 cahier both take 1825 as the opening.
The length is no better settled: the Directory record says 14 kilometres, the navigation page says 14
kilometres of navigable channel and five locks, the visitor page says 14,5 km between lock no. 1 and
lock no. 5, and the multi-use trail is 13,5 km. The statutory record's figure — 14 km — is the one
encoded on the instrument row.

<strong>Where the type content comes from, and why there are only four cards.</strong> The 2005 cahier
characterises sectors, not building types. It has no per-type table, no fiches, and nothing like the
Gatineau or Lévis catalogues. But its description of sector 19.I.1 contains, in one sentence, a
four-item typology of the old town — village houses in wood; attached or terraced houses under
mansards and false mansards, flat roofs or two-slope roofs; small one-storey brick single-family
houses, wider than deep, with crowns; and detached or continuously aligned brick duplexes and
triplexes, sometimes with carriage gates — and sector 19.U.2 names a postwar type after the contractor
who built most of it. Those sentences, plus the paragraphs in 19.I.2, 19.I.6 and 19.I.8 that repeat
and extend them, are the whole documentary basis of the four records below. Every dimension the
cahier does not state — lot widths, setbacks, roof pitches in degrees, window proportions — is null.
The village house in wood is not carded here: it is the shared West Island record
<em>maison de tradition québécoise</em>, and the French-regime stone house is the shared record
<em>maison de pierre du Régime français</em>, to which Lachine contributes the Le Ber-Le Moyne
buildings and the 18th-century houses of the western shore.

<strong>The Vétérans ensemble is a sector here and not a type, deliberately.</strong> Sector 19.U.1 is
described in four lines: a small ensemble of dwellings intended for veterans, very homogeneous,
particularly well preserved, with a beginning tendency to densification and alteration. That is all.
No storeys, no roof form, no cladding, no plan. Veterans' housing is a real type and it has a record
elsewhere on this site; making one for Lachine out of this paragraph would mean inventing four of the
five columns, so the ensemble is recorded as a sector and left there.

<strong>The maison « Gameroff » is the most local name on this site.</strong> Sector 19.U.2 says the
catalogue of types deployed across the 1950s–1970s quarters is "particulièrement impressionnant" and
that among the contractors who built them the best known locally "a laissé au type architectural
dominant son appellation familière de maison « Gameroff »". The cahier does not give the contractor's
first name, the firm, the number of houses or the years, and none of those is asserted here. What it
does give is a description of the dominant form and one captioned example, "3, 40e avenue". The name
is a piece of vernacular naming that survived into a municipal document, and it is worth a card for
that alone.

<strong>The archaeological sites the cahier lists apart.</strong> Besides the three sectors of high
archaeological potential encoded above, § 3.2.3 C lists two <em>sites archéologiques</em> that are not
sectors: 19.A.1, the canal de Lachine, where remains associated with the upstream entrance walls of
the 1825 lock may lie under the north pavement of the boulevard Saint-Joseph or behind the fill of the
îlot Monk's north revetment wall; and 19.A.2, the maison mère des Sœurs de Sainte-Anne on the
boulevard Saint-Joseph, the community's first mother house and first convent. They are recorded here
rather than as rows, because the cahier does not treat them as sectors.
