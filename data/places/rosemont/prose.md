## intro
Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie is the borough the Canadian Pacific built, and the only place on this site with a house type its own occupants put up with their own hands. When the Angus Shops opened in 1904 — 3,000 men at start-up, 12,000 during each of the world wars, 31 principal buildings and 35 annexes, a fire station, a hospital, a sawmill and a bank of its own, <em>une ville dans la ville</em> — the promoters U.-H. Dandurand and Herbert Holt formed the Rosemont Land Improvement Company and cut the Crawford farm north of the shops into long blocks with lanes and lots all of one size. The 1907 Pinsoneault atlas, which the cahier reproduces, shows a single model repeated across the whole subdivision. What put a labourer on one of those lots was not a mortgage but a <em>promesse de vente</em> needing a few dollars down, and what he built on it was a one-storey flat-roofed box, small, cheap, made of whatever came to hand, with the roof left flat so that a second storey could go on top when he could afford it. Five hundred and sixty-one of those <em>maisons shoebox</em> are still standing, and since 2019 every one of them is named at its own address in an annexe to the zoning by-law. Around them the borough is the ordinary Montréal fabric — contiguous brick duplex and triplex with exterior stairs, denser and more ornate along rue Saint-Denis — with three later inflections: a co-operative garden suburb of detached houses inaugurated in 1942, two ensembles of semi-detached Art déco duplex with their garages at the back of the lot, and, at the eastern edge, 1950s bungalows that the cahier calls <em>une ambiance de banlieue en ville</em>.

## phase:p1
The boulevard Rosemont follows the old chemin de la côte de la Visitation, opened to settlement by the Sulpicians early in the eighteenth century and farmed by the settlers' descendants into the twentieth. Limestone was known on the côte from 1780 and worked for building stone — Montréal's grey stone — and later for the crushed rock that surfaced the roads until the middle of the twentieth century. The winding rue des Carrières, which led to the quarries, is the only surviving witness and the borough's one <em>tracé fondateur d'intérêt patrimonial</em>; its line still breaks the orthogonal grid. Nothing residential survives from the period.

## phase:p2
La Petite-Patrie, named for Claude Jasmin's autobiographical novel, took its urban structure between 1885 and 1930 with the tramway. Francophones and Italian Montrealers settled the southern part from 1895, the latter keeping kitchen gardens and poultry; Notre-Dame-de-la-Défense followed early in the new century and Casa Italia decades later. The fabric is contiguous brick plex of one to three storeys, mostly duplex and triplex, with flat roofs, exterior stairs, balconies, decorative <em>couronnements</em> and artificial-stone inserts, and serially produced ornament repeated down each row. Rue Saint-Denis got the expensive version — grey stone on the façade more often, more refined detail, stained glass — and the cahier rates that street a sector of exceptional value on the strength of its plex alone. In 1931 the marché du Nord, now Jean-Talon, was built on the old Shamrock lacrosse grounds as Depression relief work and became the busiest market in Montréal.

## phase:p3
The Angus period, and the borough's own type. The shops drew thousands of workers to cheap peripheral lots, and the tramway and the growing road network meant a man no longer had to live beside the factory that employed him. The 1920s made Rosemont one of the most active Montréal quarters for house-building. The <em>shoebox</em> belongs to the beginning of that span and kept being built, through several phases, into the 1960s. Alongside it the standard plex filled the Rosemont Land grid — contiguous duplex and triplex close to the property line, streets at right angles, lanes to the back of every lot, a grid the cahier says is essentially unaltered — and at the parc Campbell the same builders produced the opposite answer, contiguous duplex with the stair brought inside and the two front doors paired at ground level.

## phase:p4
Two things happen at once. In 1942, during the celebrations of the three hundredth anniversary of the founding of Ville-Marie, the Cité-Jardin du Tricentenaire is inaugurated east of parc Maisonneuve: detached single-family houses on streets each planted with one species of tree and named after it, with a footpath network kept clear of the roads. The cahier calls it an arrangement unique in Montréal and an important witness of the City Beautiful movement, and records without ceremony that it was only partly built. Meanwhile the borough's post-war growth, driven by the demographic boom and the housing shortage, throws up whole streets at a time, and two of them — north of Saint-Zotique and near the Centre civique — are uniform enough that the cahier infers a single promoter for each: semi-detached duplex in brick with flat roofs, driveways running back to garages at the end of the lot, entrances under wrought-iron marquises hung on chains, ornamental motifs in artificial stone, and glass block lighting the interior stairwell.

## phase:p5
Angus declines through the 1960s and 1970s and closes for good in 1992; the site is split between a residential and an industrial redevelopment, and three buildings survive. Nouveau Rosemont fills in with 1950s bungalows. Then the pressure the 2004 cahier had already named — <em>la tendance est à la densification des quartiers, ce qui met en péril l'existence des petites maisons d'un seul étage qui témoignent d'un mode d'occupation du territoire en voie de disparition</em> — arrives in the land market. In May 2018 the borough council votes a moratorium on new demolition applications for shoebox houses. In December 2018 it publishes Annexe F, an address-by-address characterisation of all 561. On 12 February 2019 it adopts by-law 01-279-58 in third and final reading, the first Montréal borough to protect the type. Owners in thirty-nine zones force signature registers on the contested provisions; the borough carries them zone by zone in thirty-nine separate by-laws, 01-279-58-01 to 01-279-58-39, and on 27 March 2019 the whole territory is covered.

## notes
<strong>The arrondissement code is 26, read from inside the cahier.</strong> The file is <em>21_evaluation_patrimoine_rose.pdf</em> and every sector code inside it reads 26. Its PDF Title metadata still says "maq1_CŽte StLuc Hampstead", left over from the template. Two independent reasons, in one document, never to infer a code from a filename. The cahier also cites 14.AP.1, which confirms that Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension is code 14.

<strong>The E-sector list was flagged incomplete and was.</strong> The Part 10 memo carried three; the cahier carries eleven, 26.E.1 to 26.E.11, plus seven I sectors, four U ensembles urbains, one N ensemble industriel and three AP archaeological sectors — twenty-six coded records, all of them enumerated in <em>sectors.yaml</em>. The cahier's one <em>tracé fondateur d'intérêt patrimonial</em>, the rue des Carrières, is described in the phase text above but is deliberately not encoded as a sector: it is a route rather than a sector, it sits outside the E/I/N/U/AP grammar in the cahier's own structure, it carries no code, and none of the sector values this site defines describes it without saying something false about it.

<strong>The grading counts were corrected against the primary document.</strong> The research memo gave the three architectural-value classes as 69 / 258 / 235, which sums to 562. Parsing Annexe F itself, row by row, gives 561 rows and 170 / 233 / 158 — exactly the distribution the Ville de Montréal's own article page publishes as 30.3 %, 41.5 % and 28.2 %. The memo's other Annexe F figure is confirmed exactly: 56 houses, just under ten per cent, are built <em>en fonds de lot</em>. Two further Annexe F columns that no summary mentions are also counted here: 118 houses carry the mention <em>couronnement de qualité</em>, and 232 stand next to another shoebox.

<strong>The value scale runs the opposite way to every other grading on this site.</strong> Westmount's I* is its best and its III its plainest; Île d'Orléans runs A to E, best first; Lévis and Terrebonne likewise. Rosemont's runs 1 to 3 with 3 the best — <em>une maison ayant une valeur de 1 présente le moins de caractéristiques architecturales d'intérêt</em>. Anyone reading two of these pages side by side will invert the scale without noticing, so it is stated here and again in the grading block itself.

<strong>The scale is not advisory.</strong> Article 5 of by-law 01-279 defines a <em>maison shoebox d'intérêt</em> as a house on the Annexe F list with an architectural value of 2 or 3, and that defined term is what triggers Chapitre VIII and PIIA fascicule 10. Article 135.1 then attaches development rights to the same number: only usage categories H.1 to H.3 are permitted on a shoebox lot — one, two or three dwellings — and where the house is value 3, only H.1. The most interesting shoeboxes are therefore worth the least to a redeveloper, which is the mechanism, not a side effect.

<strong>Shoebox houses have no status under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel.</strong> None is classed, none is cited, none sits in a declared or classed heritage site. Everything protecting them is municipal and lives in a zoning by-law: a defined term in article 5, an address list in Annexe F, dimensional rules in articles 11.1, 26.1 and 52.1, a compulsory <em>couronnement</em> in article 100.1, a usage cap in article 135.1, a fascicule of PIIA criteria, and a demolition by-law. Compare Mercier–Hochelaga-Maisonneuve, where the ancienne Cité-de-Maisonneuve carries a provincial <em>classement</em>: same city, opposite instrument. It is also worth saying that the borough had to invent this. The 2004 cahier saw the threat and named it, but the Évaluation series had no mechanism for protecting a building type as such — only sectors and individually listed buildings — and the shoebox is neither concentrated enough to be a sector nor grand enough to be listed.

<strong>Why the regulation exists, in one transaction.</strong> In September 2019 <em>La Presse</em> reported a couple who had paid $500,000 for an uninhabited shoebox in Rosemont in order to demolish it and build a single-family house of more than $700,000 in its place; the project reached roughly $1.3 million, with the excavation alone rising from $15,000 to $45,000 once 800 holes had to be drilled to weaken the rock. Nothing on this site illustrates more plainly what type-level regulation is for: the house was worth nothing and the lot was worth half a million, and no grading of individual buildings by architectural merit would have caught it, because the building was never the point.

<strong>Two figures that are press, not municipal.</strong> The island-wide count — around a thousand shoebox houses in Montréal, of which about half are in Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie — comes from <em>La Presse</em> of 19 October 2018 and is reported here as press. The municipal record supports only the borough figure, 561, and that one is exact because it is a list of addresses. The $500,000 / $700,000 transaction above is likewise press. No commercial real-estate site has been used for any date or count on this page.

<strong>One type, or two?</strong> Rosemont treats the shoebox as a single type spanning the early twentieth century to the 1960s. The adjoining borough of Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension splits the same object into a <em>shoebox du début du 20e siècle</em> and a <em>shoebox du milieu du 20e siècle</em>, and proposes calling it a <em>uniplex</em> instead — a single-family member of the plex family. Two boroughs of one city, working from the same building stock in the same years, disagree about whether this is one type or two and about what to call it. The disagreement is kept visible rather than resolved.

<strong>A note on the dépliant.</strong> The definition quoted throughout the shoebox record is the Ville de Montréal's, but the dépliant carrying it — <em>Maisons de type « shoebox » — Guide pour les travaux et agrandissements</em>, 2019, source credited to Isabelle Bouchard, M. Sc. A. conservation — is published by Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension, not by Rosemont. Its statistical tables (1,159 houses by decade and by district, 46.9 % of them built between 1950 and 1960) are VSP's and are not used on this page.

<strong>Zoning.</strong> Rosemont's base by-law is <strong>01-279</strong>. The Part 10 memo's own correction is adopted and repeated here because the two numbers are easy to swap: <strong>règlement 01-283-124 is VSP's, not Rosemont's</strong>, and VSP's base by-law is 01-283.

<strong>One error in the cahier is recorded rather than silently fixed.</strong> The text of sector 26.I.5 refers the reader to "26.E.4" for the site du patrimoine de l'église Saint-Esprit. That site is 26.E.7; 26.E.4 is the Saint-Ambroise institutional group. The cahier's list of exceptional buildings also dates the citation 29-01-91 where the sector fiche says the site was constituted in 1990.
