## intro

Dorval is a single road with four centuries stacked along it. The chemin du Bord-du-Lac follows the shore of lac Saint-Louis from one end of the territory to the other, and the 2005 <em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em> calls it the first overland route laid out in the west of the island — <em>« son tracé recoupe l'ensemble des terres agricoles »</em>, the line that cuts across every farm lot. The lots ran back from the water at right angles, seigneurial fashion, and the oldest houses sit where that survey put them.

The Sulpicians established a mission on this shore in 1667 and called it Gentilly; the name became La Présentation de la Vierge Marie, and then, after Jean-Baptiste Bouchard dit d'Orval took the land in 1691, Dorval. Farming held the territory into the twentieth century. What changed it was the railway. The Grand Trunk reached Dorval in 1855, and by the end of the century the banks of lac Saint-Louis in the west of the island had become, in the cahier's phrase, <em>« des lieux de villégiature attrayants pour la société bourgeoise montréalaise »</em>. Summer people and farmers used the same village. Sailing, horse racing and golf arrived with them: the St. Lawrence Yacht Club was founded in 1888 and received the royal designation from Queen Victoria in 1894; the Forest and Stream Club took over Alfred Brown's stone manor Bel-Air in 1888; the Royal Montreal Golf Club is still at 100 boulevard Bouchard.

Then the airport. Dorval's airfield opened in 1941 and, in the cahier's own words, <em>« change radicalement l'utilisation du sol et le rôle de cette municipalité sur l'île de Montréal »</em>. Air traffic grew fast after the war, the rest of the territory was opened to subdivision, and the population went from under 3,000 in the 1940s to more than 20,000 by 1971. Eight of the cahier's twenty-four sectors are 1950s bungalow subdivisions, and the borough's densest concentration of described housing is postwar, not pre-war.

The village survived all of it, and it is worth saying why the record here is thin. The cahier grades territory and lists addresses; it publishes no type table, no fiche, no measured description of a Dorval house. Its most precise piece of typology is a sentence about three bungalow models on avenue Prince-Charles distinguished by roof form. That is why Dorval carries no type record of its own on this site, and why the shared West Island records — the villégiature villa and the postwar bungalow — carry it instead.

## phase:p1

The Sulpicians become seigneurs of the island of Montréal in 1663, and in 1667 establish a mission on the Dorval shore and on the île Dorval. The cahier is blunt about why the west end mattered: <em>« tout le secteur ouest de l'île de Montréal joue un rôle militaire et commercial stratégique »</em>. The territory is called Gentilly and then La Présentation before it takes the name of Jean-Baptiste Bouchard Dorval, who buys the land in 1691. The three islands offshore are named Courcelles from 1665; the largest is chosen as the site of a mission to Indigenous people in 1673, an idea abandoned the following year, and between 1673 and 1674 it is a seigneury distinct from that of the island of Montréal. The pointe de Valois, commanding the whole of lac Saint-Louis, is rated by the cahier's archaeologists as very high potential for a fort or a mission of this period. The oldest house in the exceptional list, the maison Jacques-Morin at 66 rue Allan-Point, is dated <em>vers 1675</em>.

## phase:p2

Agriculture is the principal economic activity from the eighteenth century to the twentieth. The lotting is seigneurial and perpendicular to the lake, and the buildings that survive from it are stone houses strung along the Bord-du-Lac and the old montées: the maison Jacques-Lepage dit Roy at 2 terrasse Ballantyne, <em>vers</em> 1790; the maison Michel or Allard at 2095 chemin du Bord-du-Lac, <em>vers</em> 1800; Quatre-Vents at 12 avenue Dalhia, 1803, which the 2024 heritage study calls one of the first stone constructions in Dorval, on one of the first lots of the Domaine La Présentation; the maison Paul-Picard at 2 avenue Martin, 1802; L'Ermitage at 4 avenue Martin, <em>vers</em> 1832; the maison André-Legault-dit-Deslauriers, called le Manoir, at 940 chemin du Bord-du-Lac, <em>vers</em> 1833; the maison Jean-Baptiste-Legault at 1549 avenue Deslauriers, <em>vers</em> 1830; the maison Jean-Baptiste-Monette at 690 avenue Monette, <em>vers</em> 1850. In 1853 the île Dorval is bought by Sir George Simpson, governor of the Hudson's Bay Company, who builds his summer house on it in the middle of the forest — the first villégiature act on this shore, two years before the railway.

## phase:p3

The Grand Trunk reaches Dorval in 1855 and the character of the place changes. The lakeshore fills with the summer houses of wealthy anglophone Montrealers; the village serves both them and the farmers. The local post office takes the name Dorval in 1878; the village municipality is incorporated under the same name in 1892; the Catholic parish of La Présentation is canonically erected in 1895; the town follows in 1903. Institutions of leisure arrive with the summer people — the St. Lawrence Yacht Club in 1888, royal from 1894, whose green boat shed of that period is the only building left to speak for its beginnings; the Forest and Stream Club from 1888 in Alfred Brown's Bel-Air of 1874; the Royal Montreal Golf Club. The village core takes its commercial shape along the Bord-du-Lac: the maison Charles-Décary of 1875 at 223, the magasin Joseph-Décary of 1888 at 365, the first hôtel de Dorval of 1887 at 444. Aqua Vista at 2120 dates from <em>vers</em> 1865 and the maison Carrière at 2205 from 1864. In 1911 the île Dorval is divided into fifty-seven lots for summer houses; the maison Eliza-Bryson of 1919 and the maison Minnie-Louise-Davis (Scott) of 1922 close the sequence. The jardins Strathmore, a Marcil Trust Company subdivision with streets on arcs of a circle, is laid out in 1926 and only half built.

## phase:p4

The airport opens in 1941 and everything after it is suburb. The population passes from under 3,000 in the 1940s to more than 20,000 in 1971; the town becomes a cité in 1956; a new air terminal is inaugurated in 1960. The cahier's eight ensembles urbains d'intérêt are all of this period and all residential: the Dorval Model Homes on avenue Dalhia, begun 1951, twenty-eight units in twelve detached and sixteen semi-detached bungalows plus two apartment buildings at the Bord-du-Lac corner; the plain-pied bungalows with hipped roofs and cul-de-sac streets on rue McNicoll and at place Décary; the split-levels north of avenue Dawson; the Californian-style low bungalows with carports on the wide, median-divided avenues Claude and Allard; the three brick models of avenue Prince-Charles; and, on avenue Saint-Louis, a northern section of War Time Housing. In the same decades the big properties go: nothing survives of Ashburton, and John Wilson McConnell's stone manor at the cap Saint-Gilles burned in 1963. Dorval becomes a borough of the new Ville de Montréal on 1 January 2002.

## phase:p5

Dorval spends four years as a Montréal borough, and it is in that window that its heritage is written down: the <em>Évaluation du patrimoine urbain</em> volume is researched from January 2003 to May 2004 and published in 2005, by which time the referendums of 20 June 2004 under Bill 9 have already been held. Dorval is reconstituted as a cité on 1 January 2006. The Musée d'histoire et du patrimoine de Dorval opens in 2002 in the Forest and Stream Club's stables of 1874 at 1850 chemin du Bord-du-Lac. Municipal instruments follow the merger rather than the province: a PIIA by-law, RCM-60C-2015 and then RCM-60I-2024, listing thirty-two heritage addresses; a demolition by-law, RCM-97-2023; and commissioned heritage studies, of which the 2024 expertise on Quatre-Vents at 12 avenue Dahlia is the most substantial. Nothing in Dorval has been classed or cited under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel.

## notes

<strong>Dorval has no protection at ensemble scale, and none at building scale either.</strong> This page records <code>protection: none-at-ensemble-scale</code> because that is what three independent checks say. First, the RPCQ's <em>Patrimoine protégé et valorisé</em> section — the part of the Répertoire that lists every element carrying a <em>statut</em> under the <em>Loi sur le patrimoine culturel</em> — returns <em>« Résultat de la recherche (0) »</em> for "Dorval". The identical query for "Senneville" returns four records, so the search works and the zero is a real zero, not a failed query. Second, the 2024 heritage study commissioned by the Cité itself for 12 avenue Dahlia states the position for that property in three tiers and finds nothing at any of them: <em>« Au niveau fédéral, il n'existe aucune reconnaissance »</em>; provincially, three related records exist but are <em>inventoriés</em> only, and the study adds the rule that matters — <em>« un bâtiment inventorié ne relève pas de l'autorité du Gouvernement du Québec ni du ministère de la Culture et des Communications »</em>; municipally, the property is named in the 2005 cahier and in the PIIA list, both of which are instruments of knowledge and design review, not of protection. Third, the 2005 cahier's own recommendation 4.3 asked the Ville to <em>« évaluer l'intérêt pan-montréalais des secteurs et des bâtiments d'intérêt patrimonial exceptionnel en vue de procéder à la création de sites du patrimoine et à la citation de bâtiments en vertu de la Loi sur les biens culturels du Québec »</em> and named eleven candidates — 940 chemin du Bord-du-Lac, 1 and 2 and 4 avenue Martin, 1800 chemin du Bord-du-Lac, 2 croissant Elliot, 2095 chemin du Bord-du-Lac, 2 terrasse Ballantyne, 66 rue Allan-Point, 12 avenue Dalhia and 1549 avenue Deslauriers. Twenty-one years later, not one of them has been cited.

<strong>The PIIA says two Dorval buildings are "cités" in the RPCQ. They are not.</strong> Article 56 of RCM-60I-2024 reads: <em>« la Cité de Dorval héberge deux bâtiments cités au répertoire du patrimoine culturel du Québec, soit le Centre culturel Peter-B.-Yeomans et la bibliothèque de Dorval, tous deux construits en 1968, ainsi que l'Église Strathmore United (310, avenue Brookhaven), édifiée en 1956 »</em>. The RPCQ record for the centre and the library, id 216208, carries the statut <em>Inventorié</em> with no authority and no date, and its only inventory of record is the <em>Inventaire des projets du Centenaire de la Confédération canadienne réalisés au Québec (2014-2019)</em>. <em>Cité</em> in the by-law's sentence means "listed in", not <em>citation</em> in the sense of article 127 of the <em>Loi sur le patrimoine culturel</em>. The same record also dates the building differently from the by-law: construction began in February 1967, the centre opened that October and was inaugurated on 18 November 1967, not 1968.

<strong>Two lists of heritage buildings that do not match.</strong> The 2005 cahier's list of <em>immeubles de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle</em> holds twenty-two entries; article 59 of the 2024 PIIA holds thirty-two addresses. They overlap heavily along the chemin du Bord-du-Lac and on avenue Martin, but the PIIA adds four churches, a round cafeteria at 479 avenue Mousseau-Vermette, the Foyer Dorval and the hôtel de ville, while dropping several of the cahier's houses — 375 chemin du Bord-du-Lac (the maison Jean-Baptiste-Décary of 1846) and 1200-1210 (the maison Ashburton of 1879) among them. Neither list is a count of heritage buildings on the ground, and they are recorded separately on the place record rather than merged.

<strong>The street is spelled two ways in Dorval's own documents.</strong> The 2005 cahier spells it <em>avenue Dalhia</em> — in the heading of ensemble 7.U.2, in its photograph captions, and in the exceptional-immeubles entry <em>« 12, avenue Dalhia / Quatre-Vents (1803) »</em>. The 2024 PIIA and the 2024 heritage study both spell it <em>avenue Dahlia</em>. This page keeps each source's own spelling in the rows that quote it and does not silently harmonise them.

<strong>Two accounts of Alfred Brown.</strong> The cahier describes Bel-Air at 1800 chemin du Bord-du-Lac as the stone manor <em>« nom donné par l'architecte Alfred Brown à son manoir de pierre de style anglais »</em>. The Société des musées du Québec, describing the stables next door that became the museum, calls him <em>« Alfred S. Brown, magasinier de la compagnie de chemin de fer Grand Tronc et directeur de la Banque de Montréal »</em>. Architect, or railway storekeeper and bank director: the two sources are not reconciled here, and both are recorded. The dates are not in dispute — 1874 for the house and stables, 1888 for the club's acquisition, per recommendation 4.3 of the cahier.

<strong>Dorval and Senneville are the same story told with different money.</strong> Both are shore territories whose fabric was made by the railway and by Montréal's anglophone commercial class between 1855 and 1930; both keep the road that organised them; both carry the same villa type. The difference is what happened afterwards. Senneville's estate belt is a National Historic Site of Canada and its fort ruins are a <em>site patrimonial classé</em>; Dorval's equivalents were subdivided, burned or built over, and it has no statutory protection of any kind. The pattern this part of the site is about — that designation tracks legibility, survival and local politics rather than architectural quality — has its clearest West Island illustration in the twelve kilometres between these two places.
