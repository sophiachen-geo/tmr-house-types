## intro

Senneville is a road with almost nothing visible from it. The chemin de Senneville runs along the shore of the lac des Deux-Montagnes at the western tip of the island of Montréal, and behind the trees on either side of it lie about a dozen country estates that between them hold the densest concentration of Arts and Crafts domestic architecture in Canada. Parks Canada's own list of what makes the district what it is includes, without irony, <em>"the siting and landscaping of most residences, which prevents a view to the buildings from chemin Senneville."</em> The invisibility is a designated heritage attribute.

The fief was conceded in 1672 to Michel-Sidrac Dugué, sieur de Boisbriand — a name that survives as the Abbott and Clouston estate, spelled <em>Boisbriand</em> by the RPCQ and by the Ville de Montréal cahier and <em>Bois-Briant</em> by Parks Canada — and sold in 1679 to Jacques Le Ber, from whom the place takes its name. Le Ber raised a fortified wooden mill in 1686, saw it burned in an Iroquois raid in 1691, and rebuilt it in stone under a contract of 1700; his son Jacques Le Ber de Senneville (1663–1735) built the private stone fort of a house, four corner bastions and a walled court in 1702–03. The RPCQ calls Fort Senneville an <em>« exemple unique de fort privé en Nouvelle-France »</em>. Benedict Arnold's troops destroyed it in 1776. For the two centuries after that the territory was farms fronting the water, and — this is the point the 2005 cahier makes and no summary should drop — it never produced a village core at all: <em>« sans toutefois donner naissance à un noyau villageois, les résidants utilisant plutôt l'église et les commerces de Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue »</em>.

What made the place was the railway and the money it carried west. In the cahier's words, <em>« Au cours de la seconde moitié du XIXe siècle, le chemin de fer est prolongé jusque dans l'ouest de l'île, ce qui amène la bourgeoisie anglophone montréalaise à construire des villas en milieu agricole. Certains optent toutefois pour l'acquisition de fermes tout en laissant l'exploitation des terres à des agriculteurs. »</em> Between about 1865 and 1930 the founders, presidents and directors of the Bank of Montreal and the Canadian Pacific bought the farms: Sir John Joseph Caldwell Abbott, mayor of Montréal and Canada's third prime minister; the banker Sir Edward Seaborne Clouston; the senator and stockbroker Louis-Joseph Forget; the banker Richard Bladworth Angus; the McGill parasitologist John Lancelot Todd; the collector Frederick Cleveland Morgan. They employed a small circle of architects — above all the brothers Edward and William Sutherland Maxwell, with George Cutler Shattuck; Percy Erskine Nobbs with George Taylor Hyde; Robert Findlay; David Shennan — and landscape designers Frederick G. Todd and Frederick Law Olmsted. More than thirty major projects; twenty surviving Maxwell buildings, which the federal record calls <em>"a unique example in a single location of their work."</em>

Senneville was incorporated as a village in 1895, when it and Baie-D'Urfé broke away from the parish of Sainte-Anne, and its first council met on 9 May 1896 under its first mayor, Louis-Joseph Forget — the man who was then building Bois-de-la-Roche. It was merged into Montréal in 2002 and reconstituted on 1 January 2006.

## phase:p1

The fief of Senneville is conceded in 1672 to Michel-Sidrac Dugué, sieur de Boisbriand, who settles a small number of colonists but whose interests are mainly in the fur trade. It is sold to Jacques Le Ber in 1679 with no mention of buildings; Charles Le Moyne cedes his share in 1683, leaving Le Ber sole holder. A wooden store for trade with Indigenous people seems to stand on the fief by that date, though where is unknown. With the Sulpicians' permission Le Ber puts up a fortified wooden mill in 1686 — the presence of a mill is an argument for settlers, at a moment when the Iroquois are attacking the French outposts of the west of the island — and it is burned in a raid in 1691. The mill is rebuilt in stone under a construction contract of 1700, and three years later Le Ber's son, Jacques Le Ber de Senneville (1663–1735), builds a stone fort of a house, four corner bastions and a walled court on the riverside terrace of the present pointe Abbott. The RPCQ reads the fort as a witness to a trade that was largely illicit, royal ordinances then restricting trade with Indigenous people outside the main centres; the reopening of the trade toward the <em>pays d'En Haut</em> in 1725 makes the fort obsolete. It is destroyed in 1776 by Benedict Arnold's troops during the American invasion — the archaeologists found fragments of exploded grenades that explain how.

## phase:p2

Farms. The land is divided into lots fronting the water, the houses and their outbuildings standing along the chemin de Senneville, and the territory serves exclusively agriculture until the middle of the nineteenth century. No parish, no village, no church: Senneville's residents use Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue's. Two stone farmhouses of this order survive inside what is now the parc agricole du Bois-de-la-Roche and are the oldest buildings the Ville de Montréal's 2018 heritage statement names — the maisons Eustache-Rouleau and Joseph-Lalonde, <em>« des maisons de pierres remarquables pour leur ancienneté, construites par le maçon Charles Brunet au début du XIXe siècle »</em>, each with a stone carré clearing the ground-floor level, a two-slope roof, wooden doors and windows, a date stone and carved corbels. Senneville's own PIIA guide dates the maison Eustache-Rouleau at 294 chemin de Senneville to 1836.

## phase:p3

The railway is extended into the west of the island in the second half of the nineteenth century and the estate belt follows. In 1865 the Montréal lawyer John Joseph Caldwell Abbott buys the point where the fort ruins stand and lays it out as a <em>domaine champêtre</em>, keeping the ruins; in 1898 the banker Edward Seaborne Clouston acquires the property, then called Boisbriand, renovates the buildings and leaves the ruins alone precisely for their picturesque romanticism. Frederick G. Todd, working with Frederick Law Olmsted, designs the grounds of the Abbott/Clouston estate; Olmsted also designs the grounds of the Forget estate. Edward Maxwell builds Bois-de-la-Roche for Senator Louis-Joseph Forget in 1899 and renovates the old Senneville mill about 1898; Nobbs and Hyde build the J.L. Todd house, its ancillary buildings and its landscaped grounds at 180 in 1911–13; David Shennan builds the Frederick Cleveland Morgan house at 264 in 1912. The village is created in 1895 and holds its first council on 9 May 1896 under Forget. By 1930 the belt holds about a dozen estates and at least eighty-two buildings, and, as the federal record notes, because they were made by a few owners using a few designers over a concentrated period, they have <em>"a strong formal and stylistic coherence."</em>

## phase:p4

The estates stop being built and start being subdivided. The Morgan Arboretum, formerly estate land, is dated 1912–45 on the federal list and is integrated into Macdonald College in the 1940s. Beside the Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue veterans' hospital, built in 1945, the Senneville Lodge convalescent buildings go up. From the first half of the twentieth century onward a postwar subdivision of nearly two hundred houses fills the southern part of the village on an orthogonal grid that contrasts sharply with the organic development of the rest — streets without sidewalks and with ditches, mature vegetation, deep setbacks, and houses that, the cahier says, <em>« reprend très souvent des éléments de composition des grandes propriétés et de l'architecture rurale de l'ancienne municipalité de Senneville : murs de pierre, formes des toits, bâtiments ancillaires et aménagements paysagers »</em>. The Pine Bluff residence, R.B. Angus's Maxwell house, is demolished in 1950; five of its outbuildings remain. In 1991 the Communauté urbaine de Montréal buys most of the Forget family farm to make the parc agricole du Bois-de-la-Roche.

## phase:p5

The Historic Sites and Monuments Board of Canada designates the Senneville Historic District in 2001, and the recognition is formally dated 2002-07-18. Québec classes the Fort-Senneville ruins as a <em>site patrimonial</em> on 20 November 2003 — 8,000 m² out of 1,400 acres, and still the only statutory protection in the village. Senneville is merged into the Ville de Montréal on 1 January 2002 and reconstituted as a village on 1 January 2006 after the referendums of 20 June 2004 under Bill 9. The Ville de Montréal publishes an <em>énoncé de l'intérêt patrimonial</em> for the Bois-de-la-Roche site on 13 February 2018; the village's PIIA by-law 452 gains its <em>Guide des principes architecturaux</em> in 2019, drawn for a new subdivision but opening with the first published account of Senneville's house architecture as a set of types. In 2019 Héritage Montréal opposes demolition permits for the surviving Pine Bluff dependencies.

## notes

<strong>The designation year is 2001, and the same record also says 2002.</strong> Parks Canada's record, id 1973 federally and id 4442 on the Canadian Register, carries both figures in different fields, and they are not in conflict once you see what each is. The heritage-value text — the part that states the reasons for designation — begins <em>"Senneville Historic District was designated a national historic site of Canada in 2001 because:"</em> and ends <em>"Source: Historic Sites and Monuments Board of Canada, Minutes, 2001."</em> That is the Board's decision. The Recognition Details block at the foot of the same page gives <em>Recognition Date: 2002/07/18</em>, the ministerial recognition that follows a Board recommendation, and <em>Listed on the Canadian Register: 2008/04/16</em>. This page publishes <strong>2001</strong>, the year the record's own prose gives in both languages — the French text reads <em>« L'Arrondissement historique de Senneville a été désigné lieu historique national du Canada en 2001 »</em> — and footnotes the 2002 claim, which is what Héritage Montréal's Memento and other secondary sources print, and which is the 2002-07-18 field read as though it were the designation.

<strong>The landscape attribution is to Frederick Law Olmsted — the elder, or the firm.</strong> The federal record's sentence is: <em>"Landscape architect and urban planner Frederick G. Todd (1876-1948), working with American landscape designer Frederick Law Olmsted, designed the grounds of the Abbott/Clouston estate. Olmsted also designed the grounds of the Forget estate."</em> The French text says the same — <em>« en collaboration avec le paysagiste américain Frederick Law Olmsted »</em>. The record carries no generational suffix and neither does this page; the commonly repeated attribution to the son is not supported by any source read here. The Ville de Montréal cahier's list of designers for sector 2.E.4 is <em>« Maxwell, Findlay, Hyde, Nobbs, Olmsted, Todd, Rose »</em> — also unqualified, and adding a seventh name, Rose, that appears in none of the other sources consulted and is recorded here without further identification.

<strong>How many named estates there are, and why the answer is not eight.</strong> The Part 11 brief calls for "Senneville's eight named estates" and then lists seven — Dow, Hillcote, the J.L. Todd house and stable, Chatblanc, Bois-de-la-Roche, Pine Bluff and Le Sabot. Counted from the sources rather than from the brief, the number is larger and depends on which source you count. Parks Canada's character-defining elements name <strong>twelve</strong> estate entries with addresses on the chemin de Senneville, eleven of them carrying a proper house name and one — the house at 246, of 1894 — carrying only an address: Dow (140), Hillcote (149-65), Bois-Briant (168-70), the J.L. Todd estate (180), Chatblanc (200), Bally Bawn (202), the R.B. Angus estate (216-19), Wanklyn (238), Birchfield (240), 246, Le Bousquet (264) and the Forget estate at 292. The Ville de Montréal cahier's list of <em>immeubles de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle</em> names <strong>twenty-five</strong> addresses on the chemin de Senneville, sixteen of them with a proper house name, and adds Château rose (26), the maison William-George-MacVicar-Stuart (31), the maison Crevier (108), the maison John C-Abbott (170), the propriété Abbot (178), the tour d'eau James-Morgan (249), the maison Eustache-Rouleau (294), the maison Lalonde (296) and the maison Guy-Boyer ou Blanche-Forget (330). Eight is not a figure either source produces.

<strong>The two names of the house at 264, and the two addresses of Bois-de-la-Roche.</strong> Parks Canada calls 264 chemin de Senneville <em>"the Frederick Cleveland Morgan house, 'Le Bousquet' (264 Senneville; 1912)"</em>; the Ville cahier calls it simply <em>la maison Frederick-Cleveland-Morgan</em>; Héritage Montréal and the Commons category call the same 1912 David Shennan house <strong>Le Sabot</strong>. All three refer to one building and this page uses the address rather than choosing between the names. Bois-de-la-Roche looks like a similar conflict and is not one: the Ville cahier lists <em>« 290, chemin de Senneville — Maison Louis-Joseph-Forget ou Bois-de-la-Roche »</em> and a separate, unnamed <em>292</em>; Parks Canada attaches the whole Forget estate, chapel, cowshed and ancillary buildings to <em>292</em>; and the Ville's archive catalogues the Maxwell barn-stable as <em>292B chemin de Senneville</em>. The house is at 290, the farm group at 292, and both numbers are correct.

<strong>Pine Bluff is real, and the main house is gone.</strong> The name does not appear in the federal record, which lists only <em>"five ancillary buildings on the R.B. Angus estate (216-19 Senneville; 1895-1903; Peach House, a small country house, the caretaker's house, a greenhouse and a barn)"</em>. It does appear in the Ville de Montréal cahier, whose exceptional list reads <em>« 216-219, chemin de Senneville — Dépendances de la maison R.-B.-Angus, Pine Bluff »</em> — dependencies, note, not the house. Héritage Montréal records that the main residence was demolished in 1950 and that in 2019 it asked the village to refuse demolition permits for what is left. The federal record lists the dependencies rather than the house because by 2001 the house had been gone for half a century.

<strong>One thousand four hundred acres designated, eight thousand square metres protected.</strong> This is the sharpest instance in this part of the site of designation and protection being different things. The National Historic Site covers "more than 1400 acres" and confers no permit power over private land; it names, it records, it plaques. The only element in Senneville carrying a <em>statut</em> under the <em>Loi sur le patrimoine culturel</em> is the Fort-Senneville ruins — <em>« un terrain en partie boisé d'environ 8000 mètres carrés »</em>, classed on 20 November 2003, privately owned and closed to visitors. The searchable protected-heritage section of the RPCQ returns four Senneville records, and three of them are commemorative plaques. What actually governs change on the estates is the village's own PIIA by-law 452, whose reach the Ville de Montréal describes in one sentence: it <em>« reconnaît l'intérêt patrimonial de plusieurs des bâtiments d'intérêt du site ainsi que des murets de pierre »</em>.

<strong>The village's own history page says things this page does not publish.</strong> Senneville's official chronology dates the stone windmill to 1686 (consistent with the cahier), the fort to 1703 (a year earlier than the RPCQ's 1702–1703 construction span), Arnold's burning of the fort to 1777 (the RPCQ says 1776), and records a "heritage declaration" of the Bois-de-la-Roche site in 2017. No instrument answering that description was found: the Ville de Montréal document is an <em>énoncé de l'intérêt patrimonial</em> dated 13 February 2018, which is a statement of value and not a designation, and no classement or citation exists for the site. The 2005 cahier is also internally inconsistent, dating <em>« les vestiges du fort et du moulin de Senneville »</em> to 1692 and 1700 in its phase summary while its own archaeology section gives 1686, 1691, 1700 and 1703. Where a municipal or promotional source and a statutory record disagree, this site follows the statutory record and says so.

<strong>Senneville is a rural-seigneurial place, and the sources insist on it.</strong> It sits in the same section of this site as the Île d'Orléans and Charlesbourg because its organising unit is the farm lot, not the street: the 2005 cahier describes even its sector of new houses, 2.E.7, as sited <em>« sur un lotissement hérité du régime seigneurial »</em>, and the 2018 énoncé praises the parc agricole's <em>« parcellaire d'origine, toujours lisible »</em>. What is unusual is that the estates were laid over that parcellaire without erasing it. The lots stayed; the farms in several cases kept working, with the new owners leaving cultivation to farmers; and the picturesque grounds were composed around field boundaries, stone walls and orchards that were already there. That is why the barn-stable at 292 is designed by the Maxwells in Shingle Style and why the Bois-de-la-Roche heritage statement can list a seventeenth-century land division and an Arts and Crafts cold-store cellar as elements of one thing.
