## intro

Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue sits on the last point of the island of Montréal, where the lac des Deux-Montagnes drops into the lac Saint-Louis over the rapides de Sainte-Anne. Everything about the place follows from that fall of water. It was first a portage, then the last obligatory halt on the island for anyone bound for the Pays d'en Haut, then — once the first locks and the canal were dug between 1840 and 1843 — a lock town on the through route to the Ottawa. The 2005 heritage evaluation reads the result as a village whose institutional core, <em>« l'église, le presbytère et le couvent »</em>, was cut off from the newer commercial town by the railways laid from 1860 and by a twentieth-century highway; the two halves are still legible on the ground.

What the sources describe is a small place with four distinct kinds of house in it. In the îlot villageois beside the canal, modest dwellings <em>« généralement recouvertes de clin »</em>, of two storeys, built close to the street. Behind them, on rue Saint-Jean-Baptiste, workers' cottages under steep dormered roofs with their ground floors barely raised, which the cahier calls <em>« un ensemble de cottages tout à fait caractéristique du patrimoine architectural de Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue »</em>. Up the slope, the college town that grew beside Macdonald College and John Abbott: solid clay-brick cottages under pitched roofs and some large stucco houses. And on the far side of the tracks, the twenty-five houses J.-J. Harpell built round his Garden City Press in 1918 on Ebenezer Howard's model.

The town markets that first sector as a <strong>village patrimonial</strong>. That is a slogan, not a status. Nothing in Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue is protected at the scale of an ensemble — no declared site, no cited site, no RPCQ site record of any kind. Two individual buildings carry the entire statutory weight of the town: the maison Simon-Fraser at 153 rue Sainte-Anne, classée in 1962, and the maison Michel-Robillard at 20345 chemin Sainte-Marie, citée in 2014. The church and the presbytery at the heart of the advertised village are merely <em>inventoriés</em>. Everything else is held by design review.

## phase:p1

The Sulpicians become seigneurs of the island in 1663, and the west end is a strategic contact point with Indigenous nations and an outpost for the defence of the French colony. The parish of Saint-Louis-du-Bout-de-l'Île — also written Saint-Louis-du-Haut-de-l'Île — is founded in 1677, and the abbé François-Saturnin Lascaris d'Urfé is recorded as its first resident curé in 1686, returning to France the following year. The abbé René-Charles de Breslay takes over the mission in about 1703 and is curé here until 1719; the parish takes the name Sainte-Anne-du-Bout-de-l'Île in 1714. Nothing residential survives above ground from this phase in the village core, and no source this project holds describes its houses.

## phase:p2

The village that stands today begins here. The 2024 plan d'urbanisme dates the noyau villageois to the end of the eighteenth century, and the oldest house in it is of that moment: the maison Simon-Fraser, built between 1790 and 1810 for Peter Grant, a partner in the North West Company. A post office opens in 1835 and the municipality is created in 1845 under the name Bout-de-l'Isle. Between 1840 and 1843 the first locks and the canal de Sainte-Anne are built, the gateway to the Ottawa river and part of the network that carried settlers to Upper Canada, then tens of thousands of steamer passengers, and all through the nineteenth century the <em>cageux</em> riding the timber rafts down from the Outaouais and the Gatineau. The village develops beside those busy locks. The parish church now standing goes up in 1853–1859, built by Félix Barbeau and John Lewis Forbes.

## phase:p3

The Grand Trunk reaches the West Island in 1855 and Sainte-Anne becomes a stopping place twice over — for the locks and for two railways, the Canadian National and the Canadian Pacific. The municipality is renamed Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue in 1878, is erected as a village municipality that year, and takes the title of ville from 1895. Baie-d'Urfé is detached from its territory in 1911. At the end of the nineteenth century the shore is taken up by Montréal's anglophone bourgeoisie as a resort — yacht clubs, hotels, tennis courts and golf links — and at the beginning of the twentieth the campuses of Macdonald College (ten buildings of 1905–1908 by Hutchison and Wood) and John Abbott change the social and urban dynamic of the village, their staff living on campus or just outside it. A manufacture, a sawmill, a cinema and a military hospital arrive. In 1918 J.-J. Harpell brings the Garden City Press, a printer of school textbooks and workers' educational material; influenced by Ebenezer Howard, he lays out streets, parks and services and builds twenty-five houses for his employees near the works, and during the Depression hires unemployed men to plant trees and commissions Frederick Todd to lay out a Japanese garden with a pond opposite the plant, of which nothing survives. He hands the firm to a workers' cooperative in 1945.

## phase:p4

Rail supplants navigation, and the canal and locks change use; the village keeps its shape while the town spreads. In 1964 the ville annexes the territory of the parish municipality. The Commission des lieux et monuments historiques du Canada recommends the designation of the canal in 1987. In 1962 the maison Simon-Fraser is restored under the direction of the architect Percy Roy Wilson — one of the precursors of modern architectural restoration in the Montréal region — with the support of the Société historique du Bout-de-l'Isle and the fondation Héritage canadien du Québec, and classed in the same year; it is sold to that foundation in 1965. On 1 January 2002 Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue is merged into the new ville de Montréal.

## phase:p5

The town is reconstituted on 1 January 2006 under Bill 9, after the referendums of 20 June 2004, with effect from 1 January. It inherits the 2005 sector evaluation the Ville de Montréal had drawn up for the merged arrondissement, and the plan d'urbanisme the Ville had adopted under number 04-047. It replaces both slowly: a PIIA by-law consolidated in 2017 (règlement 798), a demolition by-law (846), and a new plan d'urbanisme (863) adopted on 11 November 2024, whose heritage map names exactly two protected buildings. Parks Canada's management plan for the canal is tabled in Parliament on 16 December 2020. Since 2024 the agglomeration of Montréal has been running the pre-1940 inventory the 2021 <em>Loi sur le patrimoine culturel</em> requires of it, due by 1 April 2026; the town's own heritage page says so. That inventory, not a citation, is what will next change the record here.

## notes

<strong>The « village patrimonial » is a marketing phrase, and this page will not print it as anything else.</strong> The words come from the town's tourism site, <em>visiter.sadb.qc.ca</em>, where the village core is listed as an activity at 109 rue Sainte-Anne alongside the garlic festival and salsa by the water: <em>« Aux rues Sainte-Anne et de l'Église qui structurent le secteur, se greffent quelques rues résidentielles dotées d'une architecture villageoise représentative de la fin du 19e et du début du 20e siècle. »</em> That sentence is accurate as description and claims no status, and the page claims none either. The negative was checked three ways and holds three ways. The RPCQ's own search, filtered to catégorie de bien <em>« Site patrimonial »</em> and municipality Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue, returns <em>« Aucun résultat »</em>; the same search across the whole Montréal administrative region returns forty site records, from the site patrimonial de Montréal to the site patrimonial de la pointe Claire in the next town but two, and none of them is here. The RPCQ holds eleven immovable records for the municipality and every one of them is a single building or monument. And the town's own règlement 863 settles it from the municipal side: carte 7 legends <em>« Immeubles qui présentent une protection particulière en vertu de la Loi sur le patrimoine culturel »</em> and names two — <em>A Maison Simon-Fraser (classée en 1962)</em> and <em>B Maison Michel-Robillard (citée en 2014)</em>. The town's complete published by-law list contains no citation by-law at all.

<strong>Classée and inventorié are not degrees of the same thing.</strong> The maison Simon-Fraser is <em>classée immeuble patrimonial</em> by the minister: work on it requires ministerial authorisation, and the protection reaches the interior, the remise and the land. The église (RPCQ 179473) and the presbytère (RPCQ 179475) carry the status line <em>« Inventorié — -- »</em>, with no authority and no date, because they were catalogued by the <em>Inventaire des lieux de culte du Québec</em> of 2003–2004. An inventory records; it does not protect. The two buildings that anchor the advertised village are therefore the two with no legal protection at all, and the one building that is protected stands three doors along a commercial street.

<strong>The fiche for the maison Simon-Fraser contradicts itself twice, and both contradictions are left standing here rather than resolved.</strong> Its <em>valeur patrimoniale</em> calls the owner <em>« Simon Fraser (1760-1839) »</em>, born in Scotland, settled in Canada with his family in 1789, a shareholder of the North West Company and seigneur of the fief Bellevue; its own index of associated persons gives <em>« Fraser, Simon (1776 – 1862) »</em>, and the Ville de Montréal's 2005 cahier agrees with the index — <em>« Simon Fraser (1776-1862), marchand de fourrures important, y possède une maison construite vers 1800 »</em>. Those are not the same man's dates. Separately, the fiche's structured data block reads <em>« Nombre d'étages : 3 ½ »</em> while its own description reads <em>« une demeure d'inspiration française… à un étage et demi »</em>; the description agrees with the éléments caractéristiques, which describe a steep two-slope roof with two rows of dormers in the façade, so the storey count used on this site is one and a half and the 3 ½ figure is recorded as an error in the source.

<strong>The house was not built for Simon Fraser.</strong> The RPCQ is explicit: it was put up between 1790 and 1810 for <strong>Peter Grant</strong> (vers 1764–1848), one of Fraser's associates in the North West Company, and Fraser bought it only in 1820, after the manor of the seigneurie de Bellevue burned, and used it as his manor until his death. The Irish poet Thomas Moore is said to have stayed there in 1804 and composed <em>The Canadian Boat Song</em>, which is why the house carries the alternative name maison Thomas-Moore. From 1906 to 1954 it held a branch of the Bank of Montreal. Fraser's descendants owned it until 1965.

<strong>Four different founding dates circulate for this parish, and the sources do not agree.</strong> The Commission de toponymie has the parish of Saint-Louis-du-Bout-de-l'Île founded in <strong>1677</strong> and renamed Sainte-Anne-du-Bout-de-l'Île in <strong>1714</strong>. The RPCQ's fiche for the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette in neighbouring Baie-D'Urfé says the parish was founded in <strong>1686</strong> by the abbé Lascaris d'Urfé. The town's own heritage page dates the parish to <strong>1703</strong>, which is the year the Commission de toponymie gives for the abbé de Breslay taking over the mission. The place record uses 1677, because that is the date in the provincial toponymic authority, and the others are set out here rather than harmonised away.

<strong>The arrondissement code and the filename disagree again.</strong> The 2005 cahier prints its sector codes as 1.E.7 to 1.E.11, 1.I.2 to 1.I.5 and 1.U.1 — arrondissement number 1 — while the file the Ville publishes is <em>13_evaluation_patrimoine_lile.pdf</em>. This is the same mismatch Part 10 recorded for Outremont and Westmount, and both numbers are stored on the place record.

<strong>The photograph of the village core does not quite show what the fiche describes, and the caption says so.</strong> The 2005 fiche's one dimensional statement about the houses of the îlot villageois is that they are <em>« généralement recouvertes de clin, comptent deux étages et sont construites près de la rue »</em>. The Ville de Montréal's own photograph of one of those residential streets, reproduced on this page, shows the clapboard and the tight setback plainly enough, but most of the row in frame is a storey lower than the fiche says — one-and-a-half-storey cottages under steep sheet-metal roofs with dormers and galleries, beside a two-storey painted-brick house. The fiche is the source and its wording is what the type record carries; the discrepancy is recorded here rather than quietly corrected, because a photograph is evidence of a different kind and this project does not let one overwrite the other.

<strong>What this project could not read.</strong> The Ville de Montréal's <em>Grand répertoire du patrimoine bâti de Montréal</em>, which holds a zone fiche for this village core, was offline during the crawl; the Ville's replacement page states that access to it and to two sister databases <em>« sera à nouveau disponible dans le courant de l'année 2026 »</em>. The town's own <em>Plan d'urbanisme, Annexe 2, Analyse patrimoniale</em>, which the 2005 cahier quotes twice as its footnote 6, is a 1989-generation document that is not published on the town's site. And the <em>Répertoire d'architecture traditionnelle de la Communauté urbaine de Montréal</em> — whose <em>cahier sur l'architecture rurale</em> the town's own 2014 citation by-law cites as the record in which the maison Michel-Robillard was catalogued — has not been obtained either. It is the one document that would give this page per-building descriptions rather than sector paragraphs, and it covers Baie-D'Urfé as well. None of the three has been used here except through the passages other documents quote from them.
