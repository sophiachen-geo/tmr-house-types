## intro

In 1665 the Jesuits, seigneurs of Notre-Dame-des-Anges, laid out a village on the first terrace
above the rivière Saint-Charles in a shape nobody had used in New France before. Three squares
nested inside one another: a <em>réserve</em> of five arpents at the dead centre for church,
presbytery and cemetery; a <em>commune</em> of twenty arpents of shared pasture around it, fenced,
and ringed by a square road called Le Trait-Carré; and outside that a <em>ceinture</em> of forty
trapezoidal farm lots, ten to a side, radiating outward like the points of a star. A rule came with
it — the <em>règle du tiers</em> — obliging every occupant to build his house in the first third of
his land, hard against the Trait-Carré. That single rule is why this place is legible three and a
half centuries later: it pulled forty scattered farmsteads into one continuous village street while
letting every farmer live on his own land.

The royal edict of 1663 had ordered exactly this, that settlers gather into <em>bourgs</em> to make
the colony defensible and to stop the drift that the rang system produced. Charlesbourg is the
answer to it, and the plan de conservation calls the result "l'un des premiers gestes de
planification urbaine en Amérique française". Between 22 and 28 February 1665 nearly thirty lots
were conceded. The Trait-Carré itself was probably traced in 1692; the parish of Saint-Charles-
Borromée was erected canonically in 1693.

Twenty-four hectares survive as a <em>site patrimonial déclaré</em>, by decree of 17 November 1965,
holding 138 principal buildings. What they amount to is a compressed rural sequence: a stone mill of
1742 and a handful of French-inspired farmhouses; the white neoclassical Québec house with its
gallery under an overhanging larmier, which is what most of the Trait-Carré actually is; the mansard
arriving in the 1870s through the presbytery and the convent before it reached the houses; then the
industrial vernacular, dominant from the 1890s to the Second World War in three forms the plan files
under one parent; and finally the bungalow, which is what the declaration was drawn to stop.

There is no separate inventory here. The plan de conservation's own prose is the typology, five
types over four pages, and this record follows it exactly — including where it declines to say
something, which is more often than a reader might expect.

## phase:p1

The seigneurie de Notre-Dame-des-Anges is conceded to the Compagnie de Jésus in 1626 by the viceroy
Henri de Lévis, duc de Ventadour (1596–1651): one league of frontage by four of depth, on the north
bank of the rivière Saint-Charles between the ruisseau Saint-Michel and the rivière Beauport.
Settlement only begins after the Kirke brothers take Québec in 1629 and the Jesuits return in 1632,
and for a generation only the land along the river and the St Lawrence is conceded. The interior
waits.

## phase:p2

In 1665 the Jesuits set about the interior of their fief and lay out the star. A wooden chapel goes
up in the réserve between 1666 and 1674, dedicated to Saint Charles Borromeo, served by the Jesuits
and from 1675 by the Séminaire de Québec; on 24 June 1686 the Compagnie de Jésus cedes the réserve
lots to the Fabrique. A first presbytery follows about 1691 and is destroyed in 1846. By the end of
the century the bourg holds some twenty dwellings, a windmill north of the Trait-Carré that has
since vanished, and a cemetery south of the chapel, with four roads crossing at its centre: the
chemin de Québec and the chemin de Saint-Pierre-et-Saint-Claude north–south, the chemin de
Saint-Joseph and the chemin du Bourg-Royal east–west. In 1693 the parish is erected canonically and
two years later a stone church is begun, opened for worship in 1697. Early in the 18th century the
colonial authorities order the commune subdivided, but grazing rights run on for years and most of
the land is not broken up until the century's end. In 1742 the Jesuits' banal windmill is replaced
by a water mill east of the bourg, on part of the Lefebvre land, fed by a diversion of the ruisseau
de la Cabane-au-Taupier — there being no watercourse in the sector at all. It still stands.

## phase:p3

The Trait-Carré is remade in the 19th century. A new church rises south of the first, to plans by
Thomas Baillairgé (1791–1859) — a neoclassical building with a screen façade under two towers,
consecrated in 1830. The two churches stand side by side until 1836, when the older is demolished to
make room for the second cemetery. In 1846 the curé exchanges Fabrique land so that the chemin de
Saint-Joseph can be shifted north to face the church, and the boulevard Louis-XIV still carries that
kink at the centre of the Trait-Carré. Standing at a crossroads, the village fills with trade: in
1856 the baker Jacques Verret buys land in the south-west quadrant and builds a house with a general
store on the ground floor; the 1871 census records wheelwrights, blacksmiths, cobblers and
lime-burners; Dr Joseph-Eusèbe Grondin builds a house and a stable in the 1870s. Nearly thirty
buildings go up between 1850 and 1900.

## phase:p4

The institutional core is rebuilt in a single generation, and it arrives dressed as Second Empire.
The present presbytery, "une imposante demeure d'inspiration Second Empire", is built in 1875. The
Soeurs du Bon-Pasteur, given charge of the girls' school in 1880, build a convent west of the
presbytery in 1883, enlarged in 1910 to plans by François-Xavier Berlinguet (1830–1916). The church
gets a new sacristy in 1887 to plans by David Ouellet (1844–1915), set perpendicular to the old one.
The Frères maristes take over the boys' school in 1900 and in 1904 replace it with the collège
Saint-Charles. The parish cemetery, outgrown, moves outside the site in 1895, and the old one
becomes a park in 1919 with Alfred Laliberté's Sacré-Coeur statue in it. A new parish hall, now the
salle Pierre-Garon, follows about 1925 — with a parapet the plan describes as evoking Boomtown
architecture. Some fifteen houses go up in the same quarter-century, and the mansard, having come in
on the institutions, spreads to them.

## phase:p5

From the end of the 19th century to the Second World War the industrial vernacular is, in the plan's
words, "le type qui domine les nouvelles constructions": balloon frame, machined boards, catalogue
doors and windows, asbestos-cement tile. It comes in three shapes — the cubique on its square
two-storey plan under a shallow pavilion roof, the cottage vernaculaire that copies the traditional
Québec house and gives itself away at the eave, and the Boomtown hiding a flat roof behind a
parapet. The plan treats all three as sub-variants of one type. Half of Québec's other sources do
not.

## phase:p6

At the turn of the 1950s Québec's sprawl arrives and the farm lots are cut up for bungalows. Two new
roads, the chemin Samuel and the avenue Paul-Comtois, are opened along old lot lines to connect the
Trait-Carré outward. The centre Odilon-Gauthier is added to the institutional core in 1957. The
church is classed in 1959, and on 17 November 1965 the site is declared, "afin de protéger le noyau
villageois". Sixty-five buildings — 48.5 % of everything in the site — date from 1925 to 1975. In
the last quarter of the century several buildings are restored and turned into cultural spaces: the
moulin des Jésuites becomes an interpretation centre, the collège des Frères maristes a library,
enlarged in 2006 by a contemporary pavilion by Croft Pelletier Architectes whose north wall recalls
the radial plan. In 2002 Charlesbourg is merged into the Ville de Québec, and since 2017 it is the
City, not the ministère, that authorises work inside the site.

## notes

<strong>Charlesbourg was one of three, and the only one that kept its plan.</strong> This is the
single most interesting fact in this part, and it is the CPCQ's consultation report that states it:
<em>« le lotissement en étoile autour d'un carré central destiné à devenir le cœur du village et
autour duquel étaient érigées les habitations est particulier à l'ancienne seigneurie de
Notre-Dame-des-Anges. Outre celui de Charlesbourg, deux autres villages ont été créés à la même
époque : la Petite-Auvergne, qui est en réalité une demi-étoile (faute d'espace), et Bourg-Royal. À
la différence des deux autres, Charlesbourg est doté d'un noyau paroissial qui a favorisé son
développement. »</em> The plan de conservation adds the dates and the authorship: the Jesuits reused
their own model at Petite-Auvergne in 1666 but had room for only half a square; in the same year
Jean Talon expropriated part of the seigneurie and made a third star village, Bourg-Royal. Only
Charlesbourg was given a parish core, and that is why only Charlesbourg is still legible as a plan.
The other two are not gone without trace — the consultation report records the Société d'histoire de
Charlesbourg asking that attention be paid to the carré De Tracy, "qui forme un autre trait-carré,
dans le secteur de l'ancien Bourg-Royal", and the 2005 Étude notes that the carré de Tracy still
lets you follow the outline of Bourg-Royal's old commune.

<strong>The plan is the Jesuits', not Talon's.</strong> Tourism texts routinely credit the intendant
Jean Talon with the radial village, and the RPCQ fiche itself lists Talon as the one person
associated with the site. Both plans de conservation and the 2005 Étude are clear that this is
wrong, and both cite the same corrective in their bibliographies: Marcel Trudel, <em>« Le village en
étoile, une innovation des jésuites et non de Talon »</em>, Revue d'histoire de l'Amérique française,
vol. 44, no 3 (hiver 1991), p. 397–406. The 2005 Étude puts Trudel's charge plainly: Talon
"aurait non seulement « emprunté » aux Jésuites l'espace nécessaire pour implanter un village radial,
mais s'en serait attribué l'idée originale". Talon's own village was Bourg-Royal, founded a year
after Charlesbourg and without a parish core. This site attributes the plan to the Jesuits.

<strong>Three building counts, three scopes.</strong> 138 is principal buildings, and both the RPCQ
fiche and the plan de conservation give that figure; every statistic in the plan's built-fabric
survey is computed against it. About 200 counts all buildings including the secondary ones, and it
is not the plan that says so — it is the CPCQ consultation report, summarising the plan's valeur
architecturale as "liée à la présence de quelque 200 bâtiments". 149 is a third measure again: the
records linked as <em>patrimoine immobilier associé</em> on the RPCQ fiche. None of these is a
correction of another, and no single "number of buildings" should be published for this place.

<strong>The geometry, reconciled.</strong> The published areas look contradictory and are not. The
site is 24 hectares. Inside it the RPCQ and the plan both describe a central square of about 8.5
hectares (25 arpents) bounded by Le Trait-Carré Est and Ouest. The plan's valeur urbanistique then
says what that square is made of: <em>« un carré central de 8,5 hectares (composé d'une réserve de
1,7 hectare entourée d'un pâturage commun délimité par le chemin du Trait-Carré) »</em>. So 25
arpents is réserve plus commune together — 5 arpents of parish reserve inside 20 arpents of common
pasture. The commune on its own is 20 arpents, about 7 hectares. Each of the forty radiating farm
lots was to be 13.7 hectares (40 arpents). The 2005 Étude's summary is the compact version: forty
trapezoidal terres abutting the chemin du Trait-Carré, which encircles a communal holding at whose
centre five arpents are reserved for church, presbytery and cemetery — <em>« C'est la première
implantation de ce type en Nouvelle-France. »</em>

<strong>Who authorises the work.</strong> Since a transfer of responsibility registered 9 December
2016 and effective 9 June 2017, the Ville de Québec exercises certain ministerial powers over this
site. Permits and authorisations therefore issue from the City, under the Commission d'urbanisme et
de conservation de Québec, and not from the ministère. That is a genuine structural difference from
Île d'Orléans, where the ministère still authorises, and it is the reason the Ville de Québec
publishes its own owner's guide for the site.

<strong>Two dates this site does not settle.</strong> The moulin des Jésuites: the plan de
conservation gives <em>1742</em> three times, and dates the replacement of the banal windmill by a
water mill to that year. Both the 2005 Étude de caractérisation and the Ville de Québec's R.V.Q. 1324
guide say <em>« vers 1740 »</em> instead. Two sources against one, but the plan is the later and more
specific, so 1742 is used here with the minority reading recorded. The maison Éphraïm-Bédard, 7655
chemin Samuel: no single year is published on this site. The plan says <em>« la maison
Éphraïm-Bédard a plutôt été érigée vers 1830 »</em> — offered explicitly as a correction, because the
house looks like an 18th-century French-inspired farmhouse and is not one — and adds that its present
appearance dates from a restoration in the 1980s; the 2005 Étude gives 1830; other accounts place it
in the first half of the 19th century. The range is what is published, not a year, and the
possibility that the pièce-sur-pièce carré reuses an older dwelling on a lot conceded in 1665 remains
unresolved.

<strong>Maison Pierre-Lefebvre is unverified, and its date is contested.</strong> No RPCQ identifier
is published for it here, because none was confirmed; the Lefebvre family owned the land the mill was
built on, which makes name-matching unsafe. The two principal sources also disagree about when the
house was built: the plan de conservation dates it 1846, the 2005 Étude 1825. It is named in this
record only as one of the two houses the plan describes as clad in vertical planks and converted to
cultural use.

<strong>Not type cards: the mill and the secondary buildings.</strong> Two parts of the built fabric
are described at length by the plan but are not residential, and are recorded here rather than
carded. The <strong>moulin des Jésuites</strong> (1742, restored in the late 1980s) is the plan's
"exemple exceptionnel d'architecture de type préindustriel de la Nouvelle-France" and the site's only
pre-industrial building. It is representative of water-powered mills in its great size and in an
elevation that follows the fall of the ground, with the openings set asymmetrically because the
building did two jobs at once — the miller's dwelling at one end, the water chambers and machinery at
the other. It recalls French-regime building in <em>« ses murs en moellons crépis, son toit à deux
versants à forte pente couvert de bardeaux de cèdre, ses chambranles en pierre de taille et ses
souches de cheminées asymétriques »</em>, and like the oldest houses it is <em>« implanté en retrait
de la voie publique et sa façade est orientée vers le sud »</em>. It has been a museum and
interpretation centre since the late 20th century and is now the starting point of the Trait-Carré
interpretive walk. The <strong>bâtiments secondaires</strong> are agricultural and domestic
outbuildings — the barn at 7694 avenue Thomas-Baillairgé, the former stable at 8290 Le Trait-Carré
Est, the presbytery's tithe barn, one of the rare survivors of its kind in an urbanised setting, and
a garage at 7838 Le Trait-Carré Est whose oblique gable wall follows a radial lot line. Generally
they have <em>« un plan rectangulaire, une élévation d'un étage et un toit à deux versants droits »</em>
and are clad <em>« en planches verticales, en tôle ou en bardeaux de cèdre »</em>. One of them, behind
7845-7847 1re Avenue, still carries the painted words « Verret », « Graine » and « Miel » from the
years when Jacques-Ferdinand Verret (1860–1946) turned his father's general store into a seed
merchant's.

<strong>The parcours directeurs.</strong> Two 17th-century roads cross at the centre and still
organise everything. The <strong>1re Avenue</strong>, north–south, was the chemin de Québec (or de
Charlesbourg), crossing the seigneurie to link the bourg to Québec and continuing north to the
concessions of Saint-Claude and Saint-Pierre; it swings slightly as it approaches the village so as
to pass in front of the church, then straightens again. The <strong>boulevard Louis-XIV</strong>,
east–west, is two old roads joined: the chemin de Saint-Joseph to the west, which crossed the
seigneuries des Islets and de Saint-Joseph towards the Jeune-Lorette, and the chemin du Bourg-Royal
to the east, which ran to that village and on to Saint-Michel north of Beauport. They met the chemin
de Québec at two different points — Bourg-Royal through the centre of the Trait-Carré,
Saint-Joseph south of the réserve — until the 1846 realignment, and the kink at the centre is the
scar of it. Le Trait-Carré Est and Ouest is not a parcours directeur but the site's principal
<em>parcours d'implantation</em>: traced at the end of the 17th century along the edge of the
commune, square as its name says, with the first houses built outside it and later ones inside.

<strong>What the plan does not say.</strong> Four of the five type descriptions leave columns empty,
and those gaps are preserved rather than filled. The French-inspired rural house is given no ornament
and no window proportion; the neoclassical house and the mansard house no storey count; the mansard
house no cladding of its own, only a cross-reference to the type before it; the post-war residence
neither openings nor ornament. No roof pitch in degrees is given for any of the five. A narrative
typology is a looser instrument than the per-type tables of Gatineau, Lévis or the Île d'Orléans
inventory, and the shape of what it omits is part of what it is.
