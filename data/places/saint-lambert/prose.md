## intro
Saint-Lambert is a garden suburb made by a bridge. Farmland of the seigneurie of La Prairie de la Magdeleine, its low riverside long called Mouillepied, it was cut off from Longueuil as a municipality in 1857, two years before the Victoria Bridge opened and the Grand Trunk made it a fifteen-minute commute from Montréal. Anglo-Protestant families followed the railway; the electric interurban of 1909 and the subdivision of Victoria Park (1889) and Brooklyn Park (1894, taken over 1909) filled the grid with Queen Anne, Boomtown, Four Square and Arts and Crafts houses between about 1875 and 1930; a single contractor, Francis King, then stamped the interwar streets with his brick cottages, and the postwar bungalow boom and the annexation of Préville (1969) finished the town. Village in 1892, town in 1898, cité in 1921, merged into Longueuil in 2002 and reconstituted in 2006, it produced in 2020 a heritage inventory (Patri-Arch) whose ten fiches read like a compact history of Québec suburban housing, from the French-regime farmhouse to the modern house. This page presents them in the same form as Mount Royal's thirteen types, with the fiches' conservation rules kept alongside.

## phase:p1
The stone farmhouses of the riverside farms, French tradition then Québécois synthesis: steep gable, massive chimneys, small-paned casements, and later the flared eave, the gallery and a symmetrical front. Maison Marsil (mid-18th century) is the classified survivor.

## phase:p2
The maison traditionnelle québécoise dominates until about 1880. In the middle of the period the town's future is decided elsewhere: the railway reaches the shore about 1852, the Victoria Bridge opens in 1859, and Saint-Lambert is erected as a municipality in 1857.

## phase:p3
The commuter suburb. Second Empire mansards from about 1875, Queen Anne from the 1890s, Boomtown boxes with false mansards or brick parapets from 1900, Four Squares and Arts and Crafts cottages in the same decades, and the Montréal plex imported by developers. Streets are laid out over the farm lots (Victoria Park 1889, Brooklyn Park 1894); the interurban arrives in 1909; the town becomes a cité in 1921.

## phase:p4
The Depression slows building; Francis King does not. Between 1932 and 1951 he builds seventy to a hundred catalogue houses, neo-Tudor to bungalow, of which the multi-gabled brick "King Cottage" is the type the town remembers him by.

## phase:p5
Some 700 houses in the five years after the war; the bungalow with carport or garage "fait fureur" in the 1950s and 60s; Préville, created in 1948 west of the golf course, is annexed on 3 May 1969.

## notes
The Saint-Lambert fiches are conservation guides as much as descriptions: each of the five headings mixes what the type is with what may and may not be done to it (no imitation cladding, no painted brick, no aluminium railings, no front additions). This site keeps the two apart: the descriptive sentences feed the five-column profile, the prescriptive ones a separate "conservation guidance" list. The classification is by architectural current rather than by lot and massing, so several Saint-Lambert types map onto Mount Royal's or the Plateau's forms under other names: the Boomtown house with crown is Mount Royal's maison faubourienne on a suburban lot; the plex fiche compresses the Plateau's duplex, triplex and multiplex into one; the Arts and Crafts house and the Garden City house share a canonical form. Two counts circulate for the inventory (250 documented buildings; 200 "remarkable" ones in the 2023 Guide); the difference is a subset, not an error. No municipal site patrimonial cité was found for Saint-Lambert; protection runs through the PIIA (2023-220), the demolition by-law (2023-212) and the agglomeration of Longueuil's inventories.
