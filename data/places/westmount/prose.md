## intro
Westmount is a suburb that has been editing itself for a century. Grown over the Côte-Saint-Antoine farms — the founding road is reputed to follow an Indigenous trail, and the Hurtubise house of 1739 still stands on it — it became a village in 1874, a town in 1890 and Westmount in 1895, and by 1911 held 14,500 people on a slope that rises from the flat lower town to the summit. What makes it legible is control: an Architectural Commission from 1916, a first in Canada; a 1988 study that graded every building; and, since 2001, By-law 1305, which sorts the territory into 39 heritage sectors and each building into one of four categories, from I* (exceptional, with per-building conservation strategies "correspond[ing] to that observed by agencies like Parks Canada in the country's most important historic sites") down to III (neutral). About 4,750 dwellings, some 61 % of the stock, predate 1945. Parks Canada designated the district a National Historic Site in 2011 for the "diversity and the integrity of its components: residences (detached, semi-detached and row houses), apartments, public buildings … and institutions … as well as landscape features such as parks, staircases and public spaces."

## phase:p1
Farms along the chemin de la Côte-Saint-Antoine. Maison Hurtubise (1739) survives, classified in 2004 and held by Héritage canadien du Québec.

## phase:p2
Picturesque villas and country cottages on the slope: Metcalfe Terrace (1840), Braemar and Riverview (1847).

## phase:p3
The lower town fills with greystone rows (Wood, Elm, Olivier, Hallowell-Weredale) and the first elaborate Queen Anne houses appear on Metcalfe and Melbourne. Rue Saint-Antoine gets the town's rare Montréal-style greystone triplexes with outside stairs.

## phase:p4
The Boulevard is linked to Montréal in 1895 and the summit opens: "de véritables châteaux" between 1900 and 1930, brick semi-detached houses west of the mountain, brick rows on Somerville and Grosvenor spurred by the CP stations of 1889 and 1907.

## phase:p5
Neo-Tudor becomes the civic and domestic idiom (City Hall 1922); garden-city enclaves appear at Douglas and Grenville (W. S. Wright) and at Priests' Farm (1926–1930, L. E. Schlemm with the architects Shorey and Ritchie), among the first Canadian projects to adopt curving streets; interwar apartment houses line Sherbrooke and De Maisonneuve.

## phase:p6
Postwar conciergeries, then the 1960s rezoning that cleared blocks on Elm and Wood for Westmount Square (Mies van der Rohe, 1967) and Place Alexis Nihon, and the Ville-Marie autoroute of the 1970s that cut the escarpment edge.

## notes
Westmount grades buildings, not types: the four categories (I*/I/II/III) travel with the address, and the 39 sectors give the context against which a project is judged. The types on this page are therefore a reconstruction from the sector fiches and the National Historic Site's character-defining elements, not a municipal catalogue — where the sources are silent on materials or openings, the fields are left empty. Two source discrepancies are worth recording. The count of Category 1* buildings is given as 45 residences by the City's Imagine Westmount 2040 fiche and as 49 exceptional properties by its heritage pages; the difference is almost certainly residences versus all property types. And Parks Canada dates development control in Westmount to "beginning in 1914", while the City dates its Architectural Commission to 1916.
