## intro
Westmount is a suburb that has been editing itself for a century. Grown over the Côte-Saint-Antoine farms — the founding road is reputed to follow an Indigenous trail, and the Hurtubise house of 1739 still stands on it — it became a village in 1874, a town in 1890 and Westmount in 1895, and by 1911 held 14,500 people on a slope that rises from the flat lower town to the summit. What makes it legible is control: an Architectural Commission from 1916, a first in Canada; a 1988 study that graded every building; and, since 2001, By-law 1305, which divides the territory into thirty-nine heritage sectors and sorts each building into one of four categories, from I* (exceptional, <em>conservés à perpétuité</em>) down to III (neutral). About 4,750 dwellings, some 61 % of the stock, predate 1945. Parks Canada designated the district a National Historic Site in 2011 for the "diversity and the integrity of its components: residences (detached, semi-detached and row houses), apartments, public buildings … and institutions … as well as landscape features such as parks, staircases and public spaces."

## phase:p1
Farms along the chemin de la Côte-Saint-Antoine, one of the first inland <em>côtes</em> that radiated from Ville-Marie. Maison Hurtubise (1739) survives, classified in 2004 and held by Héritage canadien du Québec.

## phase:p2
Picturesque villas and country cottages on the slope: Metcalfe Terrace (1840), Braemar and Riverview (1847). Braemar, at 3219 The Boulevard, was recognised in 1984 and is the first building listed in the by-law's Annexe III.

## phase:p3
The lower town fills with greystone rows — runs of eight units or fewer, put up by small investors on Wood, Elm, Olivier and at Hallowell-Weredale — and the first elaborate Queen Anne houses appear on Metcalfe and Melbourne. Rue Saint-Antoine gets the town's rare Montréal-style greystone triplexes with straight outside stairs. On avenue Argyle the photographer William Notman subdivides the lots himself.

## phase:p4
The Boulevard is linked to Montréal in 1895 and the summit opens: <em>de véritables châteaux</em> between 1900 and 1930, brick semi-detached houses west of the mountain on lots sold with conditions specifying materials, setbacks and even completion dates, and brick rows on Somerville and Grosvenor spurred by the CP stations of 1889 and 1907.

## phase:p5
Neo-Tudor becomes the civic and domestic idiom — between 1910 and 1935 stone and neo-Tudor are used again and again for schools, churches and public buildings, City Hall (1922) among them. Garden-city enclaves appear at Douglas and Grenville (W. S. Wright) and at Priest's Farm (1926–1930, L. E. Schlemm with the architects Shorey and Ritchie), among the first Canadian projects to adopt curving streets; interwar apartment houses line Sherbrooke and De Maisonneuve.

## phase:p6
Postwar conciergeries, then the 1960s rezoning that cleared blocks on Elm and Wood for Westmount Square (Mies van der Rohe, 1967) and Place Alexis Nihon, and the Ville-Marie autoroute of the 1970s that cut the escarpment edge and displaced the families rehoused on avenue Hillside.

## notes
By-law 1305 grades buildings, not types. The four categories travel with the address; the thirty-nine sectors give the context against which a project is judged; and the guidelines — eleven booklets called <em>Rénover et construire à Westmount</em> — are organised by the work you propose (walls, roofs, windows and doors, additions, landscaping) rather than by the kind of house you own. An owner is told to work through five steps: establish the building's category from the sector map, establish whether it belongs to an architectural ensemble, establish the integrity of its original design, consult the design guidelines, and finally read the list of defining traits on the reverse of the sector fiche. The types on this page are therefore a reconstruction from that element-by-element text, the sector fiches and the National Historic Site's character-defining elements, not a municipal catalogue; where the sources are silent, the fields are left empty.

The one place the by-law does speak in something like typological terms is its roof table, and it is worth quoting because it ties form to tenure and to altitude at once: <strong>flat roofs and the decorative sloped roofs — false pavilion and false mansard — are commonest on the row houses of Lower Westmount and the semi-detached houses of Middle Westmount, while the true sloped roofs (Norman, gabled, multi-gabled, hipped and mansard) belong to the detached houses of Middle and Upper Westmount.</strong> The masonry passage does the same job for materials: Westmount is "almost entirely" brick and stone, most often local grey stone and red brick of local clay, its appeal heightened by entrances, porches, balconies and decorative woodwork — cornices, marquees, consoles and <em>tarabiscotage</em>.

Three source frictions are worth recording. The by-law numbers its sectors 1 to 39, while the 2005 Ville de Montréal evaluation numbers the same territory 23.E.<em>n</em> and 23.I.<em>n</em>; this site stores both, and the two sets match one for one by name with nothing left over on either side. The by-law itself alternates between "catégorie 1*/1/2/3" in its articles and "Catégorie I*/I/II/III" in the guidelines table for the same four tiers. And the count of exceptional buildings is given as 45 residences by the City's Imagine Westmount 2040 fiche and as 49 exceptional properties by its heritage pages — almost certainly residences versus all property types. A fourth, smaller one: article 5.3 names twelve booklets but says there are eleven, because "Nouveaux bâtiments" and "Ajouts et modifications majeures" are in fact a single booklet.

Two cross-references run out of this page. Sector 36, rue Saint-Antoine, holds the attached greystone triplexes with straight outside stairs that the 2005 fiche calls <em>ce genre de construction typique de Montréal [qui] se retrouve rarement à Westmount</em> — the direct link to the Plateau's <em>duplex</em> and <em>triplex à escalier extérieur</em> and to Saint-Lambert's plexes. And <strong>Leonard E. Schlemm</strong>, the planner and landscape architect who built Priest's Farm (sector 12) between 1926 and 1930 to plans by Shorey and Ritchie, is the same designer credited with the plan of Hampstead; the 2005 evaluation spells him "Schlemn", which is worth knowing before searching for him.

Where this page is <em>not</em> quoting a document held in the repository, it says so in the record. The « Portes et fenêtres » conservation rules were checked word for word against the City's own directive; the « Murs extérieurs » and « Toitures » passages, the article numbers and the amendment chain come from a reading of the consolidated by-law that this build could not repeat, because the by-law PDF itself has not been supplied.
