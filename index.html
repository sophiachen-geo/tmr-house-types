Québec Residential Typologies

Across Québec, towns and boroughs classify their own houses — in design-review by-laws, heritage inventories and typo-morphological studies. This site gathers those local catalogues into one structure: each place keeps its own vocabulary and its own build-out phases, and a canonical layer of forms and styles connects them, so a house type in one town can be read against its cousins everywhere else.

Planned communities

Places conceived and built to a plan — railway garden suburbs, company towns, planned enclaves — where a single design idea set the street pattern and the housing vocabulary together. Essay to come as the section fills in (Parts 2–6).

Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean · 1926 · 10 types

Arvida (borough of Jonquière, Saguenay)Arvida (arrondissement de Jonquière, Ville de Saguenay)

The aluminium company's model city on the Saguenay: 2,400 hectares bought by Alcoa, a plan by Harry B. Brainerd and Hjalmar E. Skougor (1926) mixing Garden City and City Beautiful ideas, and 270 houses raised in 135 days in the summer of 1926 from a handful of standardised models that the engineer Harold R. Wake had adapted to French-Canadian habits and climate. Three construction phases to 1950 produced some 2,000 company houses on more than 100 models; the 800-odd buildings of the core were declared a site patrimonial in 2018. Arvida is the site's clearest case of a typology defined by catalogue rather than by lot and massing.

Outaouais · 1800 · 14 types

GatineauVille de Gatineau

One city assembled in 2002 from five: Wright's timber settlement at the Chaudière that became Hull, the Regency resort and courthouse town of Aylmer, the paper company's Quartier-du-Moulin at Gatineau, and Buckingham and Masson-Angers to the east. Its housing is the Ottawa valley's industrial vernacular — above all the "maison allumette", the gable-front worker's house of the match-works era, built in three generations from the 1870s on the narrow lots of the 1864 Wright survey. In 2023 the City published fourteen type fiches, one per dominant residential form.

Agglomération de Montréal · 1914 · 3 types

HampsteadVille de Hampstead

A Garden City suburb founded in 1914 on the model of Hampstead in London and drawn during the 1920s by the landscape architect Leonard E. Schlemm: curving streets to slow traffic, large single-family lots with room to garden, no commerce or industry. It grew slowly — 53 residents in 1921, about a thousand and a hundred houses by 1944 — then filled after the war. Its interwar core, Vieux-Hampstead, is a secteur de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle of two-storey stone houses "fortement inspirés des styles anglais aussi présents en grand nombre à Westmount".

Ville de Montréal · 1722 · 7 types

Mercier–Hochelaga-Maisonneuve (borough of Montréal)Arrondissement de Mercier–Hochelaga-Maisonneuve

Five annexed municipalities at the east end of the island, and one of them is why this page exists. Maisonneuve was created on 27 December 1883 by the Hochelaga landowners who refused to be annexed with their neighbours, grew fifth among Canadian industrial cities by 1910 on the strength of the shoe trade, and in that year handed its municipal engineer, Marius Dufresne, the most ambitious town-planning scheme drawn in Québec at the time — a City Beautiful capital of stone civic buildings on a treed boulevard, paid for out of municipal borrowing until the borrowing ran out and the province annexed the city to Montréal in 1918. It is the third planning exercise at company-town scale on this site after Arvida and Témiscaming, and the only one where the client was a town council rather than a corporation. What the workers actually lived in was not a model house but the ordinary Montréal plex: three storeys of stone or brick, one dwelling to a floor, outside stairs, on streets lined with trees.

Agglomération de Montréal · 1912 · 13 types

Town of Mount RoyalVille de Mont-Royal

Only Québec locality built entirely to one garden-city plan, drawn by Frederick Gage Todd in 1912 for the Canadian Northern Railway. Three build-out phases, 1915–1975, each with its own family of houses; thirteen types codified in the town's design-review by-law.

Ville de Montréal · 1875 · 6 types

Outremont (borough of Montréal)Arrondissement d'Outremont

A village of 300 people in 1875 that doubled its population every fifteen years and was built out, almost entirely, between 1903 and 1930: homogeneous streets of semi-detached single-family houses and paired duplexes in the north and east, and detached "résidences cossues" climbing the mountain flank in every historicist and then every modern idiom of the century. Its planning control rests on a 2020 typo-morphological study that cut the territory into four landscape areas and twenty-nine landscape units, and on a 1992 inventory that graded every building.

Montérégie (agglomération de Longueuil) · 1857 · 10 types

Saint-LambertVille de Saint-Lambert

A South Shore railway suburb: the Victoria Bridge (1859) and the Grand Trunk turned a strip of farmland facing Montréal into an Anglo-Protestant commuter town, laid out on the old farm lots and built up between about 1875 and 1930, then rounded out by the contractor Francis King's interwar cottages and the postwar bungalow boom. Its inventory (Patri-Arch, 2020) names ten currents, from the French-regime farmhouse to the modern house, and gives each a conservation fiche.

Abitibi-Témiscamingue · 1917 · 2 types

TémiscamingVille de Témiscaming

A 1917 Garden-City company town on a hillside above Lake Timiskaming, planned by the Scottish planner Thomas Adams for Riordon Pulp and Paper: "The Adams plan adapted its roads to the land, setting the town on a hill by Lake Timiskaming and the pulp mill further down on more level ground." From 1918 to 1923 the Montréal architects Ross and Macdonald built the mill, the commercial district and all the houses of the lower town; from 1925 to 1950 William Lyon Somerville of Toronto designed the upper-district houses, hospital, schools and churches for Canadian International Paper. "A single design aesthetic, established when the town was created, prevailed throughout its development." No residential district has heritage status, and the 2026 regional inventory retained no house in the town.

Agglomération de Montréal · 1874 · 12 types

WestmountVille de Westmount

The bourgeois slope suburb of Montréal, grown over the Côte-Saint-Antoine farms between the 1870s and 1930 and controlled almost from the start: an Architectural Commission from 1916, a four-tier grading of every building, and thirty-nine heritage sectors that follow the topography from the greystone rows of the flat lower town up through the plateau and the slope to the summit "châteaux". About 4,750 dwellings, some 61 % of the stock, predate 1945. A National Historic Site since 2011 for "the diversity and the integrity of its components".

Capitale-Nationale (Ville de Québec, arrondissement de Charlesbourg) · 1665 · 8 types

Charlesbourg (Trait-Carré)Site patrimonial de Charlesbourg

A star-shaped village the Jesuits laid out in 1665 on their seigneurie de Notre-Dame-des-Anges: forty trapezoidal farm lots radiating from a square ring road, around a common pasture, with five arpents at the dead centre reserved for church, presbytery and cemetery, and a rule obliging every farmer to build in the first third of his lot, hard against the Trait-Carré. It was the first settlement of its kind in New France and one of three the same seigneury attempted — the others, Petite-Auvergne and Bourg-Royal, had no parish core and did not survive as legible plans. Québec's suburbs arrived in the 1950s and cut up the farms, but the radial geometry is still readable on the ground, and 138 principal buildings inside 24 hectares still carry three centuries of housing, from a 1742 stone mill to the postwar bungalow.

Agglomération de Montréal (ouest de l'île) · 1667 · 2 types

DorvalCité de Dorval

A lakeshore côte where every layer is still legible along one road. The Sulpicians put a mission on this shore in 1667 and called it Gentilly, then La Présentation de la Vierge Marie; Jean- Baptiste Bouchard dit Dorval took the land in 1691 and left his name; farming held the territory until the twentieth century. The Grand Trunk reached Dorval in 1855 and brought wealthy anglophone Montrealers, who built chalets and villas along the water and founded a yacht club, a golf club and the Forest and Stream Club. The village incorporated in 1892, became a town in 1903 and a city in 1956; the airport of 1941 turned the rest of the farmland into a bungalow suburb and took the population from under 3,000 in the 1940s to more than 20,000 by 1971. Dorval was a Montréal borough from 2002 and a reconstituted cité from 2006. Nothing here is protected under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel — not a building, not a sector — and the whole weight of conservation rests on a design-review by-law over thirty-two addresses.

Ville de Montréal · 1667 · 6 types

Lachine (borough of Montréal)Arrondissement de Lachine

The one place in this section that never left Montréal. Lachine begins as a portage stop at the head of the rapides de Lachine, where the canoe routes from the pays d'en Haut had to stop; Le Moyne and Le Ber built a stone trading post there in 1669–1671 that is still standing, and still the oldest thing on the borough's ground. The canal of 1825 and the railway of 1847 turned the east end into an industrial town of worker housing and the Dominion Bridge works, while the west end — Summerlea, annexed in 1912 — filled with Shingle Style and Arts and Crafts summer houses on very large treed lots. Between 1950 and 1970 the fields in between were laid out as dormitory quarters whose dominant house type still carries a builder's name: the maison « Gameroff ». Merged into Montréal in 2002, Lachine did not demerge in 2006, and it is a borough today while every one of its West Island neighbours is a city again.

Chaudière-Appalaches · 1861 · 18 types

LévisVille de Lévis

Ten municipalities merged in 2002 into one city facing Québec across the river, and the only place in this part with no provincially declared site — its protection is municipal and discretionary. What it has instead is the best public type catalogue in the province after Gatineau's: twenty architectural currents from the Régime français to Modernisme, each with its volumetry, plan, roof, cladding, openings, projections and ornament set out in the same seven fields, keyed to more than four thousand heritage buildings and searchable by street address.

Agglomération de Montréal (West Island) · 1698 · 6 types

Pointe-ClaireVille de Pointe-Claire

Two point seven hectares at the end of a point in Lac Saint-Louis, formally designated — and the other half of this part's argument. Pointe-Claire's institutional core is a windmill of 1709, a néogothique church of 1882–1885 by Victor Bourgeau and Alcibiade Leprohon, a convent of 1867–1868 by Henri-Maurice Perrault, a presbytery of 1913 by Théodose Daoust built on 1848 masonry, and a village school; the city cited the whole of it as a site patrimonial in 2013, and the protection reaches the ground as well as the exterior envelope of every building on it. Behind the point lies a village whose houses the city governs through a form-based Code villageois that names its three architectural styles by name — traditionnelle québécoise, Boomtown, Cubique — and behind that a country-suburb of Shingle and Arts and Crafts houses on very large treed lots. Set that against the Mille carré doré in Ville-Marie, which has the richest domestic architecture on the island and no ensemble protection at all, and the shape of Québec heritage designation becomes visible.

Capitale-Nationale · 1608 · 28 types

Québec CityVille de Québec

The one place on this site whose types were named before we came looking: the City publishes a thesaurus that sorts its housing into nine currents, from the pièce-sur-pièce rural house of the French regime and the stone urban house of the walled town, through the London terrace and the Regency villa, the néoclassique québécois and its stripped-down faubourg house, the mansard that densified the working districts, to the industrial vernacular that built Limoilou and Montcalm — Boomtown, plex, cubique, immeuble à logements — and finally the postwar bungalow. Its housing history is legible because it burned repeatedly: the fires of 1845, 1866, 1876, 1881 and 1889 wrote brick walls, tin roofs and eaveless gables into the by-laws, and the types record it.

Agglomération de Montréal · 1677 · 4 types

Sainte-Anne-de-BellevueVille de Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue

The second-oldest European settlement at the west end of the island of Montréal, and a village that water made three times over: a portage past the Sainte-Anne rapids, a fur-trade halt on the way to the Pays d'en Haut, then a lock town after the canal opened in 1843. The noyau villageois gathers round the church, the presbytery and the old convent on rues Sainte-Anne and de l'Église, with modest two-storey clapboard houses built close to the street; behind it, the workers' cottages of rue Saint-Jean-Baptiste, and beyond that the campuses of Macdonald College and the garden-suburb ensemble J.-J. Harpell built round the Garden City Press in 1918. It is marketed as a « village patrimonial », and that phrase is advertising: nothing here is protected at the scale of an ensemble. Two individual buildings carry the whole statutory weight — the maison Simon-Fraser, classée in 1962, and the maison Michel-Robillard, citée in 2014.

Montréal (île de Montréal), arrondissement du Sud-Ouest · 1825 · 15 types

Le Sud-Ouest (Montréal)Arrondissement du Sud-Ouest

The borough where the Montréal plex was made, and the one place on this site whose own planning document says so in as many words. The Patri-Arch typo-morphological study reads the whole territory as five landscape areas cut into sixty-five landscape units, and reads the buildings as nineteen architectural types on one template — of which seven are plexes, defined in a single line: « Ces immeubles possèdent de deux à six logements. » Its explanation of the exterior stair is mechanical, not folkloric: the requirement to build behind a front setback, imposed as Montréal began regulating residential construction around 1880, pushed the stair to the upper dwellings out of the building and into the yard. The triplex with exterior stair appears from 1900; the duplex and the multiplex follow. And the borough's design-review by-law turns all of it into a rule — where two typological ensembles meet, a new building « doit choisir clairement une des deux typologies ».

Lanaudière (MRC Les Moulins) · 1720 · 4 types

Vieux-TerrebonneSite patrimonial du Vieux-Terrebonne

A seigneurial mill bourg laid out from 1720 on the north bank of the rivière des Mille-Îles, where the river breaks into rapids: an orthogonal grid of narrow streets dropping from rue Saint-Louis to the water, small vernacular houses packed tight along the Bas-de-la-Côte, a stone proto-industrial complex on the island, a stone parish core on the ridge, and a line of merchants' houses on the crest. Dévitalisé after the autoroute of 1965 and the boulevard of 1967 drew life away, it was rescued by a 1970s mobilisation and is now "considéré comme une réussite en manière de réhabilitation urbaine". Declared Québec's 14th site patrimonial on 20 May 2026.

Mauricie · 1634 · 7 types

Trois-RivièresVille de Trois-Rivières

Founded in 1634 and burned on 22 June 1908, Trois-Rivières is a city whose housing is legible as before and after — but not where you would expect. The fire took the commercial centre, not the old core: the ministry's own plan finds the declared site "peu affecté par l'incendie de 1908", and inside its six and a half hectares the Ursulines' monastery, a wooden house of 1757 and three classed stone houses still stand. What the fire produced instead was a reconstruction of remarkable evenness — "Rebâti par une poignée d'architectes et d'entrepreneurs, le quartier présente une grande homogénéité architecturale qui se constate encore aujourd'hui" — brick, flat-roofed, trimmed with cornices and quoins, and that homogeneity is itself the heritage value. Then the paper mills filled the parishes of Saint-Philippe, Notre-Dame-des-Sept-Allégresses and Sainte-Cécile with Boomtown houses whose diamond attic vents are a trifluvien signature, and after 1910 with the plex that replaced them.

Montréal (île de Montréal), arrondissement de Ville-Marie · 1642 · 6 types

Ville-Marie (Montréal)Arrondissement de Ville-Marie

The borough that is the city's beginning and its argument about protection. Ville-Marie holds the declared site patrimonial de Montréal, the richest domestic architecture on the island in the Mille carré doré, the greystone terraces of the Village Shaughnessy and the working-class faubourgs of the east — and the protection they carry has almost nothing to do with how good they are. Vieux-Montréal has been a declared site since 1964 and its plan de conservation has still not been produced. The Mille carré doré, graded exceptionnelle in the Ville's own 2005 cahier as sectors 24.E.29 and 24.E.30, has no ensemble designation at all: Héritage Montréal states in as many words that it <em>"does not have a designation or specific strategy to protect and promote its heritage over current urban development considerations"</em>. Six blocks of it — the Complexe-de-La-Maison-Alcan — were classed in 2017, and they are the exception that shows the rule. This is also the one place on the site whose arrondissement number is contradicted by two Ville de Montréal documents at once: the 2005 cahier prints 24 beside every sector name, the 2016 Plan d'urbanisme calls the same territory 25, and 25 in the 2005 series is Le Plateau-Mont-Royal.

Ville de Montréal · 1845 · 12 types

Le Plateau-Mont-Royal (borough of Montréal)Arrondissement du Plateau-Mont-Royal

Montréal's densest inner borough, built out between 1845 and 1914 as a cluster of villages (Coteau-Saint-Louis, Saint-Jean-Baptiste, De Lorimier, Saint-Louis-du-Mile-End) that Montréal annexed between 1886 and 1909. Its housing is the classic Montréal plex: the duplex of the 1885–91 boom, then the triplex with exterior stair of 1904–14, whose form the City's own 1880s rules on lot size, lanes, front setbacks and brick or stone cladding produced. The 2004 PIIA framework classifies ~1,500 surveyed buildings by massing, siting, dwelling count and mode of access into fifteen types in five families.

Capitale-Nationale · 1608 · 28 types

Québec CityVille de Québec

The one place on this site whose types were named before we came looking: the City publishes a thesaurus that sorts its housing into nine currents, from the pièce-sur-pièce rural house of the French regime and the stone urban house of the walled town, through the London terrace and the Regency villa, the néoclassique québécois and its stripped-down faubourg house, the mansard that densified the working districts, to the industrial vernacular that built Limoilou and Montcalm — Boomtown, plex, cubique, immeuble à logements — and finally the postwar bungalow. Its housing history is legible because it burned repeatedly: the fires of 1845, 1866, 1876, 1881 and 1889 wrote brick walls, tin roofs and eaveless gables into the by-laws, and the types record it.

Ville de Montréal · 1904 · 8 types

Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie (borough of Montréal)Arrondissement de Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie

The borough the Canadian Pacific built. When the Angus Shops opened in 1904 the promoters U.-H. Dandurand and Herbert Holt subdivided the Crawford farm beside them, and the shopmen who could not afford a plex bought a lot on a promise of sale for a few dollars down and built a one-storey flat-roofed box on it themselves, meaning to add a floor later. Some five hundred and sixty-one of those maisons shoebox survive, and in 2018–2019 Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie became the first Montréal borough to write them into its zoning by-law — house by house, with an architectural value from 1 to 3 attached to each address. Around them the borough is otherwise the standard Montréal fabric of contiguous brick duplex and triplex with exterior stairs, denser and more ornate on rue Saint-Denis; and at its eastern edge sits the Cité-Jardin du Tricentenaire, a co-operative garden suburb of 1941–1947 that answered the row plex with detached houses on curving planted streets.

Montréal (île de Montréal), arrondissement du Sud-Ouest · 1825 · 15 types

Le Sud-Ouest (Montréal)Arrondissement du Sud-Ouest

The borough where the Montréal plex was made, and the one place on this site whose own planning document says so in as many words. The Patri-Arch typo-morphological study reads the whole territory as five landscape areas cut into sixty-five landscape units, and reads the buildings as nineteen architectural types on one template — of which seven are plexes, defined in a single line: « Ces immeubles possèdent de deux à six logements. » Its explanation of the exterior stair is mechanical, not folkloric: the requirement to build behind a front setback, imposed as Montréal began regulating residential construction around 1880, pushed the stair to the upper dwellings out of the building and into the yard. The triplex with exterior stair appears from 1900; the duplex and the multiplex follow. And the borough's design-review by-law turns all of it into a rule — where two typological ensembles meet, a new building « doit choisir clairement une des deux typologies ».

Mauricie · 1634 · 7 types

Trois-RivièresVille de Trois-Rivières

Founded in 1634 and burned on 22 June 1908, Trois-Rivières is a city whose housing is legible as before and after — but not where you would expect. The fire took the commercial centre, not the old core: the ministry's own plan finds the declared site "peu affecté par l'incendie de 1908", and inside its six and a half hectares the Ursulines' monastery, a wooden house of 1757 and three classed stone houses still stand. What the fire produced instead was a reconstruction of remarkable evenness — "Rebâti par une poignée d'architectes et d'entrepreneurs, le quartier présente une grande homogénéité architecturale qui se constate encore aujourd'hui" — brick, flat-roofed, trimmed with cornices and quoins, and that homogeneity is itself the heritage value. Then the paper mills filled the parishes of Saint-Philippe, Notre-Dame-des-Sept-Allégresses and Sainte-Cécile with Boomtown houses whose diamond attic vents are a trifluvien signature, and after 1910 with the plex that replaced them.

Ville de Montréal · 1875 · 6 types

Verdun (borough of Montréal)Arrondissement de Verdun

A separate city for ninety-five years — ville from 1907, cité from 1912, borough of Montréal from 2002 — built almost entirely between 1900 and 1960 on land that a dike had to make habitable first. Verdun was the third-largest city in Québec by the 1920s and by 1946 every one of its eighteen thousand dwellings was occupied. Its housing is plex from end to end: three-storey brick fronts with the stair climbing the outside in the north, two-storey duplexes with deep balconies and stained glass in the south, row duplexes after 1950. Two things interrupt that: Crawford Park, an award-winning National Housing Act subdivision of 1½-storey brick houses laid out against the grid, and île des Sœurs, planned with Mies van der Rohe, who left three residential slabs and a filling station on it.

Montréal (arrondissement; Évaluation du patrimoine urbain code 14) · 1896 · 13 types

Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-ExtensionArrondissement de Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension

The borough with the most formal residential typology by-law in Montréal: règlement 01-283-124's Annexe F names thirteen house types, numbers them, gives each a set of lettered variants, and in several cases ties a variant to the zones it is allowed in. It is also the borough that disagrees with its neighbour about what a shoebox is. Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie treats the shoebox as one type; Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension splits it in two — a 1900-1940 shoebox of contiguous brick fronts, found « principalement dans Villeray et Parc-Extension », and a 1940-1960 shoebox that sheds its ornament, lifts its ground floor over a basement, detaches from its neighbours and pulls back from the street to make room for a car. The borough's own census says why: of its 1 159 shoebox houses, 544 were built in the eleven years from 1950 to 1960. Around that argument sits everything else — a single exceptional sector, rue Saint-Denis, singled out for its three-storey plex; five interesting sectors; three ensembles urbains d'intérêt, two of them built on Wartime Housing Limited models; and a limestone quarry with lime kilns as the only archaeological interest in a borough where no archaeological research has ever been done.

Section D

Rural seigneurial territories

The first places on this site whose organising unit is the farm lot rather than the street. A seigneurial territory was divided into long narrow *terres* running back from a river or a road, and the house sat where the survey put it — so the shape of the settlement is a land division, not a plan of blocks. Île d'Orléans strings six parishes along a single ring road around an island still 95 % agricultural; Charlesbourg's Trait-Carré radiates forty trapezoidal lots from a common, the first settlement of its kind in New France. Both are provincial *sites patrimoniaux déclarés*, and in both the oldest house type on this site — the low stone house of French inspiration — is still the commonest old building on the ground.

Agglomération de Montréal · 1686 · 2 types

Baie-D'UrféVille de Baie-D'Urfé

A shore town of Sulpician farm lots that never grew a village. The chemin du Roy — later the Queen's Highway, now the chemin du Lakeshore — runs along the lac Saint-Louis past what the 2005 heritage evaluation calls the densest concentration of heritage buildings in its arrondissement: old farmhouses, bourgeois houses and summer villas of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, standing far back from the road behind mature trees, with no sidewalks. Detached from Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue and chartered in 1911 with fewer than two hundred inhabitants, the town quintupled in the 1950s and is now almost entirely postwar. It is spoken of as a heritage village and it is not one: a single building is protected, the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette of about 1700, and only the outer envelope of its oldest wing. Everything else is held by a demolition by-law, design review, and lots so large they cannot be subdivided.

Capitale-Nationale (Ville de Québec, arrondissement de Charlesbourg) · 1665 · 8 types

Charlesbourg (Trait-Carré)Site patrimonial de Charlesbourg

A star-shaped village the Jesuits laid out in 1665 on their seigneurie de Notre-Dame-des-Anges: forty trapezoidal farm lots radiating from a square ring road, around a common pasture, with five arpents at the dead centre reserved for church, presbytery and cemetery, and a rule obliging every farmer to build in the first third of his lot, hard against the Trait-Carré. It was the first settlement of its kind in New France and one of three the same seigneury attempted — the others, Petite-Auvergne and Bourg-Royal, had no parish core and did not survive as legible plans. Québec's suburbs arrived in the 1950s and cut up the farms, but the radial geometry is still readable on the ground, and 138 principal buildings inside 24 hectares still carry three centuries of housing, from a 1742 stone mill to the postwar bungalow.

Capitale-Nationale (MRC de L'Île-d'Orléans) · 1636 · 11 types

Île d'OrléansSite patrimonial de l'Île-d'Orléans

An island of six parishes strung along a single ring road, declared a heritage site in 1970 and farmed continuously since 1648 — about 95 % of it is still agricultural. Its housing is the clearest surviving sequence of the Québec rural canon anywhere: the low stone house of French inspiration with its steep eaveless roof; the neoclassical Québec house raised on a cellar with curved eaves reaching out over a gallery; the mansard; the Regency cottage of the Sainte-Pétronille villégiature; then the 20th-century American vernacular, cubique and Boomtown arriving by catalogue. The nineteen classed houses are its spine, and the cote A–E its working instrument.

Agglomération de Montréal (pointe ouest de l'île) · 1672 · 3 types

SennevilleVillage de Senneville

A fief conceded in 1672, a fur-trade fort and mill on the point by 1703, farms fronting the lac des Deux-Montagnes for two centuries — and then, when the railway reached the west of the island, the richest single concentration of country-estate architecture in Canada. Between about 1865 and 1930 a dozen Montréal financiers — Abbott, Clouston, Forget, Angus, Morgan, Todd — bought the farms and commissioned Edward and William Sutherland Maxwell, Nobbs and Hyde, Robert Findlay and others to build manor houses, lodges, stables, dairies, chapels and tea houses on picturesque grounds laid out by Frederick G. Todd working with Frederick Law Olmsted. Twenty Maxwell buildings survive. The village was created in 1895 with Louis-Joseph Forget as its first mayor, merged into Montréal in 2002 and reconstituted in 2006. Parks Canada designated the whole 1,400-acre belt a National Historic Site in 2001; Québec has classed only the fort ruins, 0.8 hectares of it. From the road you can see almost none of it, and that invisibility is itself on the federal list of what makes the place what it is.
