Bourgeois residence of the cité, Beaux-Arts La demeure bourgeoise d'inspiration Beaux-Arts

"North facade of the Château Dufresne, 4040 Sherbrooke East, Montreal, Quebec, Canada" by Thomas1313, 18 April 2012, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0 — licence read on the Commons API before download. File: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Façade_avant_-_Château_Dufresne.JPG

The exception the record keeps insisting on. Two houses for two brothers built as one Beaux-Arts block in Indiana limestone — Marius Dufresne, who as municipal engineer designed Maisonneuve's public buildings, on one side, and Oscar Dufresne, who ran the family shoe factory, on the other — standing on twenty reinforced-concrete piers at a moment when almost no house in Montréal did. Forty rooms, a winter garden, garages underground.

Where it comes from

Maisonneuve was 80 % francophone and, in the Évaluation's phrase, « composée surtout de travailleurs et de locataires »; its promoters had founded the place in 1883 to be, as the RPCQ puts it, « la "Westmount des francophones" ». The bourgeois house is where that ambition became a building. The Dufresne brothers were both instruments of it: Oscar sat as échevin and ran Dufresne et Locke, the shoe manufactory established at Maisonneuve about 1900 and one of its largest employers; Marius was municipal engineer from 1910 to 1918 and drew the market (1912–1914), the fire station (1914–1915) and the public bath and gymnasium (1914–1916). They began their own house in 1915, the year the civic building programme was breaking the city's finances, and finished it in 1918, the year the province annexed Maisonneuve to Montréal. It was classé in 1976; the civic buildings had to wait until 2022.

What RPCQ fiche 92740 says about this type

Siting & landscape
  • Set on a large landscaped ground behind a cast-iron fence, on one of the two thoroughfares laid out for prestige — rue Sherbrooke Est, or boulevard Pie-IX, whose subdivision « privilégiait de riches constructions sur des terrains plus grands que ce que l'on retrouve dans les environs ».
  • Garages and other service space put underground at the rear rather than on the street front.
Massing
  • Rectangular two-storey corps de logis under a flat roof ringed by a balustrade.
  • Two paired houses composed as one unified block, with lateral wings set back from the main façade and rising only to half height, and a rear annex holding a winter garden.
Articulation
  • Symmetry and frontality — two avant-corps framing a central section carried on three pairs of coupled Ionic columns.
  • Classical ornament — column capitals, entablature and the terrace roof edged with a balustrade. The RPCQ names this house, with the Maisonneuve bain public et gymnase and the Saint-Sulpice library, as an illustration of Beaux-Arts composition applied in Québec.
Openings
  • Vertical rectangular and round-headed openings, disposed symmetrically about the central bay.
Materials
  • Indiana limestone facing.
  • Structure and twenty massive piers in reinforced concrete.

Every line here is read off one building, the Château Dufresne (Maisons Marius et Oscar Dufresne, 4040 rue Sherbrooke Est, 1915–1918, Marius Dufresne with the French architect Jules Renard), because it is the only house of this class in the borough with a fiche of its own; the Évaluation says as much — « Des demeures somptueuses sont également construites pour la bourgeoisie canadienne-française, mais elles constituent l'exception. Le meilleur exemple en est le château Dufresne. » Sector 27.E.3 establishes that a whole street of such houses was intended on boulevard Pie-IX and that « plusieurs bâtiments témoignent encore du faste qu'avait autrefois cette voie », but it names none of them, so the siting line is the sector's and the rest is the Château's. ONE CORRECTION TO THE PART 10 MEMO. The RPCQ does not say this was the first reinforced-concrete private residence in Montréal. Its wording is « l'une des premières utilisations de ce matériau pour la construction résidentielle à Montréal, voire au Québec », and again « il s'agit de l'une des premières résidences à structure en béton armé à Montréal » — one of the first, not the first. The superlative is not in the source and is not published here. On the style tags: <em>beaux-arts</em> is the RPCQ's own word for this building, repeated four times in its fiche. <em>city-beautiful</em> is attached deliberately and is the only house record on this site that carries it, because this is the house the author of Maisonneuve's City Beautiful plan built for himself while executing it, and because the RPCQ ties the two together explicitly — Beaux-Arts is a current « fréquemment associé aux projets urbains d'inspiration City Beautiful ». The tag belongs to the designer and the moment, not to a set of formal features that could be found in another house.
