French-regime stone house Maison de pierre du Régime français

Maison Du Calvet, rue Saint-Paul at rue Bonsecours, Vieux-Montréal — rubble stone walls, a steep two-slope roof, chimneys carried up in the plane of the gable walls and firebreak walls stepping above the roof. Photograph by Mathieu BROSSAIS, 7 August 2008, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Maison_Du_Calvet_%C3%A0_Montr%C3%A9al.jpg — licence, author and file URL read through the Commons API before download; the photograph was opened and checked against its description.

The oldest domestic form on this site and the one that recurs in six places: low walls of roughly squared rubble, a two-slope or hipped roof of medium pitch, chimneys rising in the plane of the gable walls, firebreak walls between neighbours, and small-paned wooden casements. In Vieux-Montréal it is an urban house — built to the street line, party-walled, on a narrow lot — rather than the freestanding rural house of the Île d'Orléans or the côtes.

Where it comes from

Ville-Marie was founded in 1642 as a missionary settlement and became the bridgehead of the fur trade. The Sulpicians became seigneurs of the island in 1663, and their superior François Dollier de Casson traced the first streets in 1672 with the notary-surveyor Bénigne Basset Des Lauriers: three streets parallel to the river and seven perpendicular to it. Between 1685 and 1800 the town was walled, and the wall fixed its form. The intendants' ordonnances then fixed the relation between street and building, including the alignment of construction; stone became the preferred material for new building, which is why so little of the wooden town survives. The form is the same one recorded on this site at Île d'Orléans, Charlesbourg, Québec, Terrebonne and Lachine — the same walls, the same roof, the same chimneys — with the difference that here it is packed against its neighbours.

What RPCQ id 93528 (MCC); Ville de Montréal says about this type

Description (source)
  • « Les immeubles les plus anciens - habitations et édifices conventuels - qui datent de l’époque de la Nouvelle-France présentent une maçonnerie en pierre et des toits à pignon. »
Siting & landscape
  • Built to the street line inside the walled town, on the narrow lots of Dollier de Casson's 1672 tracé; the RPCQ's characteristic elements list the alignment of buildings on the public way, the high density of ground occupation and party-wall construction as the site's defining relation.
  • Interior courts and courettes in some sectors, reached from the street.
  • The surviving concentration is in sector 24.E.45, the quartier Bonsecours, which the cahier says is the one part of Vieux-Montréal that kept its residential vocation after 1850.
Massing
  • Generally low — one or two storeys.
  • Two-slope or hipped roof of medium pitch.
  • Chimneys carried up in the plane of the gable walls, and firebreak walls between houses.
Articulation
  • Not stated by either source beyond the masonry itself. The RPCQ describes this family in one sentence and does not treat its ornament.
Openings
  • Wooden casement windows with small panes.
Materials
  • Rubble masonry of roughly squared stone.
  • Grey limestone predominates in the site as a whole until 1850, after which a wide variety of stone types appears.
Éléments caractéristiques (source)
  • « La présence de bâtiments du Régime français présentant des structures généralement basses (un ou deux étages), des toitures à deux versants ou à croupes de pente moyenne, des murs de moellons grossièrement équarris, des cheminées dans la continuité des murs pignons, des murs coupe-feu et des fenêtres en bois à battants et à petits carreaux. »
  • « L’utilisation de matériaux traditionnels marquée par une prédominance de la pierre calcaire grise jusqu’en 1850, puis par une grande variété de types de pierre. »
