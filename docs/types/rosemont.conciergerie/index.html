The point at which a Montréal building stops giving each dwelling its own front door and starts giving them all one hall. The cahier notes a few of them in the eastern part of La Petite-Patrie, dating from the first decades of the twentieth century, and says no more.

Where it comes from

La Petite-Patrie's eastern sector was built between 1885 and 1930, and the conciergeries the cahier notes belong to the later end of that span. They are recorded here so that the borough's place in the canonical type's list of occurrences is honest, and because their scarcity is itself the finding: in a borough of 561 self-built one-storey houses and street after street of two- and three-dwelling plex, the shared-hall apartment building is a marginal form.

This is the thinnest record on the page and is published thin on purpose. The cahier mentions conciergeries in Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie in exactly one clause, quoted above in full, and gives no description of them whatever. The columns are therefore almost entirely empty, and the storeys, cladding and tenure_plan values above are carried from what the canonical type means rather than from this borough's source — they should be read as the canonical's defaults, not as evidence from Rosemont. The line that defines the form — a common entrance hall serving six to twelve dwellings, which is where the plex family stops — comes from Le Sud-Ouest's typo-morphological study, not from here. Nothing else has been imported.
