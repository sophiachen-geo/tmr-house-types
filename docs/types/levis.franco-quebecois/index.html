The step between the French-regime house and the Québécois house: still one and a half storeys and low to the ground, but the eaves now curve out in a bellcast and the windows line up symmetrically. Stone, render, clapboard or shingle; a chimney and a stoop.

Where it comes from

The transitional form of the decades either side of 1800, when Pointe-Lévy was a ferry landing and the shipyards at Lauzon were beginning. The bellcast eave that defines it is the single feature that separates it from the French-regime house behind it and connects it to the Québécois house that follows. About fifty are counted city-wide.

What Ville de Lévis says about this type

Siting & landscape
  • The City counts about 50 buildings of this current across the merged territory of Lévis (fiche, « Nombre à Lévis »).
Massing
  • One and a half storeys, sitting low to the ground.
  • Rectangular plan.
  • Two-slope roof with a curved bellcast eave, of steep pitch, covered in wood shingle or Canadian-pan tin.
  • Chimney; entrance stoop.
  • 1 ½ étage peu dégagé du sol
  • cheminée, perron
  • rectangulaire
  • à 2 versants à larmier recourbé, à pente forte, revêtement de bardeau de bois ou de tôle à la canadienne
Articulation
  • Window and door surrounds; corner boards where the cladding is wood.
  • chambranles, planches cornières lorsque le revêtement est en bois
Openings
  • Casement windows with small or large panes, symmetrically placed; gabled dormers.
  • fenêtres à battants à petits ou à grands carreaux, symétriques, lucarnes à pignon
Materials
  • Stone, stucco render, wood clapboard or wood shingle.
  • pierre, crépi, clin ou bardeau de bois
