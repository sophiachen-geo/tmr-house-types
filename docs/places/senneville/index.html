Residential Architectural Styles of Senneville

Senneville is a road with almost nothing visible from it. The chemin de Senneville runs along the shore of the lac des Deux-Montagnes at the western tip of the island of Montréal, and behind the trees on either side of it lie about a dozen country estates that between them hold the densest concentration of Arts and Crafts domestic architecture in Canada. Parks Canada's own list of what makes the district what it is includes, without irony, "the siting and landscaping of most residences, which prevents a view to the buildings from chemin Senneville." The invisibility is a designated heritage attribute.

The fief was conceded in 1672 to Michel-Sidrac Dugué, sieur de Boisbriand — a name that survives as the Abbott and Clouston estate, spelled Boisbriand by the RPCQ and by the Ville de Montréal cahier and Bois-Briant by Parks Canada — and sold in 1679 to Jacques Le Ber, from whom the place takes its name. Le Ber raised a fortified wooden mill in 1686, saw it burned in an Iroquois raid in 1691, and rebuilt it in stone under a contract of 1700; his son Jacques Le Ber de Senneville (1663–1735) built the private stone fort of a house, four corner bastions and a walled court in 1702–03. The RPCQ calls Fort Senneville an « exemple unique de fort privé en Nouvelle-France ». Benedict Arnold's troops destroyed it in 1776. For the two centuries after that the territory was farms fronting the water, and — this is the point the 2005 cahier makes and no summary should drop — it never produced a village core at all: « sans toutefois donner naissance à un noyau villageois, les résidants utilisant plutôt l'église et les commerces de Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue ».

What made the place was the railway and the money it carried west. In the cahier's words, « Au cours de la seconde moitié du XIXe siècle, le chemin de fer est prolongé jusque dans l'ouest de l'île, ce qui amène la bourgeoisie anglophone montréalaise à construire des villas en milieu agricole. Certains optent toutefois pour l'acquisition de fermes tout en laissant l'exploitation des terres à des agriculteurs. » Between about 1865 and 1930 the founders, presidents and directors of the Bank of Montreal and the Canadian Pacific bought the farms: Sir John Joseph Caldwell Abbott, mayor of Montréal and Canada's third prime minister; the banker Sir Edward Seaborne Clouston; the senator and stockbroker Louis-Joseph Forget; the banker Richard Bladworth Angus; the McGill parasitologist John Lancelot Todd; the collector Frederick Cleveland Morgan. They employed a small circle of architects — above all the brothers Edward and William Sutherland Maxwell, with George Cutler Shattuck; Percy Erskine Nobbs with George Taylor Hyde; Robert Findlay; David Shennan — and landscape designers Frederick G. Todd and Frederick Law Olmsted. More than thirty major projects; twenty surviving Maxwell buildings, which the federal record calls "a unique example in a single location of their work."

Senneville was incorporated as a village in 1895, when it and Baie-D'Urfé broke away from the parish of Sainte-Anne, and its first council met on 9 May 1896 under its first mayor, Louis-Joseph Forget — the man who was then building Bois-de-la-Roche. It was merged into Montréal in 2002 and reconstituted on 1 January 2006.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

1663–1760 · Fief, fort and mill on the point

1663 – 1760

The fief of Senneville is conceded in 1672 to Michel-Sidrac Dugué, sieur de Boisbriand, who settles a small number of colonists but whose interests are mainly in the fur trade. It is sold to Jacques Le Ber in 1679 with no mention of buildings; Charles Le Moyne cedes his share in 1683, leaving Le Ber sole holder. A wooden store for trade with Indigenous people seems to stand on the fief by that date, though where is unknown. With the Sulpicians' permission Le Ber puts up a fortified wooden mill in 1686 — the presence of a mill is an argument for settlers, at a moment when the Iroquois are attacking the French outposts of the west of the island — and it is burned in a raid in 1691. The mill is rebuilt in stone under a construction contract of 1700, and three years later Le Ber's son, Jacques Le Ber de Senneville (1663–1735), builds a stone fort of a house, four corner bastions and a walled court on the riverside terrace of the present pointe Abbott. The RPCQ reads the fort as a witness to a trade that was largely illicit, royal ordinances then restricting trade with Indigenous people outside the main centres; the reopening of the trade toward the pays d'En Haut in 1725 makes the fort obsolete. It is destroyed in 1776 by Benedict Arnold's troops during the American invasion — the archaeologists found fragments of exploded grenades that explain how.

1760–1855 · Farms on the lac des Deux-Montagnes, no village

1760 – 1855

Farms. The land is divided into lots fronting the water, the houses and their outbuildings standing along the chemin de Senneville, and the territory serves exclusively agriculture until the middle of the nineteenth century. No parish, no village, no church: Senneville's residents use Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue's. Two stone farmhouses of this order survive inside what is now the parc agricole du Bois-de-la-Roche and are the oldest buildings the Ville de Montréal's 2018 heritage statement names — the maisons Eustache-Rouleau and Joseph-Lalonde, « des maisons de pierres remarquables pour leur ancienneté, construites par le maçon Charles Brunet au début du XIXe siècle », each with a stone carré clearing the ground-floor level, a two-slope roof, wooden doors and windows, a date stone and carved corbels. Senneville's own PIIA guide dates the maison Eustache-Rouleau at 294 chemin de Senneville to 1836.

1855–1930 · The railway and the estate belt

1855 – 1930

The railway is extended into the west of the island in the second half of the nineteenth century and the estate belt follows. In 1865 the Montréal lawyer John Joseph Caldwell Abbott buys the point where the fort ruins stand and lays it out as a domaine champêtre, keeping the ruins; in 1898 the banker Edward Seaborne Clouston acquires the property, then called Boisbriand, renovates the buildings and leaves the ruins alone precisely for their picturesque romanticism. Frederick G. Todd, working with Frederick Law Olmsted, designs the grounds of the Abbott/Clouston estate; Olmsted also designs the grounds of the Forget estate. Edward Maxwell builds Bois-de-la-Roche for Senator Louis-Joseph Forget in 1899 and renovates the old Senneville mill about 1898; Nobbs and Hyde build the J.L. Todd house, its ancillary buildings and its landscaped grounds at 180 in 1911–13; David Shennan builds the Frederick Cleveland Morgan house at 264 in 1912. The village is created in 1895 and holds its first council on 9 May 1896 under Forget. By 1930 the belt holds about a dozen estates and at least eighty-two buildings, and, as the federal record notes, because they were made by a few owners using a few designers over a concentrated period, they have "a strong formal and stylistic coherence."

The summer house of anglophone Montréal on the western lake shore — free-standing in its own grounds, set back and screened from the road, two to two and a half storeys of shingle over rubble stone, big loose roofs and a deep arcaded verandah facing the water. The railway of 1855 made it possible; sailing, golf and horse racing came with it; and at Dorval speculation on the shore has already taken the two largest of them. One record, two municipalities: the photograph on this card is Senneville's Wanklyn house, and Dorval's maison Beaurivage stands beside it on the type page.

Where it comes from

From the late nineteenth century the shores of lac Saint-Louis and the lac des Deux-Montagnes became the summer address of bourgeois Montréal, and the train is what did it. At Dorval the village fed both the summer people and the farmers at once, and the resort came with a whole apparatus — sailing, horse racing, golf, a yacht club, a Protestant chapel of fieldstone built for the villégiateurs in 1898. Île Dorval, bought in 1853 by Sir George Simpson of the Hudson's Bay Company for a summer house in the woods, was cut into fifty-seven lots in 1911, and about fifteen families were summering there by the 1930s; it is still only reachable by boat. At Senneville the same impulse produced something denser and grander, a dozen estates along one country road with their houses turned to the lake and hidden from the chemin, and Parks Canada designated the whole of it. The two places show the same form at two scales, and they show the same erosion: the Senneville fiche records the great lots being cut up and the territory densifying, and Dorval's records speculation on shore land, the disappearance of the great properties and new houses of a size that breaks the street. Ashburton of about 1879 is gone. The stone manor that J. W. McConnell built after it burned in 1963. What is left of that property is the entrance pavilion and one summer house in its gardens.

Siting & landscape
  • Free-standing on a large treed lot between the shore road and the water, set well back from the road and, at Senneville, deliberately screened from it — Parks Canada records that the siting and planting of most Senneville houses "prevents a view to the buildings from chemin Senneville".
  • At Dorval the belt runs along the chemin du Bord-du-Lac, the old shore road, and out onto the points that reach into lac Saint-Louis. Île Dorval was divided into 57 lots for summer houses in 1911 and is still reachable only by ferry.
  • Boat clubs, a yacht club, horse racing and golf belong to the same siting. The Dorval cahier treats them as part of the phase, not as background.
Massing
  • Two to two and a half storeys, informal in plan, with the volumes and roofs put together loosely rather than symmetrically — the Senneville fiche says the houses « agencent des volumes nets et des toitures importantes de façon plus ou moins complexe ».
  • Large roofs carrying dormers, and deep verandahs across the front, often arcaded on heavy rubble piers.

Arts and Crafts country estate Le domaine Arts and Crafts

Style: Arts and Crafts
source ›
Phase attribution: verified

A dozen country estates on one road, more than 1,400 acres, and at least 82 buildings put up between 1860 and 1930 — houses of stone and shingle set far back in wooded grounds, each with its own stable, chapel, farm buildings, tea house and gatekeeper's lodge in the same manner, and grounds designed to keep the house out of sight from the chemin. The Maxwells built dozens of them; twenty survive.

Where it comes from

Sir John Joseph Caldwell Abbott, mayor of Montréal and later prime minister, bought a Gothic revival house at Senneville for a second residence, and the rest of financial Montréal followed him — Forget, Angus, Clouston, Morgan, Todd, founders and presidents of the Bank of Montreal and the Canadian Pacific among them. Between 1865 and 1930 they turned the old Senneville farms into estates and hired one small circle of architects and landscape architects to do it: Edward and William Sutherland Maxwell, often with George Cutler Shattuck; Percy Erskine Nobbs with George Taylor Hyde; Robert Findlay; and, for the grounds, Frederick G. Todd working with the American Frederick Law Olmsted. Because so few people commissioned so few designers over so short a period, the result has, in Parks Canada's words, "a strong formal and stylistic coherence" — which is exactly what makes it legible as a type rather than a collection. The 2005 Ville de Montréal cahier, which is not given to superlatives, calls the road one of the most picturesque in Montréal and the sector exceptional « non seulement à Montréal, mais au Canada ». Set that against the Mille carré doré in Part 11's other half, which has no ensemble protection at all: a country road of eighty-two seasonal houses is a National Historic Site, and the richest domestic architecture on the island is not. That asymmetry is the argument of this part, and Senneville is one end of it.

Siting & landscape
  • The estate, not the house, is the unit. Parks Canada counts "more than 1400 acres" and "at least 82 buildings constructed between 1860 and 1930 as part of about a dozen country estates", strung along one country road that runs parallel to the lac des Deux-Montagnes.

1930–2001 · Arboretum, veterans' housing and the postwar streets

1930 – 2001

The estates stop being built and start being subdivided. The Morgan Arboretum, formerly estate land, is dated 1912–45 on the federal list and is integrated into Macdonald College in the 1940s. Beside the Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue veterans' hospital, built in 1945, the Senneville Lodge convalescent buildings go up. From the first half of the twentieth century onward a postwar subdivision of nearly two hundred houses fills the southern part of the village on an orthogonal grid that contrasts sharply with the organic development of the rest — streets without sidewalks and with ditches, mature vegetation, deep setbacks, and houses that, the cahier says, « reprend très souvent des éléments de composition des grandes propriétés et de l'architecture rurale de l'ancienne municipalité de Senneville : murs de pierre, formes des toits, bâtiments ancillaires et aménagements paysagers ». The Pine Bluff residence, R.B. Angus's Maxwell house, is demolished in 1950; five of its outbuildings remain. In 1991 the Communauté urbaine de Montréal buys most of the Forget family farm to make the parc agricole du Bois-de-la-Roche.

One storey, hipped roof, brick with artificial stone and clapboard for variety, a carport or a garage, a cul-de-sac and mature trees. Built from a short catalogue of models across all six West Island places from the late 1940s, and the only twentieth-century housing the 2005 evaluation series took seriously enough to invent a category for.

Where it comes from

The wave arrives at different moments and for different reasons. At Dorval it is the airport: the aerodrome opened in 1941, air traffic grew fast after the war, the rest of the territory was opened to subdivision and the population went from under 3,000 in the 1940s to more than 20,000 by 1971. At Pointe-Claire it is a straight housing shortage — a veterans' sector on the Wartime Housing Limited model about 1947, then the Magil project of 1953–55. At Lachine it is what the cahier calls « l'urbanisation massive des années 1950-1970 », running east across the last open ground and producing a catalogue of contractors' types so consistent that the dominant one is still known locally by its builder's name. At Baie-D'Urfé it is the Métropolitaine and the demographic boom together, pushing settlement north across the railway for the first time. What the 2005 cahiers do with all of it is the interesting part. The series invented a category, Ensembles urbains d'intérêt, to hold postwar suburbs, said openly that the exercise was exploratory, and then valued these places for their street layouts, their planting and the consistency of their materials rather than for their architecture — while recording, in the same paragraphs, that almost every house had already been raised, extended, re-clad or re-roofed. It is the one wave on these six pages that the source treats as heritage-in-progress rather than as heritage.

The 2005 Ville de Montréal series created a category — Ensembles urbains d'intérêt — expressly to catch postwar suburban housing, and Dorval's cahier alone gives it eight sectors with real building description, so the columns here are unusually well supported for a place with no typology document. But the support is not even across the six places, and that is the thing to know before trusting this record. Dorval and Pointe-Claire are described in detail. Lachine is well described too, but most of what its sector 19.U.2 describes is the one- or two-storey contractor's box that Lachine's own page records as the maison « Gameroff »; only the Californian bungalow alignment at the 51e Avenue is unambiguously this type, and that is what is claimed here. Senneville is attested by one clause in sector 2.E.4, which records postwar houses inside the estate belt and says they continue its tradition of setting buildings into the landscape — no bungalow sector of its own is named, and the well described bungalow sector in the same cahier, 2.U.2 Belvédère, is in the Pierrefonds half of that arrondissement and not in Senneville. Baie-D'Urfé is attested only by its cahier's historique, which dates the acceleration of its suburban growth to the 1950s–60s and to the Métropolitaine, and names no postwar sector; the postwar ensembles in that cahier, 4.U.1 Devon-York with its « bâtiments de l'après-guerre … rappelant le "ranch" californien », 4.U.2 Sunny Acres and 4.U.3 Lakeview-Morgan, are introduced under a paragraph about the territory of Beaconsfield. Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue is the one membership no retrieved source attests at all: its cahier, once found, names no postwar sector and its single ensemble urbain d'intérêt, 1.U.1, is the Garden City Press ensemble of J.-J. Harpell, which is not this type. It is kept in `places` because the record is the shared West Island postwar record and because a later source may supply what this one does not, and it is flagged here rather than asserted quietly. Fields left null and why: `roofing` — no source names a roof covering anywhere in the six places; `roof.pitch_deg` — « toit pavillon » with no figure; `lot_width_m`, `setback_front_m`, `setback_side_m`, `front_yard_green_pct` — the cahiers describe setbacks, front-yard parking and landscaping at length and measure none of it; `sectors` — a shared record cannot carry sector codes that exist in only one of its places, and here they would run 7.U.x, 5.U.x, 19.U.x and 2.U.x at once. `window_proportion: horizontal` is the weakest entry in the record: it is read from the form and from the Dorval note that window types vary between otherwise identical houses, not from any stated proportion, and it should be the first field to correct. `styles: [minimal-traditional]` is a classification made here, not a style any West Island source names; the nearest a source comes is Dorval's « banlieue de type californien » and Lachine's « bungalows d'inspiration californienne ».

Conservation guidance (fiche)
  • This is the only wave in the West Island whose recognition is explicitly experimental. The 2005 series says its own identification of these sectors « est exploratoire » and aims to link them to conservation concerns « pour une première fois ».
  • Where the cahiers return to it, they say the same thing twice - the value lies in the plan, the planting and the consistency of the materials rather than in any individual house, and personalisation is what erodes it. 2001–2026 · Designation, merger, demerger

2001 – 2026

The Historic Sites and Monuments Board of Canada designates the Senneville Historic District in 2001, and the recognition is formally dated 2002-07-18. Québec classes the Fort-Senneville ruins as a site patrimonial on 20 November 2003 — 8,000 m² out of 1,400 acres, and still the only statutory protection in the village. Senneville is merged into the Ville de Montréal on 1 January 2002 and reconstituted as a village on 1 January 2006 after the referendums of 20 June 2004 under Bill 9. The Ville de Montréal publishes an énoncé de l'intérêt patrimonial for the Bois-de-la-Roche site on 13 February 2018; the village's PIIA by-law 452 gains its Guide des principes architecturaux in 2019, drawn for a new subdivision but opening with the first published account of Senneville's house architecture as a set of types. In 2019 Héritage Montréal opposes demolition permits for the surviving Pine Bluff dependencies.

Notes on the approach

The designation year is 2001, and the same record also says 2002. Parks Canada's record, id 1973 federally and id 4442 on the Canadian Register, carries both figures in different fields, and they are not in conflict once you see what each is. The heritage-value text — the part that states the reasons for designation — begins "Senneville Historic District was designated a national historic site of Canada in 2001 because:" and ends "Source: Historic Sites and Monuments Board of Canada, Minutes, 2001." That is the Board's decision. The Recognition Details block at the foot of the same page gives Recognition Date: 2002/07/18, the ministerial recognition that follows a Board recommendation, and Listed on the Canadian Register: 2008/04/16. This page publishes 2001, the year the record's own prose gives in both languages — the French text reads « L'Arrondissement historique de Senneville a été désigné lieu historique national du Canada en 2001 » — and footnotes the 2002 claim, which is what Héritage Montréal's Memento and other secondary sources print, and which is the 2002-07-18 field read as though it were the designation.

The landscape attribution is to Frederick Law Olmsted — the elder, or the firm. The federal record's sentence is: "Landscape architect and urban planner Frederick G. Todd (1876-1948), working with American landscape designer Frederick Law Olmsted, designed the grounds of the Abbott/Clouston estate. Olmsted also designed the grounds of the Forget estate." The French text says the same — « en collaboration avec le paysagiste américain Frederick Law Olmsted ». The record carries no generational suffix and neither does this page; the commonly repeated attribution to the son is not supported by any source read here. The Ville de Montréal cahier's list of designers for sector 2.E.4 is « Maxwell, Findlay, Hyde, Nobbs, Olmsted, Todd, Rose » — also unqualified, and adding a seventh name, Rose, that appears in none of the other sources consulted and is recorded here without further identification.

How many named estates there are, and why the answer is not eight. The Part 11 brief calls for "Senneville's eight named estates" and then lists seven — Dow, Hillcote, the J.L. Todd house and stable, Chatblanc, Bois-de-la-Roche, Pine Bluff and Le Sabot. Counted from the sources rather than from the brief, the number is larger and depends on which source you count. Parks Canada's character-defining elements name twelve estate entries with addresses on the chemin de Senneville, eleven of them carrying a proper house name and one — the house at 246, of 1894 — carrying only an address: Dow (140), Hillcote (149-65), Bois-Briant (168-70), the J.L. Todd estate (180), Chatblanc (200), Bally Bawn (202), the R.B. Angus estate (216-19), Wanklyn (238), Birchfield (240), 246, Le Bousquet (264) and the Forget estate at 292. The Ville de Montréal cahier's list of immeubles de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle names twenty-five addresses on the chemin de Senneville, sixteen of them with a proper house name, and adds Château rose (26), the maison William-George-MacVicar-Stuart (31), the maison Crevier (108), the maison John C-Abbott (170), the propriété Abbot (178), the tour d'eau James-Morgan (249), the maison Eustache-Rouleau (294), the maison Lalonde (296) and the maison Guy-Boyer ou Blanche-Forget (330). Eight is not a figure either source produces.

The two names of the house at 264, and the two addresses of Bois-de-la-Roche. Parks Canada calls 264 chemin de Senneville "the Frederick Cleveland Morgan house, 'Le Bousquet' (264 Senneville; 1912)"; the Ville cahier calls it simply la maison Frederick-Cleveland-Morgan; Héritage Montréal and the Commons category call the same 1912 David Shennan house Le Sabot. All three refer to one building and this page uses the address rather than choosing between the names. Bois-de-la-Roche looks like a similar conflict and is not one: the Ville cahier lists « 290, chemin de Senneville — Maison Louis-Joseph-Forget ou Bois-de-la-Roche » and a separate, unnamed 292; Parks Canada attaches the whole Forget estate, chapel, cowshed and ancillary buildings to 292; and the Ville's archive catalogues the Maxwell barn-stable as 292B chemin de Senneville. The house is at 290, the farm group at 292, and both numbers are correct.

Pine Bluff is real, and the main house is gone. The name does not appear in the federal record, which lists only "five ancillary buildings on the R.B. Angus estate (216-19 Senneville; 1895-1903; Peach House, a small country house, the caretaker's house, a greenhouse and a barn)". It does appear in the Ville de Montréal cahier, whose exceptional list reads « 216-219, chemin de Senneville — Dépendances de la maison R.-B.-Angus, Pine Bluff » — dependencies, note, not the house. Héritage Montréal records that the main residence was demolished in 1950 and that in 2019 it asked the village to refuse demolition permits for what is left. The federal record lists the dependencies rather than the house because by 2001 the house had been gone for half a century.

One thousand four hundred acres designated, eight thousand square metres protected. This is the sharpest instance in this part of the site of designation and protection being different things. The National Historic Site covers "more than 1400 acres" and confers no permit power over private land; it names, it records, it plaques. The only element in Senneville carrying a statut under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel is the Fort-Senneville ruins — « un terrain en partie boisé d'environ 8000 mètres carrés », classed on 20 November 2003, privately owned and closed to visitors. The searchable protected-heritage section of the RPCQ returns four Senneville records, and three of them are commemorative plaques. What actually governs change on the estates is the village's own PIIA by-law 452, whose reach the Ville de Montréal describes in one sentence: it « reconnaît l'intérêt patrimonial de plusieurs des bâtiments d'intérêt du site ainsi que des murets de pierre ».

The village's own history page says things this page does not publish. Senneville's official chronology dates the stone windmill to 1686 (consistent with the cahier), the fort to 1703 (a year earlier than the RPCQ's 1702–1703 construction span), Arnold's burning of the fort to 1777 (the RPCQ says 1776), and records a "heritage declaration" of the Bois-de-la-Roche site in 2017. No instrument answering that description was found: the Ville de Montréal document is an énoncé de l'intérêt patrimonial dated 13 February 2018, which is a statement of value and not a designation, and no classement or citation exists for the site. The 2005 cahier is also internally inconsistent, dating « les vestiges du fort et du moulin de Senneville » to 1692 and 1700 in its phase summary while its own archaeology section gives 1686, 1691, 1700 and 1703. Where a municipal or promotional source and a statutory record disagree, this site follows the statutory record and says so.

Senneville is a rural-seigneurial place, and the sources insist on it. It sits in the same section of this site as the Île d'Orléans and Charlesbourg because its organising unit is the farm lot, not the street: the 2005 cahier describes even its sector of new houses, 2.E.7, as sited « sur un lotissement hérité du régime seigneurial », and the 2018 énoncé praises the parc agricole's « parcellaire d'origine, toujours lisible ». What is unusual is that the estates were laid over that parcellaire without erasing it. The lots stayed; the farms in several cases kept working, with the new owners leaving cultivation to farmers; and the picturesque grounds were composed around field boundaries, stone walls and orchards that were already there. That is why the barn-stable at 292 is designed by the Maxwells in Shingle Style and why the Bois-de-la-Roche heritage statement can list a seventeenth-century land division and an Arts and Crafts cold-store cellar as elements of one thing.
