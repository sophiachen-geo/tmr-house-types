Residential Architectural Styles of Lévis

Lévis is the place in this project with the best public catalogue of house types and the least legal protection for the houses in it. The City publishes twenty architectural courants, one page each, from the Régime français of 1620–1800 to Modernisme of 1950–1975, and every one of them is set out in the same seven fields — Volumétrie, Plan, Toiture, Revêtements, Ouvertures, Saillies, Ornementation — with a count of how many survive, a period, a line drawing, a strip of historic photographs, and a before-and-after pair showing a real Lévis house restored. It is searchable by street address. Nothing else in Québec except Gatineau's fiches comes close to it as a public instrument.

What sits underneath it is thinner. Lévis has no site patrimonial déclaré. Its two cited sites are both former village church cores on the outskirts. The historic core itself — Pointe-Lévy on the promontory, the carré Déziel, the bourgeois streets of Wolfe and Guenette and Fraser — has no status under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel at all, and is covered only by a discretionary municipal by-law. The City says so itself, in the plainest terms it could have chosen: « Il y a très peu de bâtiments qui sont visés par cette Loi sur le territoire lévisien. »

The catalogue's own numbers are the argument for reading it as a whole rather than type by type. Of the roughly four thousand heritage buildings the City counts, the vernaculaire américain alone accounts for about 1,700 — more than a third — and the four industrial currents of 1880 to 1945 together account for well over three thousand. The Régime français accounts for fifteen. This is a city whose heritage is overwhelmingly the plain wooden workers' house of the shipyard and railway decades, and whose catalogue is honest enough to say so.

The catalogue stops at 1975 and nothing in it describes what was built afterwards. In 2002 ten municipalities are merged into a single Ville de Lévis of three boroughs — Desjardins, Les Chutes-de-la-Chaudière-Est and Les Chutes-de-la-Chaudière-Ouest — which is why a catalogue of urban house types has to carry an agricultural vernacular as one of its twenty currents.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

1780 – 1861

John Caldwell, who inherits the seigneurie, lays a street grid on the top of the cliff and calls it Aubigny; the avenue Bégin was his Bergami, then his Eden Street. The transitional form of the decades either side of 1800, when Pointe-Lévy was a ferry landing and the shipyards at Lauzon were beginning. Where it comes from

At about seven hundred buildings this is the second most numerous current in the catalogue, and the one the City's own walking-tour brochure uses to explain how the Norman house of the colonists became something Québécois. The brochure reads the maison Lefrançois at 39 rue Wolfe as exactly that evolution — dormers cut into the roof and clapboard on the walls — and the maison Pampalon at 40 rue Wolfe as its later stage, where the ground floor lifts far enough to make the basement usable.

1975–2002 · Suburbanisation and the merger

1975 – 2002

The catalogue stops at 1975 and says nothing about what came after. In 2002 ten municipalities are merged into a single Ville de Lévis of three boroughs, which is why a catalogue of a river town's house types has to carry an agricultural vernacular among its twenty currents, and why thirteen separate village cores each need their own heritage-sector map sheet.

Notes on the approach

There is no declared site, and the two cited sites are not where you would expect them. Lévis has no site patrimonial déclaré at all. It has exactly two cited sites, and both of them are former village church cores on the periphery rather than the historic urban core: the Site du patrimoine de Saint-Nicolas, eight buildings of 1760–1887 strung along the old chemin du Roy west of the Saint-Nicolas village, and the Site du patrimoine de l'Église-et-du-Presbytère-de-Saint-Jean-Chrysostome, a church, presbytery, cemetery and square in what is now an inland borough. Vieux-Lévis itself — Pointe-Lévy, the carré Déziel, rue Wolfe, rue Guenette — has no cited or declared status. Neither does Vieux-Lauzon, and neither does Saint-Romuald. All three are covered only by the discretionary PIIA by-law, and the City warns readers not to confuse the two things: « Il est important de ne pas confondre le règlement municipal (PIIA - volet patrimonial) avec les dispositions prévues dans cette Loi car les implications ne sont pas les mêmes… Il y a très peu de bâtiments qui sont visés par cette Loi sur le territoire lévisien. » A 2005 request by the GIRAM to create heritage sites for « le Vieux-Lévis et le corridor de la rue Saint-Joseph à Lauzon » was not acted on.

The four-thousand figure is the City's, and it covers the whole merged territory. The catalogue's index reads « La Ville de Lévis regroupe plus de 4000 bâtiments patrimoniaux qui sont souvent associés à un style ou une typologie architecturale ». That is a count for the entire post-2002 city of ten former municipalities, not for any historic core, and it is not a statutory inventory figure. The per-courant « Nombre à Lévis » counts on the twenty fiches sum to roughly 4,050, which is consistent with it.

Twenty courants, counted on the crawl. The research memo behind this part said twenty-one. The crawl of 16 August 2026 harvested the fiche links from the index rather than guessing a range, and found exactly twenty distinct ?idfiche= links, numbered 1 to 20, each of which returned a page carrying all seven of the catalogue's field labels. Twenty is the number published here. The idfiche numbers do not follow the index order — Boomtown is fiche 10 and Vernaculaire américain is fiche 1 — so the ids have to be harvested, never iterated.

The statutory pre-1940 inventory: not established either way. Since the 2021 amendments to the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel, MRCs and equivalent territories have been required to adopt an inventory of their pre-1940 buildings, with a deadline of 1 April 2026. As of that deadline only 65 of the province's 106 MRCs and equivalent authorities had adopted one, and Lévis's own position was not established by the sources consulted here. This record therefore asserts nothing either way. What is documented is that Lévis has an Inventaire du patrimoine moderne (2016, 79 buildings of 1946–1975, RPCQ inventory 1313) and that RPCQ building records for Lévis cite an Inventaire des bâtiments patrimoniaux significatifs de Lévis, mise à jour 2023; whether either satisfies the statutory obligation is unverified. If a statutory pre-1940 inventory exists it would supersede the 4,000-building catalogue figure as the authoritative count.

The Saint-Nicolas citation date, resolved on the register. Two dates circulate for the citation of the Site du patrimoine de Saint-Nicolas — 5 November 1987 and 5 October 1987. The RPCQ statut block, consulted directly, reads Citation, Site patrimonial, Municipalité (Lévis), 1987-10-05, which is 5 October; it also lists two anterior statuses both dated 1987-07-06, « Avis de motion de citation » and « Citation ». So the register supports 5 October 1987, not 5 November, and adds a third date that neither version of the conflict mentioned. The 5 November figure is recorded here as unsupported rather than simply dropped.

Two records the catalogue files under « Autre type » get no card here, and one that nearly joined them does. The accessory-building fiche and Rationalisme industriel describe sheds, barns and factories, and are recorded and exported but not rendered. The Bâtiment mixte ou à vocation commerciale fiche is rendered, on reflection: all seven of its fields describe the commercial ground floor — vitrines, portes commerciales, corniche d'affichage, enseignes, auvents rétractables — and none describes the dwellings on the two or three storeys above, but that is how a mixed-use fiche is normally written. The shopfront is what distinguishes the type; the flats over it are what it shares with the rest of the street. The same form carries a card at Gatineau and at Trois-Rivières on this site, and the City's own walking-tour brochure says of the édifice Laurentien that it « jumelle aujourd'hui les vocations commerciale et résidentielle ». Its profile therefore carries the street level and is silent above it — the fiche's silence, not the building's.

Two corrections to the brief. It marked Beaux-arts as conditional, to be encoded only if the fiche shows houses; it does — a two-storey dwelling with a covered veranda, quoins and a modillion cornice, with examples at 150 Wolfe, 108 du Passage, 69 Saint-Louis and 55 Bégin — so it is encoded as a house. And the brief gave the maison Alphonse-Desjardins at 6 rue du Mont-Marie; the RPCQ record for the classed building gives 8 rue du Mont-Marie, and that is the address used.
