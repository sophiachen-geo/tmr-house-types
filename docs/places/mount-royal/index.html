Residential Architectural Styles of Town of Mount Royal

Town of Mount Royal is the only place in Québec built in its entirety to a single garden-city plan, drawn by Frederick Gage Todd in 1912 for the Canadian Northern Railway to pay for the tunnel under the mountain. It filled in over three periods, and each period left its own family of houses. The town's design-review by-law (PIIA By-law No. 1449, 2018) names them, photographs them and lists what makes each one itself. This page is that catalogue, in English.

The Town publishes By-law 1449 in French only. Photos below are the by-law's own; the “What the by-law says” lists are direct translations of its tables; the short descriptions are ours.

Phase 1 · Garden City and Montreal vernacular

1915 – 1935

The first houses rose near the station and along the diagonal boulevards while the tunnel was still being bored (1910–1918); the town's first mayor, Thomas S. Darling, was the railway land company's man on the ground. Two lineages meet in this phase, one imported and one local.

Phase 1 · 1915 – 1935 · By-law art. 19

Garden City House Maison cité-jardin

Style: Arts and Crafts / English Domestic Revival

The founding house type of TMR. When Frederick Todd laid out the town in 1912 he was borrowing directly from Ebenezer Howard's Garden City movement, and the first houses near the station borrowed the movement's architecture too: the English Arts-and-Crafts cottage of Letchworth and Hampstead Garden Suburb. Look for steep 45° gables, a brick ground floor with stucco or half-timbering above, small-paned windows, and a deliberately picturesque, asymmetrical silhouette that reads as a cottage in a garden rather than a house on a street.

Where it comes from

The Garden City movement's architecture as well as its planning. Ebenezer Howard's 1898 tract produced Letchworth (Parker & Unwin, 1903) and Hampstead Garden Suburb (1907), and their house vocabulary, itself drawn from Voysey, Baillie Scott and the English cottage, travelled with the idea: steep gables, roughcast or half-timbered upper storeys over brick, small-paned casements, picturesque asymmetry, the house read as a cottage in a garden. The by-law's Garden City House is this, with the 45° roof, stucco-and-timber upper floor and small brick module written out as criteria.

Faubourg House Maison faubourienne

Style: Montreal faubourg vernacular (with Foursquare crossings)

“Faubourien” refers to the vernacular of Montreal's old inner suburbs (the faubourgs): a plain two-storey brick box, flat or hipped roof, sometimes a false mansard, and a front verandah on wooden columns. In TMR the type is what the Plateau or Rosemont row house becomes when it is given its own lot, a side driveway and a lawn. Its virtue is restraint: symmetry, aligned windows, one material.

Where it comes from

Faubourien points to the maison de faubourg, the plain brick house of Québec's nineteenth-century inner suburbs: two or three storeys, flat or false-mansard roof, aligned openings, minimal ornament, a front gallery on wooden posts. Montreal's Plateau PIIA uses the same term for its own housing stock. In TMR the type is what that urban house becomes on a detached or semi-detached lot with a side driveway; several examples take on the cubic mass and hipped roof of the American Foursquare, which was being built across Canada in the same years.

Faubourg Apartment Block Faubourg (collectif)

Style: Interwar walk-up, with Art Deco and stripped-classical trim

The interwar Montreal walk-up, three storeys of red brick under a flat roof, with a decorated parapet, a stone base, patterned brickwork and a prominent entrance portico. Balconies with iron or stone railings give the façade its rhythm. These sit on the town's few multi-family lots and are the collective sibling of the faubourg house.

Where it comes from

Three-storey brick apartment houses of the 1920s and 30s, Montreal's standard multi-family building of the period. This is where Art Deco actually lives in TMR: stepped and decorated parapets, geometric brick panels, artificial-stone entrance surrounds, iron balcony rails with angular motifs. Héritage Montréal describes the same repertoire on 1920s Montreal plexes (dark brick, artificial-stone insertions around doors and windows, appliqués with geometric forms). The by-law absorbs it as "distinctive brick motifs and bonds" and "a body crowned by a brick motif and a parapet" rather than naming the style.

Phase 2 · The period revivals

1935 – 1955

Building slowed in the Depression and resumed in the late 1930s and after the war, when the historical revivals then dominant across anglophone North America set the tone. Lots widened to 15–22 m and the garage attached itself to the house. Four revivals and one apartment idiom account for nearly everything.

Phase 2 · 1935 – 1955 · By-law art. 22

Canadian House Maison canadienne

Style: Québec Revival (maison canadienne, néo-québécois)

A 1930s–50s revival of the 18th-century Quebec farmhouse: a steep roof whose eaves flare out into “larmiers” that shelter the front door, a row of dormers, fieldstone walls, and a calm symmetrical front. It was the domestic face of a broader interwar interest in Québécois heritage. The distinctive move is the roof, which is asked to express the base of the house through its overhang.

Where it comes from

A regionalist revival of the eighteenth-century Laurentian farmhouse: bell-cast roof whose eaves flare into larmiers sheltering the door, a row of dormers, fieldstone walls, a calm symmetrical front. The Canadian Encyclopedia locates its source in the anglophone rediscovery of pre-industrial Québec during the 1920s–30s, stimulated by Clarence Gagnon's paintings and encouraged by Percy Nobbs and Ramsay Traquair at McGill, whose surveys of old Québec buildings fed a fashion for fieldstone and pitched roofs among the province's leading domestic architects. TMR's Canadian houses are the suburban, builder-scale version of that fashion.

New England House Maison Nouvelle-Angleterre

Style: Colonial Revival — New England / Cape Cod branch

Colonial Revival as it arrived from the American Northeast: a long, simple gabled rectangle with its broad side to the street, symmetrical windows, dormers set halfway up the roof, and a discreet canopy over the front door. Brick or stone below, clapboard or stucco above. It is quieter than the Georgian house, and the by-law wants it kept quiet: bay windows are the only projections allowed.

Where it comes from

A long side-gabled rectangle with its broad face to the street, symmetrical openings, dormers half-way up the roof, a discreet door canopy; brick or stone below and clapboard or stucco above. It is the New England colonial house as codified in 1920s–40s pattern books and builders' plan services, the quieter cousin of Georgian Revival. The by-law keeps it quiet: bay windows are the only projections it allows.

New England Apartment Block Nouvelle-Angleterre (collectif)

Style: Collegiate Gothic / Tudor apartment court

Low-rise red-brick apartment courts arranged in U or L plans around a green forecourt, with steep gables, dormers and the occasional turret. A stone base and stone string courses tie them to the collegiate-Tudor mood of the interwar decades. Entrances are marked by a distinct architectural element rather than a big portico.

Where it comes from

Low-rise red-brick apartment blocks in U or L plans around a green forecourt, with stone bases, stone string courses, steep gables, dormers and the occasional turret. The idiom is the residential cousin of interwar school and college architecture, and of the "garden apartment" courts built across North America in the 1930s–40s. The by-law's "New England (collective)" label is looser than the architecture: these blocks are more Tudor than New England.

Georgian House Maison géorgienne

Style: Georgian Revival

Georgian Revival: the most formal of TMR's houses. A red-brick box of two full storeys, three or five bays wide, with a central door flanked by pilasters and topped by an elaborate crown, sash windows with small panes, and a frieze under a moderately pitched hip or side-gabled roof. Symmetry is the whole point; the by-law lets you emphasise the central bay with a slight projection and a pediment, and nothing more.

Where it comes from

The formal wing of Colonial Revival. Two full storeys of red brick, three or five bays, a central door with pilasters and an entablature or pediment, sash windows with small panes, a frieze under a moderately pitched hip or side-gable roof. Symmetry is the whole point; the by-law permits one flourish, a slightly projected central bay under a pediment, and nothing else. In Canada the style carried an Imperial-loyalist inflection that made it a natural fit for a town founded and first settled by anglophones.

English Manor House Maison manoir anglais

Style: Tudor Revival

Tudor Revival, TMR's most romantic type: steep 45° roofs broken into several gables, a natural-stone ground floor with brick or half-timbered stucco above, patterned brickwork (herringbone, Dutch bond), small-paned casements, sometimes a round turret at the entrance. Where the Georgian house is symmetrical, the manor is deliberately picturesque, and its garage is meant to hide.

Where it comes from

The by-law's "English manor" is Tudor Revival by any other name: stone ground floor with brick or stucco-and-timber above, patterned brick (herringbone, Dutch bond), 45° multi-gabled roofs, small-paned casements, sometimes a round entrance turret. In North America the style peaked in the 1920s–30s and lingered into the 1940s; its Montreal fashion was strongest in the anglophone west end and suburbs. Tudor detailing also surfaces on some Phase 1 Garden City houses, which is why the two types share the half-timbered gable.

Phase 3 · Postwar suburban modern

1955 – 1975

The widest lots (20–25 m) were the last to fill, and they filled under the car. Roofs flatten to 30° or less, windows lose their muntins and turn horizontal, the garage moves into the façade. Héritage Montréal lists Mount Royal among the Montreal-area districts where the postwar house is common, and notes that some such ensembles now carry real heritage value. Four house types and one apartment type carry the period.

Phase 3 · 1955 – 1975 · By-law art. 27

Bungalow Maison bungalow

Style: Ranch / postwar bungalow

The postwar ranch bungalow: one storey, long and low, with a shallow-pitched roof whose overhang shelters a recessed door, a garage pushed forward into the façade, and big undivided windows wider than they are tall. Buff (“chamois”) brick or stone, black shingles, a wide driveway. The type that arrived with the car and the last empty lots of the 1950s and 60s.

Where it comes from

One storey, long and low, a shallow-pitched roof whose overhang shelters a recessed door, a garage pushed forward into the façade, big undivided windows wider than tall, buff ("chamois") brick or stone, a wide driveway. In Québec usage bungalow simply means the postwar single-family suburban house; the form is the Ranch house that spread from the American Southwest after 1945 and became the default Canadian suburban dwelling of the 1950s–60s.

Cottage Maison cottage

Style: Postwar two-storey (Québec cottage), with Minimal Traditional and Moderne notes

In Quebec usage a “cottage” is simply a two-storey detached house, and this is its postwar version: a plain box under a low hip roof, stone or brick on the ground floor with stucco or siding above, garage built into the front, and a flat marquise over the door. A visible line at the ground-floor lintels or the upper sills divides base from body.

Where it comes from

In Québec real-estate usage a cottage is a two-storey detached house, as opposed to the one-storey bungalow; the OQLF notes the word's British origin and its earlier sense of an elegant, English-inspired country house. TMR's postwar cottage is a plain box: stone or brick base, stucco or siding above, low hip roof, integrated garage, flat marquise over the door, a horizontal line at the ground-floor lintels or upper sills dividing base from body. Some examples keep vestigial colonial detailing (Minimal Traditional); others, in cut stone with flat roofs and rounded or banded corners, are the town's clearest echo of Streamline Moderne.

Split-Level Maison demi-niveau

Style: Split-level

Two volumes offset by half a storey: a one-storey wing beside a one-and-a-half-storey wing, with the garage tucked under the bedrooms and pushed forward, and a recessed entrance under the eaves. Salmon, buff or brown stone with wood or metal infill panels; both roofs share one low-pitched material. The interior stair does the work the façade shows.

Where it comes from

A plan type more than a style, dominant across North American suburbs from the mid-1950s: two volumes offset by half a storey, the garage under the bedrooms and pushed forward, a recessed entrance under the eaves. TMR's are dressed in Ranch and Contemporary materials, salmon, buff or brown stone with wood or metal infill panels, under one shared low-pitched roof.

Prairie House Maison Prairie

Style: Prairie / Wrightian Contemporary

Frank Lloyd Wright's Prairie idiom, filtered through 1960s Montreal: relentless horizontality, low pyramidal or multi-hipped roofs with deep soffits, upper windows tucked right under the eaves and running around the corners as ribbons, long “Roman” brick or long-cut stone, and one-storey wings (garage, porch, solarium) that stretch the composition sideways. Asymmetrical, and the most architect-driven of the phase-3 types.

Where it comes from

Frank Lloyd Wright's Prairie idiom of 1900–1915, revived and simplified in the 1950s–60s Contemporary house: relentless horizontality, low pyramidal or multi-hipped roofs with deep soffits, upper windows tucked under the eaves and wrapping the corners as ribbons, long "Roman" brick or long-cut stone, one-storey wings that stretch the composition sideways. Asymmetrical and the most architect-driven of the Phase 3 types; the by-law's insistence on the Roman brick module is the tell.

Modern Apartment Block Moderne (collectif)

Style: International Style / Modernist apartment block

The 1950s–70s modernist apartment building: flat roof finished with a horizontal coping band, a set-back penthouse floor on top, balconies grouped into recessed loggias, wide undivided windows and a marquise over the door. Sand or red brick, often several brick colours on one façade. Parking goes underground; the front yard stays almost entirely green.

Where it comes from

Flat-roofed brick slabs of the 1950s–70s with a horizontal coping band, a set-back penthouse floor, loggia balconies and wide undivided windows, in sand or red brick and often several brick colours on one façade. The town's share of the postwar Modernism that lines Côte-des-Neiges and Sherbrooke; the earliest examples, with brick-and-stone banding, still carry a Streamline accent.

Notes on the approach

By-law 1449 sorts the town's houses into thirteen types, named for period, lot and massing rather than for the movements that produced them. This section restores the conventional style names and says where each came from. The frame is the town's own history: a speculative railway suburb laid out in 1912 by landscape architect Frederick Gage Todd for the Canadian Northern Railway, whose lot sales were meant to pay for the tunnel under the mountain; a plan mixing Garden City, City Beautiful and urban-park ideas that Parks Canada recognised in 2008 as a National Historic Site; and a build-out over roughly six decades that tracked, phase by phase, the shift in North American suburban taste from English cottage to historical revival to postwar modern.

Two styles that visitors expect to find by name, Art Deco and Streamline Moderne, are present in TMR mainly as ornament on Phase 1 walk-ups and as a minority of Phase 3 cottages and early apartment blocks. Neither dominates a zone, so neither receives a table in the by-law; such buildings are reviewed under the general criteria of Chapter III instead. Tudor Revival is fully present, but named by its massing ("English manor") rather than its style. The by-law's own count has also grown: the version in force through the 2000s recognised eight house styles across the same three phases; the 2018 by-law splits out the apartment types and adds Georgian and Prairie, for thirteen.

By-law 1449 is a discretionary review instrument. Québec's Act respecting land use planning and development has allowed municipalities to adopt PIIA by-laws since 1989 (sections 145.15 to 145.20.1); the provincial planning guide is explicit that a PIIA does not judge whether a project is warranted, only its appearance and its integration into its setting, and that approval adds a condition to the permit without changing zoning. Council decides by resolution on the recommendation of the Planning Advisory Committee. Because approvals and refusals must be reasoned and can be litigated (TMR's PIIA survived a Court of Appeal challenge over a Kindersley Avenue addition in the early 2000s), the by-law describes buildings in observable, measurable terms: setback, lot width, roof pitch, window proportion, brick module. "Not Tudor enough" is not a finding; "roof pitch under 45° and no small-paned glazing" is.

The thirteen categories follow the typo-morphological method used across Québec heritage characterisation studies and municipal PIIAs: period of formation crossed with lot, volume, roof, openings and material. Montreal's Plateau by-law and Québec City's built-heritage thesaurus classify their housing the same way, and use some of the same words. Chapter II of By-law 1449 is that kind of inventory, condensed. Each type is assigned to zones through the zoning grid and mapped in Annex A, which means only house forms frequent enough to dominate a zone become named types. Frequency, not significance, is the filter. That is why Tudor Revival appears under the name of its massing, why Art Deco is absorbed into the Faubourg apartment block, and why single Deco, Moderne or hybrid houses have no table of their own.

The by-law is candid that its tables are references, not templates. Article 18 states that the PIIA "does not aim to restrict creativity and originality by imposing solutions copied from the architecture of the past, but rather to support the development of an architecture adapted to today's needs, in harmony with the specific character that stands as heritage value." In practice a house that fits no type is judged against the dominant type of its zone plus the general criteria of Chapter III: composition, subordination and legibility of additions, preservation of original components, materials, landscape. That is the approach's weakness as well as its strength. It is consistent and defensible, and a minority house is always read through the majority's lens.

The style names on this page are ours, added so the town's houses can be read in the vocabulary of architectural history as well as in the by-law's own. Where the two disagree, the by-law's tables govern what the Town will approve.

A note on the by-law's intent. Its own words, translated: the PIIA “does not aim to restrict creativity and originality by imposing solutions copied from the architecture of the past, but rather to support the development of an architecture adapted to today's needs, in harmony with the specific character that stands as heritage value.” The characteristics above are guidance the Planning Advisory Committee weighs; the by-law's evaluation criteria (siting, massing, composition, materials, details, landscaping) apply on top of them.

Vocabulary. Articulation translates découpage, the by-law's word for how the façade is broken into base, body and crown. Larmier is the flared drip edge of a Québec roof. Chamois is buff-coloured brick. A cottage, in Québec French, is any two-storey detached house.
