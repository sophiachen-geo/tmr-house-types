Residential Architectural Styles of Vieux-Terrebonne

The site is a piece of river geography before it is a piece of town. The rivière des Mille-Îles reaches the end of an archipelago here and drops over a break of slope into rapids, and that fall of water is why there is a bourg at all. In 1720 the seigneur Louis Lepage de Sainte-Claire (1690–1762) put a grist mill and a saw mill on the island in the middle of it — flour for the censitaires and the region's grain merchants, timber for shipbuilding — and at the same time began conceding urban lots on the north bank. The mills and the street grid are the same act.

Three owners in succession rebuilt the works and each left the town a layer. Jacob Jordan (1741–1796), an English merchant, took the seigneury in 1784 and moved it from grain into the fur trade. Simon McTavish (c. 1750–1804), the driving force of the North West Company, bought it in 1802 meaning to make Terrebonne a production and distribution centre for the western trade: he threw a dyke across to the island to raise the head of water and built a bakery, and the fur merchants who followed him built their houses in the bourg. Joseph Masson (1791–1847) and Marie-Geneviève-Sophie Raymond (1798–1882) bought the seigneury in 1832 and rebuilt and enlarged the mill complex again, and the French-speaking business élite settled in behind them.

What the declaration reads off the ground is the social geography those three centuries produced, still legible as four distinct pieces. Below the coteau, in the Bas-de-la-Côte, an orthogonal grid of narrow streets runs down to the water, packed with small vernacular houses in wood and brick under tin roofs, set almost on the street line. On the crest, rue Saint-Louis winds along with the merchants' houses strung out on ample lots behind traditional fences and mature trees — the lots the RPCQ says were "divisés en lots plus amples recherchés par les propriétaires fonciers aisés" from McTavish's day. Between them, at the meeting of boulevard des Braves and rue Saint-Louis, the parish core moved up the hill around 1850 to sit beside the rebuilt manor: church of 1877–1879, convent of the Congrégation de Notre-Dame of 1882, with an Anglican parish at Saint-Michael for the anglophone population. And on the island, production — stone, sober, industrial.

Then the town nearly lost it. A fire ravaged part of Terrebonne in 1922 and took most of the houses between rues Sainte-Marie and Chapleau south of rue Saint-Pierre. The depression of the 1930s checked growth. In 1965 the autoroute 25 replaced rue Chapleau and the pont Sophie-Masson as the main link to Montréal, and in 1967 the boulevard des Seigneurs became the main east–west axis and pulled the merchants off rue Saint-Pierre. The Vieux-Terrebonne was, in the fiche's word, dévitalisé. What saved it was a mobilisation in the 1970s — classements, restorations, public investment, and infill housing such as the Terrebourg ensemble deliberately fitted to the old fabric — after which, the RPCQ says, it "en vient à être considéré comme une réussite en manière de réhabilitation urbaine". On 20 May 2026 it became Québec's fourteenth declared site patrimonial.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

1720–1784 · Lepage de Sainte-Claire — the mills and the first bourg

1720 – 1784

Louis Lepage de Sainte-Claire becomes seigneur and from 1720 builds the infrastructure that makes the place: a grist mill and a saw mill forming one complex on the island, a church, and the concession of urban lots. The seigneury had been granted to André Daulier Deslandes (1621–1715) back in 1673, but successive seigneurs neglected their duties and nothing came of it until Lepage. By 1781 the village counts more than sixty dwellings. Jacques Perras (1725–1786) takes a concession in 1759 and builds shortly after — the house that survives as the maison Bélisle, and the only substantial witness to this phase still standing.

1720–1784 · 1720 – 1784 · RPCQ

Stone house in the French tradition La maison de tradition française en pierre

UP-1
Phase attribution: verified

One building carries this type, and it is a reconstruction around old walls. The maison Bélisle of about 1759 at 844 rue Saint-François-Xavier is a low roughcast-stone carré of one and a half storeys, barely raised off the ground, under a steep two-slope roof with short larmiers, no dormers and cedar shingle, with casement windows of small panes and cut-stone chimney stacks staggered in the gable walls. The RPCQ calls what remains of it "un rare témoin du noyau villageois du milieu du XVIIIe siècle".

Where it comes from

Jacques Perras (1725–1786), écrivain du munitionnaire du Roy, took a land concession in 1759 and had the house built shortly afterwards, when Lepage's bourg was some four decades old; by 1781 the village counted more than sixty dwellings. The RPCQ reads the house as an example of "la maison rurale québécoise d'inspiration française" — the French model progressively adapted by local know-how to the climate and the available materials. It was considerably altered between 1830 and 1850, when coyaux were added at the ends of the rafters and the roof took on the flared larmiers it now shows, and an angled corner door was cut at the turn of the twentieth century. The Bélisle family bought it in 1916 and held it for much of the century. It escaped the 1922 fire that destroyed most of the houses between rues Sainte-Marie and Chapleau south of rue Saint-Pierre, but it burned in 1999 — only the masonry could be kept, and the building was restored in 2002–2003 to return it to its eighteenth-century appearance. What stands today is therefore old walls with new everything else, and the record should be read that way. It is classed as an immeuble patrimonial (30 October 1973) with an aire de protection of the same date, and the protection covers the exterior and the interior but not the land.

1784–1832 · Jordan and McTavish — grain, fur and the digue

1784 – 1832

In 1784 the seigneury passes to Jacob Jordan (1741–1796), an English merchant who goes into the grain trade and progressively draws the bourg into the fur trade. In 1802 Simon McTavish (c. 1750–1804), principal partner of the North West Company, becomes owner, builds the dyke to raise the mills' hydraulic power and adds a bakery. The fur trade brings merchants who settle in the town. In this period the bourg runs from the riverbank west to rue Saint-Norbert — now rue Chapleau — in the east; the rue de l'Attrape, now boulevard des Braves, leads to a public square beside the church; and the margins of rue Saint-Louis are cut into the larger lots the well-off want. The bourgeois migration up to rue Saint-Louis begins after the second manor is built at the end of the eighteenth century, and the stone and wood houses that line it go up through the first third of the nineteenth century — the maison Roussil among them.

1832–1900 · The Masson seigneury and the bourgeois crest

1832 – 1900

Joseph Masson (1791–1847) and Marie-Geneviève-Sophie Raymond (1798–1882) buy the seigneury in 1832 and invest in rebuilding and enlarging the mill complex; members of the francophone élite move into the bourg behind them. The parish core is moved up the coteau next to the manor rebuilt around 1850, the new church going up east of it in 1877–1879 and the convent of the Congrégation de Notre-Dame in 1882, while an Anglican parish is erected to serve the anglophone population at Saint-Michael. In 1860 the bourg obtains the status of Ville, a measure of its regional weight, and growth continues steadily if more slowly, still attracting new firms such as that of Matthew Moody (c. 1810–1887). This is the phase that fills both the Bas-de-la-Côte with vernacular houses and the crest with the ornamented maisons cossues.

Maison cossue of rue Saint-Louis La maison cossue de la rue Saint-Louis

UP-4
Phase attribution: provisional

The merchants' house of the crest — stone or brick, sometimes wood, under a tin roof, standing well back from rue Saint-Louis in a fenced and planted front yard with its old outbuildings beside it, and ornamented, the declaration says, elaborately. It is the only one of the four types here whose record rests on the sector paragraph alone, with no classed building behind it, which is why its styles are provisional and its massing and openings rows are empty.

Where it comes from

A migration, and a datable one. The RPCQ's fiche for the maison Roussil explains that the building of the second manor at the site of today's parc Masson at the end of the eighteenth century "entraîne une migration de la bourgeoisie vers la rue Saint-Louis. Plusieurs résidences cossues en pierre ou en bois sont alors érigées le long de cette artère." The fur merchants McTavish attracted after 1802 began it; the francophone business élite that followed Joseph Masson's purchase of the seigneury in 1832 continued it; and the street kept filling through the nineteenth century, which is why the surviving houses on it run from the maison Joseph-Augé of 1798–1801 through the maison Roussil of about 1830 to the maison Hercule-Masson of about 1880–1900, the maison Louis-de-Gonzague-Masson of about 1896 and the maison Raymond-Masson of about 1910–1935. Rue Saint-Louis carries 44 of the site's 225 associated RPCQ records, more than any other street.

1832–1900 · 1832 – 1900 · RPCQ

Neoclassical Québécois stone house La maison québécoise d'inspiration néoclassique

UP-4
Phase attribution: provisional

The maison Roussil of about 1830 on rue Saint-Louis, classed in 1972 and given an aire de protection in 1975, is the anchor — a rectangular stone house of two and a half storeys under two slightly flared slopes, its front elevation laid in rubble dressed to look like cut stone, symmetrical and regularly fenestrated, with carved chambranles, quoins, a cornice on modillons, and wide two-headed cut-stone chimney stacks joined by a low wall at each end of the roof.

Where it comes from

Built by and for Noël-Théodore Roussil (c. 1798–1890), master joiner and militia captain, as both dwelling and craftsman's workshop — a door cut in the west elevation once gave onto the shop. The RPCQ reads the house as representative of the influence of neoclassicism on Québec residential architecture in the nineteenth century, expressed in the symmetry of the main façade, the regular ordering of the openings, the medium-pitched roof and the ornamental vocabulary of carved casings, quoins and modillon cornice; Roussil gave every interior bay a different treatment, which the fiche says is what gives the house its originality. As master joiner he is said to have taken contracts for work on the parish church and on the seigneurial office; as militia captain he sided with the loyalists in the rebellions of 1837–1838, and some authors hold that patriotes were held in the house awaiting transfer to the prison du Pied-du-Courant in Montréal. A second example stands a few doors away — the maison Alexandre et Joseph Roussil, built in stone about 1830 by Noël-Théodore's uncles "tout près de celle de leur neveu", two and a half storeys under a tôle à baguettes roof, cited by the Ville on 13 September 2021.

Small-gabarit vernacular village house of the Bas-de-la-Côte La maison vernaculaire villageoise du Bas-de-la-Côte

UP-1

The ordinary house of the Bas-de-la-Côte, and by count the commonest thing in the declared site — small in height and width, wood or brick under a tin roof, set almost on the street line so that the row of them makes a continuous urban wall, with wooden galleries and balconies over the footway and an angled door where a house turns a corner. The declaration names no storey count, roof form or window type for it, so neither does this record.

Where it comes from

The bourg's fabric rather than any one architect's product. Louis Lepage de Sainte-Claire began conceding urban lots from 1720, and by 1781 the village counted more than sixty dwellings; the grid of narrow streets dropping from rue Saint-Louis to the rivière des Mille-Îles was laid out before 1850 and filled with modest houses for the artisans, tradespeople and workers who served the mills and, later, the grain and fur trades. The RPCQ's valeur architecturale calls the result "un ensemble de résidences villageoises de petit gabarit et d'architecture vernaculaire". A fire ravaged part of Terrebonne in 1922, destroying most of the houses between rues Sainte-Marie and Chapleau south of rue Saint-Pierre, so what stands in that stretch is in part post-1922 rebuilding on the old lot pattern.

1900–1965 · Town, fire and slow growth

1900 – 1965

A fire ravages part of Terrebonne in 1922, destroying most of the dwellings between rues Sainte-Marie and Chapleau south of rue Saint-Pierre; the maison Bélisle escapes it. Over the following decade the economic crisis weighs heavily on growth, and at the same time larger manufacturing centres emerge elsewhere in Québec. The town keeps its shape rather than adding to it, which is much of the reason the pre-1900 fabric survived to be declared.

1965–2026 · Dévitalisation, mobilisation, declaration

1965 – 2026

The autoroute 25, opened in 1965, replaces rue Chapleau and the pont Sophie-Masson as the main link to Montréal; the boulevard des Seigneurs, opened in 1967 and better placed at the centre of the municipality, becomes the main east–west axis and draws away the merchants who had had their shopfronts on rue Saint-Pierre. The Vieux-Terrebonne is abandoned and dévitalisé. In the 1970s a mobilisation forms against the loss; the Ville and the ministère des Affaires culturelles act; classements, restorations and investment halt the decline, and well-integrated housing projects such as the Terrebourg ensemble are built into the old urban fabric. The quarter recovers, and comes to be regarded as a success in urban rehabilitation. The minister initiates the file on 12 November 2020, recommends declaration on 20 June 2025, the CPCQ holds its public hearing on 10 September 2025, and the government declares the site on 20 May 2026.

Notes on the approach

What the declaration lists beyond the four sectors. The éléments caractéristiques are not exhausted by the unités de paysage. They open with the setting — "sa situation sur la rive nord de la rivière des Mille-Îles, à la fin d'un archipel, dans un secteur où la rivière présente une rupture de pente produisant des rapides" — and with the site's extent, "sa superficie d'environ 25 hectares". They name a linked system of parks and green spaces, some of them old: the parc Masson "avec ses gloriettes et sa percée visuelle centrale"; the parc de l'école Saint-Sacrement with its mature trees on lawn and its traditional wooden fence along rue Saint-Louis; the parc du Souvenir/Jardins de Vitré with its winding path along the river; and the parc de l'île des Moulins with its mature trees and open ground. They list the visual qualities: the view of the église de Saint-Louis-de-France from rue Sainte-Marie and from the island, the visual relationship between the manoir Masson and the island, the built landmarks — the hôtel de ville tower and the spires of Saint-Louis-de-France and Saint-Michael — and "les séquences bâties homogènes, notamment celle au sud de la rue Saint-Joseph avec son bâti en maçonnerie de briques". They cover the archaeology, which is registered under nine Borden codes: BkFj-1, BkFj-4, BkFj-9, BkFj-10, BkFj-11, BkFj-12, BkFj-13, BkFj-14 and BkFj-28, the last marked "en réservation". And they close on the spaces of sociability — the island itself, the place du Marché at the corner of rues Saint-Pierre and Sainte-Marie, the parc du Souvenir/Jardins de Vitré, the parc Masson and rue Saint-Pierre.

The effect date is earlier than the declaration, and that is not an anomaly. The statut block reads "Déclaration, Site patrimonial, Ministre de la Culture et des Communications, 2026-05-20, Prise d'effet : 2025-07-02", above two prior statuses, "Recommandation ministérielle de déclaration, 2025-06-20" and "Dossier initié par le ministre, 2020-11-12". Under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel the protection of a site under study runs from the ministerial notice, not from the decree that concludes the process, so the effect date properly precedes the declaration by some ten months. All four dates are encoded on the place record.

Three numbers, three different things. The RPCQ's description says the site "comprend environ 210 édifices construits à partir du XVIIIe siècle", and its éléments caractéristiques say "ses quelque 210 bâtiments résidentiels, institutionnels, industriels et commerciaux" — that is the count of buildings on the ground, and it is the figure this site uses. The fiche's Éléments associés header says "Patrimoine immobilier associé (225)" — that is a count of RPCQ records falling inside the perimeter, which is a different thing: it includes parks, an étang, a street, a set of gloriettes, a retaining wall, and six components of the Île-des-Moulins ensemble that are each catalogued separately. The CPCQ consultation page and the press of 2025 said "environ 200 bâtiments" — the pre-declaration rounding, published before the fiche was finalised. None of the three is wrong; they measure buildings, records, and an earlier estimate of buildings.

The fiche contradicts itself on McTavish, and 1804 is the right date. The valeur patrimoniale calls him "Simon McTavish (vers 1750-1844)"; the informations historiques, three screens further down the same page, call him "Simon McTavish (vers 1750-1804)". The second is correct — McTavish died at Montréal in 1804, which is also why his purchase of the seigneury in 1802 is described as the last of his ventures. The historique is quoted throughout this page and the valeur's date is not used.

The Île-des-Moulins is a provincial classement, not a national historic site. The ensemble is registered as the Ensemble d'immeubles patrimoniaux de l'Île-des-Moulins, RPCQ id 92594, classed on 12 December 1973 by the Québec minister. It is not a Parks Canada national historic site, and the research memo behind this page was wrong to describe it as one. The distinction is not pedantry: the classement is what makes work on the island subject to ministerial authorisation under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel, and a federal designation would carry no such power over a provincially and municipally owned site.

The type records are thin because the source characterises sectors, not types. The declaration's éléments caractéristiques are organised by unité de paysage; there is no per-type table anywhere in it, and no fiche for "the vernacular village house" of the kind Gatineau or Lévis publishes. The four records below were built from what the sector text says plus what the individual classed fiches say about named buildings, and where the source gives no storey count, roof form or window type, the column is left empty rather than filled from a comparable place. Two of the four rest on a single classed building each; the maison cossue rests on the sector paragraph alone, and its styles are provisional until the Bergeron Gagnon inventory can be read. The per-building evidence exists — some 600 pre-1946 buildings and 7,000 photographs in that inventory, and the 225 associated RPCQ records tabulated above — and belongs to Part 8b.

What could not be verified. Every provincial source on this page was fetched and read: the site fiche, all 225 associated records, and the classed-building fiches. The municipal ones were not. terrebonne.ca answered every request with an HTTP 403 Cloudflare interstitial and the Internet Archive was offline during the crawl, so the Règlement 810 page, the Vieux-Terrebonne heritage page and the inventory page are cited from the brief rather than from the documents. One municipal fact did survive independently: 28 of the 225 associated records carry a Citation by the Municipalité (Terrebonne) dated 2021-09-13, which confirms the by-law's adoption date from the provincial side. The 2024 MRC list was downloaded directly and is real: 609 numbered fiches, 104 of them struck out as "Retirée".
