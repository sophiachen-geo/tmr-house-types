Residential Architectural Styles of Témiscaming

Témiscaming is a Garden City company town on a hillside above Lake Timiskaming, planned in 1917 by the Scottish planner Thomas Adams for Riordon Pulp and Paper. The Canadian Centre for Architecture describes the result exactly: "The Adams plan adapted its roads to the land, setting the town on a hill by Lake Timiskaming and the pulp mill further down on more level ground." What followed was built by two firms in two periods. From 1918 to 1923 the Montréal architects Ross and Macdonald — then Canada's largest practice — built the mill, the commercial district and all the houses of the lower town; from 1925 to 1950 William Lyon Somerville of Toronto designed the upper-district houses, the hospital, and the town's schools and churches for Canadian International Paper, which had bought mill and town in 1925. "A single design aesthetic, established when the town was created, prevailed throughout its development." The town describes itself by its "Anglo-Saxon architecture, the flower gardens and the works of art of European origin" on a mountain slope. CIP gave way in 1972 to Tembec, formed when employees bought the mill to prevent its closure, and the plant passed later to RYAM. No residential district in Témiscaming has heritage status.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

1917–1923 · Adams plan and the Ross and Macdonald lower town

1917 – 1923

Adams draws the plan in 1917; the mill and the first houses go up from 1918; the municipality is created in 1920 as Kipawa and renamed Témiscaming in 1921. Ross and Macdonald build the whole lower town for Riordon, mill and commercial district included, by 1923.

The houses Ross and Macdonald built in series for Riordon between 1918 and 1923, together with the mill and the commercial district: the whole lower town by one Montréal firm, to one aesthetic.

Where it comes from

Thomas Adams laid the town on the hill and the mill below it in 1917; Riordon commissioned Ross and Macdonald, then Canada's largest architectural practice, to build everything downhill. CIP bought mill and town in 1925.

1925–1950 · CIP and the Somerville upper district

1925 – 1950

Canadian International Paper buys mill and town in 1925 and retains William Lyon Somerville, who designs the upper district's houses and the town's institutions — hospital, schools, churches — through to 1950. The railway station of 1927, built to CPR engineers' plans, is the one building in town with provincial protection.

1950–1972 · Late company period

1950 – 1972

The late company period: the town continues under CIP ownership, its single design aesthetic intact, until the mill's future comes into question at the end of the 1960s.

1972–2020 · Tembec — the town without a company

1972 – 2020

In 1972 employees buy the mill and form Tembec, and the town ceases to be company-owned. It is the phase in which Témiscaming becomes an ordinary municipality with a planned town's fabric.

Notes on the approach

The regional inventory came back empty for Témiscaming. The MRC de Témiscamingue published its inventaire du patrimoine bâti on 8 April 2026, a week after the statutory deadline of 1 April. It screened 1,730 pre-1940 buildings across the MRC, and for the Ville de Témiscaming it retained exactly three properties, none of them residential: the Gare de Témiscaming (15 rue Humphrey, 1927), the Poste de relais pour le flottage du bois d'Opémican (5555 chemin Opémican, 1883) and the Ancien bassin de charge de la Gatineau Power (lot 3 658 710, between 78 and 126 avenue Thorne). The published inventory carries no materials field at all — only addresses, dates, roles and particularités/justifications — so even where it does inventory a building it would not have filled this page's columns. Behind it sits a separate 51-page heritage analysis by Julien Rivard, Paul Trépanier and Manon Sartou, which is not publicly available; it is the document that might still carry house-level description, and chasing it is a live task. This negative result is recorded at ingest and could not be verified against the inventory itself: no public copy was located, the MRC's website was unreachable when this page was built, and the Répertoire du patrimoine culturel du Québec lists no inventory entry for the MRC.

The house typologies in that inventory are not Témiscaming's. It does document regional house types — maison de colonisation, maison de colonisation à trois pignons, grande maison carrée, maison de colonisation avec galerie ceinturante, cottage carré, maison à pignon sur rue avec galerie ceinturante — but they sit along 5e-et-6e Rang Est, 7e-et-8e Rang Est/Ouest, 9e Rang Est/Ouest, rue du Carrefour Nord and rue Principale Est/Ouest, in Latulipe-et-Gaboury, not in Témiscaming. They are rural colonisation types with nothing to do with company housing, and they are out of scope here.

The founding dates differ by source and are all correct in their own terms: the Adams plan is 1917, the mill and first construction 1918, the municipality 1920 (as Kipawa), the present name 1921. Only one house-type designation is documented anywhere — Type 171C, on Elm Street, recorded in the title of a 1995 Gabor Szilasi photograph — and no materials, roof form or storey count attach to it, or to any other Témiscaming model; the two records on this page are therefore defined by builder and period, which is what the sources support. The row houses on rue Anvik, eight of which burned in 2019, have a disputed date: the fire chief described them as "over 80 years old", which would place them before 1939, while Radio-Canada reported them "construites dans les années 1940".

And the Site patrimonial du Quartier-du-Moulin is in Gatineau, not Témiscaming — an easy confusion, because Canadian International Paper built both, and the RPCQ's Gatineau entry explicitly links the two developments; nothing in Témiscaming carries that status. Part 6 connects the two through the canonical type company-house-executive-1925, which is the right way to read the resemblance: one company, two towns, no shared designation.
