Residential Architectural Styles of Verdun (borough of Montréal)

Verdun exists because of a wall. The land is flat, low and right against the Saint-Laurent at the foot of the Lachine rapids, and until the river was held back every spring flood made it unbuildable — the 2005 Évaluation du patrimoine urbain says so in one sentence: residential development « prend son envol au début du XXe siècle, après la mise en place de la digue protégeant le territoire des fortes inondations printanières ». The dike went up from the 1890s; the town filled in behind it in about sixty years and then stopped, because it had run out of ground.

The corporate history is short and steep. The village of la Rivière-Saint-Pierre was detached from the paroisse Notre-Dame on 1 January 1875 and took the name Verdun the following year; it became a ville in 1907 and a cité in 1912, and it was a separate municipality until 1 January 2002, when it became a borough of Montréal. Its population was about 1,900 in 1901 and 25,000 twenty years later. By the 1920s, the cahier records, Verdun was the third-largest city in Québec, after Montréal and Québec, and it was extraordinarily dense for a place with no tall buildings. The housing shortage that followed became, in the document's word, endémique: by 1946 every one of the 18,000 dwellings in the city was occupied.

What was built is plex, almost without exception, and the cahier's own sector fiches quietly divide it in two. North of avenue Desmarchais — the older half, built through « la vague de 1900 à 1920 » — the standard building is three storeys of brick with the stair to the upper flats climbing the outside, elaborate cornices, vertical windows with stained glass, and lanes laid out in an H behind. The document's verdict on it is five words: « Ces constructions sont typiquement montréalaises. » South of Desmarchais the same fabric changes register — two storeys, few exterior stairs, and a deep front balcony with woodwork the cahier twice pauses to admire. The two residential sectors it ranks as exceptional are both of the southern kind, and both are ranked for uniformity rather than for rarity.

Two things interrupt the plex. Crawford Park, laid out just before the Second World War under the federal National Housing Act, is a few hundred brick houses of a storey and a half built to award-winning plans, on streets that abandon the grid — the borough's one piece of planned suburb, and the same object as the wartime housing already recorded at Lévis and in the Sud-Ouest. And île des Sœurs, farmland belonging to the Congrégation de Notre-Dame from 1769 until it was annexed in 1956, was replanned in the 1960s in collaboration with Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, who left three residential slabs and a filling station on it. A city built out of one repeated building, with a garden suburb and a modernist new town stapled to either end.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

21.E.1B La rue Wellington exceptional Along its whole length rue Wellington is a major axis of Verdun's commercial life. It is lined with an ensemble of generally three-storey buildings, occupied at ground level by shops and on the upper floors by dwellings. Access to the commercial spaces is level with the street. The shopfronts are tall and carry a transom, and the commercial storey is set off from the residential storeys by an elaborate cornice. Because the buildings are built right up to the property line, an original typology developed in order to give the dwellings alcove balconies. The façades are generally of brick with insertions of worked stone at the lintels, jambs and sills.
Sur toute sa longueur, la rue Wellington constitue un axe majeur de la vie commerçante de Verdun. On y retrouve un ensemble de bâtiments généralement de trois étages, occupés au rez-de-chaussée par des commerces et aux étages par des logements. L'accès aux espaces commerciaux est de plain-pied. Les vitrines commerciales sont hautes et dotées d'un tympan, et l'étage commercial est démarqué des étages résidentiels par une corniche élaborée. Les immeubles étant construits à la limite de la propriété, une typologie originale s'est développée pour doter les logements de balcons en alcôve. Les façades sont généralement en brique avec des insertions de pierre travaillée aux linteaux, jambages et allèges.
The decisive sentence for this site is « une typologie originale s'est développée pour doter les logements de balcons en alcôve » — a balcony recessed into the wall plane rather than projecting from it, invented because building to the property line left nowhere to project into. The cahier calls it an original typology in so many words; it is the one clearly Verdun-specific residential invention the document names. The same alcove balcony recurs in 21.I.5 and 21.I.6 on rue de Verdun.

21.E.1C La rue de l'Église exceptional As one of Verdun's founding routes, rue de l'Église holds the oldest commercial buildings. Fifty feet wide, with buildings on both sides erected right on the property line, the street projects a strongly urban image. The buildings that line it show a great variety of characteristics, making up an eclectic composition. The façade claddings are brick or stone, and the façades are generally ornamented. The general height of the buildings is three storeys and the ground floors are at ground level.
Comme tracé fondateur de Verdun, la rue de l'Église compte les plus anciennes constructions commerciales. D'une largeur de cinquante pieds et avec des constructions de part et d'autre érigées à la limite de la propriété, cette rue projette une image très urbaine. Les bâtiments qui la bordent présentent une grande variété de caractéristiques, créant une composition éclectique. Les parements de façade sont la brique ou la pierre, et les façades sont généralement ornementées. La hauteur générale des bâtiments est de trois étages et les rez-de-chaussée sont au niveau du sol.

21.E.2 L'avenue Desmarchais exceptional Avenue Desmarchais presents an exceptional and distinctive environment. Its broad landscaped median helps create a more open atmosphere. On either side are two-storey dwellings of various typologies, notably ensembles of two semi-detached duplexes or of three-dwelling buildings in which the largest dwelling is on the ground floor. The whole has a fine homogeneity. The level of the ground floor is much the same along the entire avenue. The buildings are clad in brick with tall vertical windows ornamented with stained glass, often combined in groups of two or three. Deep balconies with refined woodwork adorn the façades.
L'avenue Desmarchais présente un environnement exceptionnel et particulier. Son large terre-plein paysagé contribue à créer une atmosphère plus dégagée. Les bâtiments sont à parement de brique avec de hautes fenêtres verticales et ornées de vitraux, souvent combinées en groupes de deux ou trois. 21.E.5 Le voisinage Crawford exceptional A few years before the Second World War the government enacted the "National Housing Act". New housing typologies took shape within that framework, and in particular the mass-produced single-family dwelling. The voisinage Crawford offers an exceptional example of this new kind of urban intervention, made up of one-and-a-half-storey buildings clad in brick. These buildings were built on the basis of prize-winning architectural plans. The homogeneity of the heights, the materials, the roof pitches, the mode of siting and the landscaping contributes to the quality of the ensemble. Here the orthogonal street grid is abandoned.
Quelques années avant la Seconde Guerre mondiale, le gouvernement promulgue le « National Housing Act ». De nouvelles typologies d'habitation prendront forme dans ce cadre, et plus particulièrement l'habitation unifamiliale de masse. Le voisinage Crawford offre un exemple exceptionnel de ce nouveau type d'intervention urbaine, constitué d'immeubles d'un étage et demi recouverts d'un parement de brique. Ces immeubles ont été construits sur la base de plans d'architecture primés. L'homogénéité des hauteurs, des matériaux, des pentes de toit, du mode d'implantation et du paysagement contribue à la qualité de l'ensemble. On abandonne ici la trame de rues orthogonale.
The two streets come from the photo captions — « 21.E.5 Le développement Crawford Park, rue Fayolle » and « rue Truman ». The cahier repeats the whole description almost word for word at 21.I.9, « Un projet "National Housing Act" », for a second NHA scheme built « suivant les mêmes typologies que dans le secteur Crawford » but « inscrit dans la trame de rues orthogonale de la ville » and laid out around a park; and it records at 21.I.10 that « la typologie "War Time" dans un projet intégré décrite précédemment s'est aussi implantée dans ce secteur, sur des terrains lotis préalablement selon une trame conventionnelle ». 21.E.6 L'île des Sœurs exceptional The urban planning of this sector of île des Sœurs was worked out in collaboration with the internationally renowned architect Ludwig Mies van der Rohe. Three residential towers by the architect are also found there, as well as the Esso company's filling station. Van der Rohe is also the author, in Montréal, of the Westmount Square complex and of the Saidye Bronfman Centre. The urban planning of île des Sœurs rests on building tall so as to free up larger areas of ground (parks, community spaces and so on), on public development of the shoreline, on a separation between pedestrian and automobile circulation, and on an orientation of the buildings that allows views through to the river. It is these urban elements that characterise the sector.
La planification urbaine de ce secteur de l'île des Sœurs a été élaborée en collaboration avec l'architecte de réputation internationale Ludwig Mies van der Rohe. On y retrouve aussi trois tours d'habitation de l'architecte ainsi que la station d'essence de la pétrolière Esso. Van der Rohe est aussi l'auteur, à Montréal, du complexe Westmount Square et du Centre Saidye Bronfman. La planification urbaine de l'île des Sœurs repose sur la construction en hauteur dégageant de plus grands espaces au sol (parcs, espaces communautaires, etc.), l'aménagement public de la berge, une séparation entre la circulation piétonne et la circulation automobile, et une orientation des bâtiments permettant les percées visuelles sur le fleuve. Ce sont ces éléments urbains qui caractérisent ce secteur.
The three towers and the filling station are named with their addresses in the cahier's own list of immeubles de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle: « 201, rue Corot — Habitations multiples – Mies van der Rohe »; « 100 et 200, rue De Gaspé — Habitations multiples – Mies van der Rohe »; « 201, rue Berlioz — Station-service Esso – Mies van der Rohe ». The document spells the name two ways: the body text of this fiche and the immeubles list give « Mies van der Rohe », while the photo captions on this page and the §3.1 text give « Mies van de Rohe ». The correct form is used in the records here and the source's slip is recorded rather than reproduced. The cahier also carries a separate ensemble urbain d'intérêt, 21.U.1 L'île des Sœurs, for the later development « dans le prolongement du plan d'implantation original ».

21.E.7 Le secteur de l'aqueduc exceptional In Verdun the aqueduct sector takes in the essential part of the canal itself. The land occupied today by the plant was originally Verdun's. The aqueduct canal sector forms part of a larger sector that also overlaps the boroughs of LaSalle and Le Sud-Ouest. In 1852 the design of a new water-supply scheme was entrusted to the engineer Thomas C. Keefer. That scheme included the digging of a 2.4 km canal bringing water drawn upstream of the Lachine rapids to the Pavillon des roues, which stood where the Atwater filtration plant is now. Today, at the ends of the canal — widened twice — stand the pumping station built in 1923 and the filtration plant ordered by the municipal council after the typhoid epidemic of 1910. Montréal's aqueduct system is a formidable work of engineering that bears witness to the history of technological advance and to the management of public-health and fire-fighting problems. On that count it deserves recognition as exceptional heritage.
À Verdun, le secteur de l'aqueduc comprend l'essentiel du canal lui-même. Le territoire occupé aujourd'hui par l'usine était à l'origine verdunois. Le secteur du canal de l'aqueduc fait partie d'un secteur plus grand qui chevauche aussi les arrondissements de LaSalle et du Sud-Ouest. En 1852, on confie la conception d'un nouveau plan d'approvisionnement en eau à l'ingénieur Thomas C. Keefer. Ce plan comprend le creusage d'un canal de 2,4 km qui amène l'eau puisée en amont des rapides de Lachine au Pavillon des roues, qui était situé à l'emplacement de l'actuelle usine de filtration Atwater. Aujourd'hui, aux extrémités du canal, élargi à deux reprises, on retrouve la station de pompage construite en 1923 et l'usine de filtration commandée par le conseil municipal à la suite de l'épidémie de typhoïde de 1910. Le système d'aqueduc de Montréal constitue une formidable œuvre d'ingénierie qui témoigne de l'histoire des avancements technologiques et de la gestion des problématiques de santé publique et de lutte contre les incendies. À ce titre, il mérite une reconnaissance comme patrimoine exceptionnel.
Infrastructure, not housing, but it is the reason Verdun has the shape it has: « Le canal de l'aqueduc délimite le territoire final de la ville de Verdun. Des terres concédées à l'origine en bordure du fleuve et qui débordaient au-delà du tracé actuel de l'aqueduc, seule la portion au sud dudit canal demeurera partie de la ville constituée. » The sector is shared with LaSalle and Le Sud-Ouest, so the same ground will carry a 22.E.n code in the Sud-Ouest cahier.

1660–1875 · The côte de Verdun and île Saint-Paul

1660 – 1875

The côte de Verdun — « ou des Argoulets » — is the strip of first concessions south of Montréal at the foot of the Lachine rapids, occupied by soldiers in 1660 to hold the approach to the town against Iroquois raids. After the Grande paix of 1701 the land goes over to farming. Facing it in the river, île Saint-Paul is bought in part by the fur merchant Jacques LeBer in the last quarter of the seventeenth century, who builds a fortified farm on the north-east terrace; his daughter Jeanne passes the land to the Congrégation de Notre-Dame, and by the second half of the eighteenth century the sisters own the whole island, farming it from the ferme Saint-Paul and then, after a fire, the ferme Sainte-Famille. The aqueduct canal that will become Verdun's northern boundary is dug on Thomas C. Keefer's 1852 scheme and supplies Montréal with drinking water from 1856. Almost nothing residential survives from this phase; the cahier says plainly that « il ne reste pratiquement aucune trace des premiers établissements dans Verdun », and names the maison Étienne-Nivard-de-Saint-Dizier on boulevard LaSalle as the one reminder of the road's age.

1875–1900 · The village, the floods and the dike

1875 – 1900

The village is created in 1875 and renamed Verdun in 1876, and then almost nothing happens for twenty-five years, because the river will not let it. Spring floods make the banks unusable and stall the municipality; the council looks for a fix and builds a first dike in the 1890s. It works well enough to open the land, though the cahier notes that the flooding was only ended for good by the canalisation of the Saint-Laurent much later. The dike also produced the promenade along the river that is still the borough's front. The two founding routes are already in place: the chemin de la rivière Saint-Pierre or Lower Lachine Road, now boulevard LaSalle, and the chemin de la côte Saint-Paul, now rue de l'Église.

1900–1930 · The wave: Québec's third city

1900 – 1930

The wave. Behind the dike the town fills up in a single generation, mostly between 1900 and 1920, with a working population employed in Montréal and — in 1915 — built for by small proprietors rather than by developers. The building is the three-storey plex with the exterior stair, repeated street after street on conventional lots with lanes; three separate sector fiches describe it in the same words. Verdun becomes a ville in 1907 and a cité in 1912, and by the 1920s it is the third city of Québec. On rue Wellington and rue de l'Église, built to the property line with no setback to project a balcony into, the same fabric produces the one invention the cahier calls Verdun's own: the balcon en alcôve, cut back into the wall. South of avenue Desmarchais the plex loses a storey and its outside stair and gains its deep balcony, and it is that southern version that will be ranked exceptional.

Three flats stacked behind a brick front, one to a floor, each reached by its own door and the upper two by a stair climbing the outside. Built through the 1900–1920 wave on lanes laid out in an H, it is what most of northern Verdun is made of, and the cahier disposes of it in five words: typically Montréal.

Where it comes from

Verdun's plex is the Montréal plex, arriving late and all at once. The 2005 cahier dates the fabric to « la vague de 1900 à 1920 » in three separate sector fiches and links it directly to the dike: residential development « prend son envol au début du XXe siècle, après la mise en place de la digue protégeant le territoire des fortes inondations printanières ». The mechanism that puts the stair outside is not explained here — for that see the shared explainer at /topics/escalier-exterieur/ and the Sud-Ouest synthèse, which traces it to the compulsory front setback. What Verdun adds is the counter-case: the cahier's fiche 21.I.7 records that south of avenue Desmarchais « il y a peu d'escaliers extérieurs, mais la présence des balcons avant s'affirme ». One borough, one form, and a line across it where the stair goes back indoors.

1930–1950 · Depression, the National Housing Act and the war

1930 – 1950

The Depression hits hard and the housing shortage becomes chronic. Two federal instruments answer it. The National Housing Act, enacted a few years before the war, produces « l'habitation unifamiliale de masse », and Verdun gets one of its best examples: Crawford Park, brick houses of a storey and a half to prize-winning plans, uniform in height, material, roof pitch, siting and planting, on curving streets that break with the grid. The cahier records the same house twice more in the borough on conventional street plans, so that the three occurrences differ only in layout. Meanwhile munitions plants operate in Verdun through both world wars, in a town the document otherwise calls almost exclusively residential, and wartime construction pushes the stock to its peak: in 1946 all 18,000 dwellings are occupied.

1950–2002 · Build-out, île des Sœurs and the Mies plan

1950 – 2002

The last mainland ground goes after 1950 — the strip between the aqueduct canal and rue Bannantyne, built up in row duplexes from a handful of repeated models — and by the end of the 1950s the territory is full. The expansion moves to the only virgin land left. Île des Sœurs is annexed in 1956, the sisters leave, and through the 1960s the island is replanned as, in the cahier's phrase, « un vaste projet de développement urbain planifié » worked out with Mies van der Rohe: build tall to free the ground, make the shore public, separate people from cars, and turn the blocks so the river shows between them. Three residential slabs and an Esso station by Mies survive from it. By 1968 the island holds over 800 dwellings and 3,000 residents; it now holds more than 13,000, out of about 60,000 in the borough. The pont Champlain, begun in 1957 and open in 1962, crosses at its edge. Verdun is merged into Montréal on 1 January 2002.

The most sparsely evidenced record on this page, and included because it closes Verdun's plex sequence rather than because the source says much. Everything comes from one four-sentence fiche, 21.I.8, which is a secteur de valeur intéressante — hence `sectors: null`, since the ten 21.I codes are not encoded in this part. That fiche gives the date, the row form, the dwelling count implied by "duplex", and a judgement about materials; it gives no storey count, no roof form, no cladding, no opening description and no ornament. `storeys: 2`, `roof.form: flat` and `principal_cladding` are entered from the word "duplex" and from the borough's uniform practice as the cahier describes it elsewhere, not from this fiche, and are the fields to distrust. Articulation and openings are left empty. The canonical link is to the plex family as a whole rather than to one of its seven children, because nothing in the source says where the stair to the upper dwelling is.

Notes on the approach

This page is built on a territorial characterisation, not a type catalogue, and six things about that need stating plainly.

The borough code is 21, and it was read from inside the document. The cahier is published by the Ville de Montréal as 24_evaluation_patrimoine_ver.pdf, but every code printed inside it begins 21 — 21.E.1A to 21.E.7, 21.I.1 to 21.I.10, 21.U.1, and 21.AP.1 and 21.AP.2. The file prefix belongs to the numbering of the published volumes, not to the sectors, and the two sequences do not correspond: the Sud-Ouest is code 22 in file 12, while Saint-Laurent is file 22. Never infer one from the other.

Only the exceptional-value sectors are encoded. Nine records, covering the seven secteurs de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle — 21.E.1 is printed in three lettered parts. The cahier also carries ten secteurs de valeur patrimoniale intéressante, one ensemble urbain d'intérêt and two secteurs d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel, and they are not thin: 21.I.1 le Vieux-Verdun, 21.I.2 le voisinage Nord, 21.I.3 l'îlot ancien (a pre-lane subdivision where « l'absence de ruelle a entraîné une typologie particulière donnant accès à la cour arrière par une porte cochère »), 21.I.4 le boulevard LaSalle, 21.I.5 le secteur Rielle et de Verdun, 21.I.6 le secteur des Avenues, 21.I.7 le secteur Woodland et de Verdun, 21.I.8 le voisinage Ouest, 21.I.9 un projet « National Housing Act », 21.I.10 le voisinage Sud, 21.U.1 l'île des Sœurs, and 21.AP.1 île des Sœurs and 21.AP.2 zone côtière for archaeology. Four of the type records on this page are built out of those fiches even though the sectors themselves are not published here, and each says so in its profile_note. Enumerating the I, U and AP sectors is Part 10b.

The cahier disagrees with itself, and with the municipal record, about dates. Chapter 2 says the village of la Rivière-Saint-Pierre was created « en 1874 » and renamed Verdun « en 1878 »; the archaeology chapter of the same document says « le village est créé en 1876 »; the municipal-status record gives 1 January 1875 for the creation and 28 December 1876 for the renaming. Chapter 2 also says Verdun obtained « son statut de ville en 1909 », where the municipal record gives 14 March 1907 for ville and 21 December 1912 for cité. The dike is dated 1895 in chapter 2 and « dont la construction s'amorce en 1896 » in the archaeology chapter. The corporate dates used on this page are the municipal ones and the phase boundaries avoid resting on any of the contested years; the cahier's own figures are kept wherever they are the thing being quoted. The superficie is a fourth case: the Part 10 memo gives 9.7 km², the fr.wikipedia infobox 983 ha, and no municipal source giving an area was retrieved — both figures are printed rather than averaged.

Two instruments could not be confirmed as the memo states them. The Part 10 memo lists a « 987-series » demolition by-law for Verdun. No municipal source found gives one; the borough's demolition regime is RCA21 210020, under which the members of the comité consultatif d'urbanisme sit as the comité de démolition, and that is what is recorded. Separately, the 2020 fee schedule — Règlement sur les tarifs (exercice financier 2020), RCA19 210007 — is indexed as carrying a PIIA study fee, $265, reserved for one operation and one building: enlarging the roof volume of a "wartime"-type house to add habitable space. That is the only fee schedule anywhere on this site that names a house type, and it is worth saying why it matters — a tariff line is the point at which a typological category stops being descriptive and starts costing money. But the by-law text itself could not be retrieved: the Ville's legacy document tree returns 404 for every path tried, the 311 information bank redirects in a loop, and the web archive is unreachable from here. The fee is therefore stated in English only, and no French is quoted for it. Getting the primary is a Part 10b task.

Mies van der Rohe is spelled two ways in the source. The body of fiche 21.E.6 and the list of immeubles de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle give Mies van der Rohe; the photo captions on the same page and the text of §3.1 give Mies van de Rohe. The correct form is used throughout this page and the slip is recorded rather than reproduced. The three residential slabs are at 100 and 200 rue De Gaspé and 201 rue Corot; the Esso station is at 201 rue Berlioz.

Verdun is also a French commune, and the two have nothing to do with each other. Verdun in the Meuse — the battlefield town — has its own heritage designation, a site patrimonial remarquable classed by arrêté du 13 avril 2022. It shares nothing with this borough but a name, taken here from the côte de Verdun, itself named for the first concessions on the ground. This belongs in the site glossary as well as here; it is recorded in this note because data/canon/glossary.yaml is owned by another part of the current work.

Finally, worth listing because the cahier singles them out and no type record on this page covers them: under LES HABITATIONS in its list of exceptionally valuable buildings it names four maisons urbaines — 703-707 rue Gordon, 3267, 3513 and 3523 rue Wellington — a maison bourgeoise at 5695 boulevard LaSalle, and the maison Nivard-de Saint-Dizier at 7244 boulevard LaSalle, monument historique reconnu, which the document elsewhere dates to « possiblement au début du XVIIIe siècle » and attributes to the Sœurs de la Congrégation de Notre-Dame. It gives no typological description of any of them, so none has been turned into a type record here.
