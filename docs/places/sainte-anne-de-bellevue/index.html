Residential Architectural Styles of Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue

Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue sits on the last point of the island of Montréal, where the lac des Deux-Montagnes drops into the lac Saint-Louis over the rapides de Sainte-Anne. Everything about the place follows from that fall of water. It was first a portage, then the last obligatory halt on the island for anyone bound for the Pays d'en Haut, then — once the first locks and the canal were dug between 1840 and 1843 — a lock town on the through route to the Ottawa. The 2005 heritage evaluation reads the result as a village whose institutional core, « l'église, le presbytère et le couvent », was cut off from the newer commercial town by the railways laid from 1860 and by a twentieth-century highway; the two halves are still legible on the ground.

What the sources describe is a small place with four distinct kinds of house in it. In the îlot villageois beside the canal, modest dwellings « généralement recouvertes de clin », of two storeys, built close to the street. Behind them, on rue Saint-Jean-Baptiste, workers' cottages under steep dormered roofs with their ground floors barely raised, which the cahier calls « un ensemble de cottages tout à fait caractéristique du patrimoine architectural de Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue ». Up the slope, the college town that grew beside Macdonald College and John Abbott: solid clay-brick cottages under pitched roofs and some large stucco houses. And on the far side of the tracks, the twenty-five houses J.-J. Harpell built round his Garden City Press in 1918 on Ebenezer Howard's model.

The town markets that first sector as a village patrimonial. That is a slogan, not a status. Nothing in Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue is protected at the scale of an ensemble — no declared site, no cited site, no RPCQ site record of any kind. Two individual buildings carry the entire statutory weight of the town: the maison Simon-Fraser at 153 rue Sainte-Anne, classée in 1962, and the maison Michel-Robillard at 20345 chemin Sainte-Marie, citée in 2014. The church and the presbytery at the heart of the advertised village are merely inventoriés. Everything else is held by design review.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

During the Depression he hired unemployed men to plant trees and commissioned Frederick Todd to make a Japanese garden with a pond opposite the works, of which nothing survives. In 1945 he transferred control of the firm to a workers' cooperative.

1663–1760 · Seigneurial côtes, the portage and the fur-trade halt

1663 – 1760

The Sulpicians become seigneurs of the island in 1663, and the west end is a strategic contact point with Indigenous nations and an outpost for the defence of the French colony. The parish of Saint-Louis-du-Bout-de-l'Île — also written Saint-Louis-du-Haut-de-l'Île — is founded in 1677, and the abbé François-Saturnin Lascaris d'Urfé is recorded as its first resident curé in 1686, returning to France the following year. The abbé René-Charles de Breslay takes over the mission in about 1703 and is curé here until 1719; the parish takes the name Sainte-Anne-du-Bout-de-l'Île in 1714. Nothing residential survives above ground from this phase in the village core, and no source this project holds describes its houses.

1760–1855 · The parish, the village and the canal

1760 – 1855

The village that stands today begins here. The 2024 plan d'urbanisme dates the noyau villageois to the end of the eighteenth century, and the oldest house in it is of that moment: the maison Simon-Fraser, built between 1790 and 1810 for Peter Grant, a partner in the North West Company. A post office opens in 1835 and the municipality is created in 1845 under the name Bout-de-l'Isle. Between 1840 and 1843 the first locks and the canal de Sainte-Anne are built, the gateway to the Ottawa river and part of the network that carried settlers to Upper Canada, then tens of thousands of steamer passengers, and all through the nineteenth century the cageux riding the timber rafts down from the Outaouais and the Gatineau. The village develops beside those busy locks. The parish church now standing goes up in 1853–1859, built by Félix Barbeau and John Lewis Forbes.

The ordinary house of the two West Island village cores — modest, one and a half to two storeys, rectangular, under a steep two-slope roof with dormers, standing close to a narrow street, in clapboard at Sainte-Anne and roughcast at Pointe-Claire. Both places advertise a heritage village. Between them they have three protected buildings and no protected ensemble. One record, two municipalities: the photograph on this card is a Sainte-Anne street, and Pointe-Claire's stands beside it on the type page.

Where it comes from

Pointe-Claire's parish dates from 1698 and Sainte-Anne's core grew around a mission that took its present name in 1714, and both produced a small village around the church rather than a string of farms along a côte. A plan of 1767 already shows Pointe-Claire's clustered on the three avenues it still occupies; by the nineteenth century it held hotels, artisans' workshops and shops serving the surrounding farms and the first summer people on the lake shore. Most of what stands there now is younger than that, because a fire in 1900 took a good part of it — the cahier says so plainly, and then argues that the value survived the fire anyway, in the width of the streets and the height of the houses. Sainte-Anne's core sits at the point of the island between the canal locks of 1843 and the railway bridges, and the 2005 cahier separates it into two things worth naming - the îlot villageois round the church, of two-storey clapboard houses built close to the street, and the workers' cottages of rue Saint-Jean-Baptiste, steep-roofed and dormered and low-floored, which it calls the most characteristic thing in the town's architectural heritage. One classed house, built between 1790 and 1810 for the fur trader Peter Grant and bought by Simon Fraser in 1820, shows the whole arc in a single building: a French-tradition rubble carré under a steep roof, then a bell-cast eave and two rows of ornamented dormers added as taste changed, then two flats in 1892, then a bank counter from 1906 to 1954, then a restoration in 1962 that kept the evidence of all of it.

Siting & landscape
  • Both cores are small, tight and close to the water. Sainte-Anne's sits at the point of the island beside the canal, gathered round the church, the former convent and the presbytery; Pointe-Claire's stands around avenues Sainte-Anne, Saint-Joachim and Saint-Jean-Baptiste, an agglomeration already drawn on a plan of 1767.
  • Houses stand close to the street, or set back only slightly, along narrow streets. Both cahiers make the lotting, the scale and the alignment — rather than any single building — the thing of value.
  • The Sainte-Anne cahier is explicit that it is the combination that carries the interest, naming together the quality of the institutional buildings, the subdivision and the shape of the lots, the modest scale of the houses and the nearness of the canal.
Massing
  • Modest. One and a half storeys, rectangular, under a steep two-slope roof — the cottages of Sainte-Anne's rue Saint-Jean-Baptiste and the roughcast houses of Pointe-Claire's village streets. The taller two-storey clapboard house of Sainte-Anne's îlot villageois is a neighbouring form and carries its own record on that page.
  • Ground floors sit low — the Sainte-Anne cahier names « la faible élévation des rez-de-chaussée » as one of four things producing the effect of the ensemble on rue Saint-Jean-Baptiste.
  • Dormers light the attic. At the classed Sainte-Anne house they run in two rows, large and small, an arrangement the RPCQ fiche singles out; on the village streets there is usually one row.
  • Where the roof slope is bell-cast the eave is carried out past the wall to shelter a gallery across the front.
Articulation
  • Sober. On the classed house the cornice returns as a pediment on the gable walls and a neo-Gothic porch stands in front of the door, both nineteenth-century additions.
  • Galleries, verandahs and turned or wrought railings across the front, added and re-added over the life of these streets.
Openings
  • Regular but not academic. The classed house's front entry was moved to the right side at some point in the nineteenth century.
Materials
  • Roughcast render is the norm on the Pointe-Claire village street. Clapboard runs alongside it in Sainte-Anne, where the cahier says the îlot villageois houses are « généralement recouvertes de clin ».
  • Rubble stone at the two protected Sainte-Anne houses, one of them with a stone spine wall in the basement. Brick and stone on the commercial frontages of rue Sainte-Anne.
  • Chimney stacks in the gable walls.

Conservation guidance (fiche)
  • Neither village core carries an ensemble instrument. Pointe-Claire's 2013 municipal citation covers the site patrimonial de la pointe — the institutional ground with the windmill, the church, the convent and the presbytery — and stops at its edge; the village street fabric next door has nothing.
  • Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue has no ensemble instrument at all. Two individual houses are protected - the maison Simon-Fraser, classed by the minister in 1962, and the maison Michel-Robillard, cited by the town in 2014. The church and the presbytery that anchor the advertised « village patrimonial » are inventoriés only, which records and does not protect.
  • What both places do have is design review. Sainte-Anne's PIIA by-law 798 carries a chapter of objectives and criteria for built and archaeological heritage — an instrument that governs how a permit is judged, not one that designates a place.

1855–1930 · The railways, the college campuses and the Garden City Press

1855 – 1930

The Grand Trunk reaches the West Island in 1855 and Sainte-Anne becomes a stopping place twice over — for the locks and for two railways, the Canadian National and the Canadian Pacific. The municipality is renamed Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue in 1878, is erected as a village municipality that year, and takes the title of ville from 1895. Baie-d'Urfé is detached from its territory in 1911. At the end of the nineteenth century the shore is taken up by Montréal's anglophone bourgeoisie as a resort — yacht clubs, hotels, tennis courts and golf links — and at the beginning of the twentieth the campuses of Macdonald College (ten buildings of 1905–1908 by Hutchison and Wood) and John Abbott change the social and urban dynamic of the village, their staff living on campus or just outside it. A manufacture, a sawmill, a cinema and a military hospital arrive. In 1918 J.-J. Harpell brings the Garden City Press, a printer of school textbooks and workers' educational material; influenced by Ebenezer Howard, he lays out streets, parks and services and builds twenty-five houses for his employees near the works, and during the Depression hires unemployed men to plant trees and commissions Frederick Todd to lay out a Japanese garden with a pond opposite the plant, of which nothing survives. He hands the firm to a workers' cooperative in 1945.

Two-storey clapboard village house of the îlot villageois La maison villageoise en clin du noyau villageois

1.E.9
Phase attribution: provisional

The ordinary house of the village core beside the canal, and the thing the town means when it advertises a « village patrimonial » — two storeys, clapboard, set close to the street on the old lots, in the group of streets that runs from the church and the former convent down to the water. The sector is graded exceptional by the Ville de Montréal's 2005 evaluation and protected by nothing but design review.

Where it comes from

The village grew where the portage and then the locks made travellers stop. It was constituted a village municipality in 1878 and became a ville in 1895, and by the early twentieth century the cahier describes a denser urban centre beside the canal, with the older institutional group — church, presbytery, convent — cut off from it by the railways laid from 1860 and by a twentieth-century highway. Sainte-Anne's shore had by then become a resort for Montréal's anglophone bourgeoisie, and the campuses of Macdonald College and John Abbott were changing who lived in the village.

Twenty-five houses built in 1918 for the workers of the Garden City Press, on streets, parks and services laid out at the same time — a garden-city quarter on Ebenezer Howard's model at the far end of the island of Montréal, graded an ensemble urbain d'intérêt in 2005. What the houses look like is not recorded in any source this project has read, and nothing is invented here to fill the gap.

Where it comes from

J.-J. Harpell brought the Garden City Press to Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue in 1918, a printer of school textbooks and educational material for workers, choosing the site for the two railways that crossed it and allowed fast shipping across Canada. He was influenced by the theories of the Briton Ebenezer Howard, who proposed garden cities as the cure for urban insalubrity; in that spirit he set out to help his employees buy their own homes and paid attention to the land around the plant. In 1918 streets, parks and urban infrastructure were laid out and twenty-five houses built near the printing works. During the Depression he hired unemployed men to plant trees and commissioned Frederick Todd to make a luxurious Japanese garden with a pond facing the works, of which nothing survives today. In 1945 he transferred control of the firm to a workers' cooperative.

1930–2002 · Suburbanisation and the merger

1930 – 2002

Rail supplants navigation, and the canal and locks change use; the village keeps its shape while the town spreads. In 1964 the ville annexes the territory of the parish municipality. The Commission des lieux et monuments historiques du Canada recommends the designation of the canal in 1987. In 1962 the maison Simon-Fraser is restored under the direction of the architect Percy Roy Wilson — one of the precursors of modern architectural restoration in the Montréal region — with the support of the Société historique du Bout-de-l'Isle and the fondation Héritage canadien du Québec, and classed in the same year; it is sold to that foundation in 1965. On 1 January 2002 Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue is merged into the new ville de Montréal.

Wikimedia Commons was searched for a freely licensed photograph of a postwar bungalow in any of the six places and none was found. The one candidate that looked promising by its address — « Quatre-Vents, 12, avenue Dahlia (Dorval) », on the Model Homes street itself — was downloaded and inspected and is not a bungalow at all but a two-and-a-half-storey stuccoed house with a tiled coping and an arcaded loggia, so it was rejected. The Cité de Dorval's own heritage study of that address (LEMAYMICHAUD + Réal Paul architecte, « Expertise historique et étude patrimoniale, 12, avenue Dahlia, Dorval ») confirms the rejection and dates the house to 1803, enlarged 1874–85 in a neo-Gothic manner and wholly remade 1927–37 into the Italianate it wears now — one of the first stone houses in the district, standing on the street the bungalows were later built along, and not one of them. The same study independently repeats the cahier's figures for the 1951 project - 28 dwellings, 12 detached and 16 semi-detached bungalows, and two apartment buildings.

One storey, hipped roof, brick with artificial stone and clapboard for variety, a carport or a garage, a cul-de-sac and mature trees. Built from a short catalogue of models across all six West Island places from the late 1940s, and the only twentieth-century housing the 2005 evaluation series took seriously enough to invent a category for.

Where it comes from

The wave arrives at different moments and for different reasons. At Dorval it is the airport: the aerodrome opened in 1941, air traffic grew fast after the war, the rest of the territory was opened to subdivision and the population went from under 3,000 in the 1940s to more than 20,000 by 1971. At Pointe-Claire it is a straight housing shortage — a veterans' sector on the Wartime Housing Limited model about 1947, then the Magil project of 1953–55. At Lachine it is what the cahier calls « l'urbanisation massive des années 1950-1970 », running east across the last open ground and producing a catalogue of contractors' types so consistent that the dominant one is still known locally by its builder's name. At Baie-D'Urfé it is the Métropolitaine and the demographic boom together, pushing settlement north across the railway for the first time. What the 2005 cahiers do with all of it is the interesting part. The series invented a category, Ensembles urbains d'intérêt, to hold postwar suburbs, said openly that the exercise was exploratory, and then valued these places for their street layouts, their planting and the consistency of their materials rather than for their architecture — while recording, in the same paragraphs, that almost every house had already been raised, extended, re-clad or re-roofed. It is the one wave on these six pages that the source treats as heritage-in-progress rather than as heritage.

  • Detached on its own lot, set back from the street with side margins, and with parking in the front yard — the Lachine cahier's description of the same wave, and the one place any West Island source describes the siting rule rather than the building.
  • Cul-de-sac and crescent street patterns recur, sometimes with a planted median down a wide avenue, and Dorval's cahier ties the effect of every one of these sectors to mature trees on the properties rather than to the houses.

Conservation guidance (fiche)

2002–2026 · Merger, demerger, and inventory

2002 – 2026

The town is reconstituted on 1 January 2006 under Bill 9, after the referendums of 20 June 2004, with effect from 1 January. It inherits the 2005 sector evaluation the Ville de Montréal had drawn up for the merged arrondissement, and the plan d'urbanisme the Ville had adopted under number 04-047. It replaces both slowly: a PIIA by-law consolidated in 2017 (règlement 798), a demolition by-law (846), and a new plan d'urbanisme (863) adopted on 11 November 2024, whose heritage map names exactly two protected buildings. Parks Canada's management plan for the canal is tabled in Parliament on 16 December 2020. Since 2024 the agglomeration of Montréal has been running the pre-1940 inventory the 2021 Loi sur le patrimoine culturel requires of it, due by 1 April 2026; the town's own heritage page says so. That inventory, not a citation, is what will next change the record here.

Notes on the approach

The « village patrimonial » is a marketing phrase, and this page will not print it as anything else. The words come from the town's tourism site, visiter.sadb.qc.ca, where the village core is listed as an activity at 109 rue Sainte-Anne alongside the garlic festival and salsa by the water: « Aux rues Sainte-Anne et de l'Église qui structurent le secteur, se greffent quelques rues résidentielles dotées d'une architecture villageoise représentative de la fin du 19e et du début du 20e siècle. » That sentence is accurate as description and claims no status, and the page claims none either. The negative was checked three ways and holds three ways. The RPCQ's own search, filtered to catégorie de bien « Site patrimonial » and municipality Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue, returns « Aucun résultat »; the same search across the whole Montréal administrative region returns forty site records, from the site patrimonial de Montréal to the site patrimonial de la pointe Claire in the next town but two, and none of them is here. The RPCQ holds eleven immovable records for the municipality and every one of them is a single building or monument. And the town's own règlement 863 settles it from the municipal side: carte 7 legends « Immeubles qui présentent une protection particulière en vertu de la Loi sur le patrimoine culturel » and names two — A Maison Simon-Fraser (classée en 1962) and B Maison Michel-Robillard (citée en 2014). The town's complete published by-law list contains no citation by-law at all.

Classée and inventorié are not degrees of the same thing. The maison Simon-Fraser is classée immeuble patrimonial by the minister: work on it requires ministerial authorisation, and the protection reaches the interior, the remise and the land. The église (RPCQ 179473) and the presbytère (RPCQ 179475) carry the status line « Inventorié — -- », with no authority and no date, because they were catalogued by the Inventaire des lieux de culte du Québec of 2003–2004. An inventory records; it does not protect. The two buildings that anchor the advertised village are therefore the two with no legal protection at all, and the one building that is protected stands three doors along a commercial street.

The fiche for the maison Simon-Fraser contradicts itself twice, and both contradictions are left standing here rather than resolved. Its valeur patrimoniale calls the owner « Simon Fraser (1760-1839) », born in Scotland, settled in Canada with his family in 1789, a shareholder of the North West Company and seigneur of the fief Bellevue; its own index of associated persons gives « Fraser, Simon (1776 – 1862) », and the Ville de Montréal's 2005 cahier agrees with the index — « Simon Fraser (1776-1862), marchand de fourrures important, y possède une maison construite vers 1800 ». Those are not the same man's dates. Separately, the fiche's structured data block reads « Nombre d'étages : 3 ½ » while its own description reads « une demeure d'inspiration française… à un étage et demi »; the description agrees with the éléments caractéristiques, which describe a steep two-slope roof with two rows of dormers in the façade, so the storey count used on this site is one and a half and the 3 ½ figure is recorded as an error in the source.

The house was not built for Simon Fraser. The RPCQ is explicit: it was put up between 1790 and 1810 for Peter Grant (vers 1764–1848), one of Fraser's associates in the North West Company, and Fraser bought it only in 1820, after the manor of the seigneurie de Bellevue burned, and used it as his manor until his death. The Irish poet Thomas Moore is said to have stayed there in 1804 and composed The Canadian Boat Song, which is why the house carries the alternative name maison Thomas-Moore. From 1906 to 1954 it held a branch of the Bank of Montreal. Fraser's descendants owned it until 1965.

Four different founding dates circulate for this parish, and the sources do not agree. The Commission de toponymie has the parish of Saint-Louis-du-Bout-de-l'Île founded in 1677 and renamed Sainte-Anne-du-Bout-de-l'Île in 1714. The RPCQ's fiche for the maison Rangé-Dit-Laviolette in neighbouring Baie-D'Urfé says the parish was founded in 1686 by the abbé Lascaris d'Urfé. The town's own heritage page dates the parish to 1703, which is the year the Commission de toponymie gives for the abbé de Breslay taking over the mission. The place record uses 1677, because that is the date in the provincial toponymic authority, and the others are set out here rather than harmonised away.

The arrondissement code and the filename disagree again. The 2005 cahier prints its sector codes as 1.E.7 to 1.E.11, 1.I.2 to 1.I.5 and 1.U.1 — arrondissement number 1 — while the file the Ville publishes is 13_evaluation_patrimoine_lile.pdf. This is the same mismatch Part 10 recorded for Outremont and Westmount, and both numbers are stored on the place record.

The photograph of the village core does not quite show what the fiche describes, and the caption says so. The 2005 fiche's one dimensional statement about the houses of the îlot villageois is that they are « généralement recouvertes de clin, comptent deux étages et sont construites près de la rue ». The Ville de Montréal's own photograph of one of those residential streets, reproduced on this page, shows the clapboard and the tight setback plainly enough, but most of the row in frame is a storey lower than the fiche says — one-and-a-half-storey cottages under steep sheet-metal roofs with dormers and galleries, beside a two-storey painted-brick house. The fiche is the source and its wording is what the type record carries; the discrepancy is recorded here rather than quietly corrected, because a photograph is evidence of a different kind and this project does not let one overwrite the other.

What this project could not read. The Ville de Montréal's Grand répertoire du patrimoine bâti de Montréal, which holds a zone fiche for this village core, was offline during the crawl; the Ville's replacement page states that access to it and to two sister databases « sera à nouveau disponible dans le courant de l'année 2026 ». The town's own Plan d'urbanisme, Annexe 2, Analyse patrimoniale, which the 2005 cahier quotes twice as its footnote 6, is a 1989-generation document that is not published on the town's site. And the Répertoire d'architecture traditionnelle de la Communauté urbaine de Montréal — whose cahier sur l'architecture rurale the town's own 2014 citation by-law cites as the record in which the maison Michel-Robillard was catalogued — has not been obtained either. It is the one document that would give this page per-building descriptions rather than sector paragraphs, and it covers Baie-D'Urfé as well. None of the three has been used here except through the passages other documents quote from them.
