Residential Architectural Styles of Outremont (borough of Montréal)

Outremont began as farmland on the far side of the mountain — the côte Sainte-Catherine, whose first concessions date from 1694 and which became a municipality in its own right in 1855. The village of Outremont was detached from it on 23 February 1875, at the urging of Louis Beaubien, with something like three hundred residents; the story that barns had to be counted among the buildings to reach the legal threshold is repeated in every local history, and it gives the measure of the place at its start. For its first thirty-five years it was an anglophone suburb — roughly eighty per cent in the 1880s — and it did not elect a francophone mayor until 1910.

What turned the farms into streets was partly religious. The Clercs de Saint-Viateur arrived in 1887, and five more communities followed by 1920; their houses, colleges and churches fixed the axes along which the land was subdivided, and the église Saint-Viateur of 1910–13 — which carries the first work Guido Nincheri executed in Canada — still anchors the quarter that bears its name. The village became a ville in 1895 and a cité in 1915; parc Outremont was laid out in 1903, parc Pratt in 1931.

The result is a suburb built almost entirely in one generation. Between 1903 and 1920 the north and east filled with semi-detached single-family houses and paired duplexes with independent entrances — two storeys, brick, flat roofs, ground floors raised about a metre — and that short span is precisely why those streets are so homogeneous. The population, as the histories like to put it, doubled every fifteen years. The mountain flank went a different way: Édouard Gohier's Northmount Land Company subdivided it from 1906 into large irregular lots on the steepest ground in the borough, and through the 1920s and 1930s it filled with detached résidences cossues in every historicist idiom available — Beaux-Arts, château, Tudor, Arts and Crafts, neoclassical — and then, without pause, in the new ones: Art Deco, split-level, International Style. The Université de Montréal's expansion truncated what was left of the Northmount plan in 1961.

Outremont returned to ville status in 1970 and became a borough of Montréal on 1 January 2002. Its planning control today rests on two documents that describe territory rather than buildings: a 2020 typo-morphological study that cut the borough into four landscape areas and twenty-nine landscape units, and the 1992 Bisson inventory that graded every building and from which the list of témoins architecturaux significatifs was drawn.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

16.E.1 Secteur de la montagne exceptional This is the sector covering the built-up part located in the south of the borough, and it is the steepest zone in Outremont; the topography of the secteur de la montagne encouraged architectural and landscape feats. Opulent single-family residences dominate, though there are also a few semi-detached two-family buildings, and the sector presents a sampling of styles evocative of the past as well as of the new twentieth-century trends (Art déco, "split level", International Style). The lots vary in size because of the street layout: the streets are winding — sometimes switchbacking to avoid overly steep slopes — and lined with numerous trees, which reinforces the picturesque character of the neighbourhood. The opulent residences bordering the chemin de la Côte-Sainte-Catherine are perched on the upper part of their lots and reached by long staircases. Part a), situated south of the boulevard du Mont-Royal, is included in the arrondissement historique et naturel du mont Royal (the Mount Royal heritage and natural district).
Il s'agit du secteur qui couvre la partie bâtie située au sud de l'arrondissement. Cette zone est la plus escarpée d'Outremont. La topographie du secteur de la montagne a favorisé les prouesses architecturales et paysagères. Les résidences unifamiliales cossues dominent, mais on trouve aussi quelques bâtiments jumelés bifamiliaux. 16.E.4 Le parc Outremont exceptional This sector corresponds to the built frontage facing parc Outremont, as well as the segments of streets located between the park and chemin de la Côte-Sainte-Catherine. The development of this block is associated with the Clercs de Saint-Viateur, who in 1896 founded there a farm-school (ferme-école) for the deaf-mute on the former ferme Bouthillier-McDougall. It is a very homogeneous sector whose built fabric is composed essentially of residences. One finds there detached two-storey houses, generally of brick and with flat roofs, with a ground floor raised about one metre above ground level. L'église Saint-Viateur and l'académie Querbes complete the panorama of this neighbourhood.

16.E.5 Le secteur Saint-Viateur et Beaubien exceptional The sector was named in honour of the Beaubien family, which played a major role in the development of Outremont. It covers the former lands of the congregation of the Clercs de Saint-Viateur and of the Wiseman family. The built fabric of the Saint-Viateur et Beaubien sector is composed mainly of semi-detached single-family residences (résidences unifamiliales jumelées); a restricted choice of materials and colours makes it possible to preserve a certain uniformity, even though the residences are of great stylistic diversity. The residences are set on carefully landscaped grounds, and the mature trees found on the parcels contribute to the quality of the sector's built fabric. A few institutions worthy of mention are also noted, in particular the maison provinciale des Clercs de Saint-Viateur.

16.E.6 L'avenue Bernard exceptional This sector corresponds to avenue Bernard between rue Hutchison and avenue McEachran. The great majority (75%) of the buildings lining avenue Bernard were built in the 1920s, which accounts for the harmony of this very lively commercial street, especially during the summer season (sidewalk cafés). Conciergeries (apartment buildings) are the dominant architectural type of the sector: these multifamily buildings have three or four storeys, and their half-basements and ground floors are generally occupied by shops. Front setbacks are about 3 metres and the lots are most often paved. Note the presence on this artery of the cinéma Outremont, a building given municipal heritage status (cité) in 2000 and entirely restored.
Ce secteur correspond à l'avenue Bernard entre la rue Hutchison et l'avenue McEachran. La grande majorité (75 %) des bâtiments qui bordent l'avenue Bernard ont été construits dans les années 1920, ce qui explique l'harmonie qui se dégage de cette voie commerciale très animée, surtout durant la saison estivale (cafés-terrasses). Les conciergeries constituent le type architectural dominant de ce secteur. Ces immeubles multifamiliaux comptent trois ou quatre étages et leurs demi-sous-sols ainsi que leurs rez-de-chaussée sont généralement occupés par des commerces. Les marges de recul avant sont d'environ 3 mètres et les terrains sont le plus souvent recouverts de pavés. On notera la présence sur cette artère du cinéma Outremont, bâtiment cité en 2000 et qui a entièrement été restauré.

16.E.7 Le secteur centre exceptional This sector borders the industrial and working-class quarters, and this portion of Outremont's territory is marked by strong homogeneity, resting on the fact that most of its buildings were put up within a short span of time, between 1903 and 1920. The urban landscape is dominated by semi-detached single-family houses or semi-detached duplexes with independent entrances; there are also a certain number of detached single-family houses, particularly around parc Pratt. The sector's exceptional quality results from its visual coherence but also from the presence of well-laid-out parks (Joyce, Vimy and Pratt) as well as the plant cover that adorns the properties. The presence of collège Stanislas, a renowned teaching institution, is worth noting.
Ce secteur est limitrophe aux quartiers industriel et ouvrier. Cette portion du territoire d'Outremont se caractérise par une forte homogénéité, laquelle repose sur le fait que la majorité des immeubles se sont construits en un court laps de temps, entre 1903 et 1920. Le paysage urbain est dominé par des résidences unifamiliales jumelées ou des duplex jumelés aux accès indépendants. Il existe aussi un certain nombre de résidences unifamiliales isolées, particulièrement autour du parc Pratt. La qualité exceptionnelle de ce secteur résulte de sa cohérence visuelle mais aussi de la présence de parcs bien aménagés (Joyce, Vimy et Pratt) ainsi que du couvert végétal qui orne les propriétés. 16.E.8 Le cimetière Mont-Royal exceptional This garden-type cemetery, designed by Sidney and Neff in 1852, was declared a national historic site in 1997 by the federal government. The designation is explained by the quality of the site's landscaping and by the fact that several figures famous in Canada's history are buried there. It also contains a large number of exceptional monuments created by renowned artists, as well as several vaults belonging to prominent Montreal families, including those of the Molson family. Of note are the entrance gate, designed by the architect J.W. Hopkins in 1862, and the Gothic-influenced chapel located nearby. The cimetière Mont-Royal forms an integral part of the arrondissement historique et naturel du mont Royal.
Ce cimetière de type jardin, dessiné par Sidney et Neff en 1852, a été décrété lieu historique national en 1997 par le gouvernement fédéral. Cette désignation s'explique par la qualité de l'aménagement du site et le fait qu'y sont enterrés plusieurs personnages célèbres de l'histoire du Canada. On y trouve également un grand nombre de monuments exceptionnels réalisés par des artistes de renom, ainsi que plusieurs caveaux appartenant à des familles bien en vue de Montréal, dont ceux de la famille Molson. On notera le portail d'entrée, conçu par l'architecte J.W. Hopkins en 1862, et la chapelle d'influence gothique située à proximité. Le cimetière Mont-Royal fait partie intégrante de l'arrondissement historique et naturel du mont Royal.

16.I.4 Le quartier ouvrier interesting The built fabric of this sector expresses the more modest origins of its residents. It has a strong concentration of contiguous triplexes (one dwelling per floor) faced in brick and with terrace roofs (toit en terrasse), and these form continuous, harmonious alignments. It is worth noting that every block of the quartier ouvrier is served by back lanes (ruelles). Avenue Van Horne, the sector's commercial artery, is lined with multiple-dwelling buildings. The église Sainte-Madeleine and the école Lajoie are the sector's structuring elements.

1694–1875 · The Côte-Sainte-Catherine farms

1694 – 1875

The côte Sainte-Catherine is conceded from 1694 and farmed for a century and a half — a strip of long lots running up the north-west flank of Mount Royal, with the Montréal side of the mountain between it and the city. The municipality of Côte-Sainte-Catherine is created in 1855. Nothing residential survives from this phase in any quantity; it is the land division itself, and the côte road that still runs across the borough, that carried forward.

1875–1903 · Village and first subdivisions

1875 – 1903

The village of Outremont is detached on 23 February 1875 with about three hundred residents, on Louis Beaubien's initiative. It is anglophone by a large majority, semi-rural, and slow: the first subdivisions go in along the eastern edge nearest the city. The Clercs de Saint-Viateur arrive in 1887 and are followed by five more religious communities to 1920, whose properties become the structuring elements of the street grid. Outremont becomes a ville in 1895.

1903–1920 · The homogeneous build-out

1903 – 1920

The build-out. Between 1903 and 1920 the greater part of the north and east is constructed, in a very short span and by a small number of builders, which is the whole explanation for the homogeneity the 2005 evaluation singles out: rows of semi-detached single-family houses and paired duplexes with independent entrances, two storeys, brick, flat-roofed. Parc Outremont opens in 1903, the église Saint-Viateur is built in 1910–13, the first francophone mayor is elected in 1910, and the town becomes a cité in 1915. Detached single-family houses appear in the same idiom, particularly on the ground that would become parc Pratt.

1920–1940 · The mountain flank and the bourgeois houses

1920 – 1940

Édouard Gohier's Northmount Land Company had subdivided the mountain flank from 1906; through the 1920s and 1930s it fills with the résidences cossues — detached, large, on the steepest and most expensive land in the borough, and drawing on every revival style then in circulation as well as the first modern ones. Parc Pratt is created in 1931. The Théâtre Outremont opens in 1928 to René Charbonneau's design with a Briffa interior, and is today the borough's only classed heritage immovable.

1940–1975 · Apartment buildings

1940 – 1975

Apartment buildings arrive — walk-ups in Art Deco and Streamline Moderne dress, and later modern blocks — and the remaining gaps on the flank are filled with International Style, split-level and Art Deco houses that the 2005 evaluation reads as one continuous sequence with the historicist houses beside them. The Université de Montréal's expansion in 1961 cuts off what was left of the Northmount plan, and the borough is essentially complete. Outremont reverts to ville in 1970 and is merged into Montréal as a borough in 2002; the redevelopment of the Outremont rail yard as the MIL campus is the one large change since.

Notes on the approach

This page is built on territorial characterisation, not on a type catalogue, and three things about that should be said plainly.

The landscape units are real but not named here. The 2020 study by L'Enclume divides the borough into four aires and twenty-nine unités de paysage, and the PIIA annexes carry a table of characteristic elements for each unit. Neither the study nor the annexes could be retrieved for this part, so the six type records below are drawn from the 2005 Évaluation du patrimoine urbain alone and each carries a note saying so. When the unit tables are obtained they will supersede these records rather than supplement them.

The by-law number is unconfirmed. Legacy listings give Outremont's PIIA as by-law 1189; the 2020–21 consolidation appears to use an AO-### series, with AO-530 reported. Both are recorded in the instruments list with the uncertainty marked, and neither is asserted here.

The sector codes are the document's own. The fourteen sectors below are transcribed from the 2005 evaluation, which numbers them 16.E.1 to 16.E.8 (valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle) and 16.I.1 to 16.I.6 (valeur patrimoniale intéressante). The file as published by the Ville de Montréal is named with a 17_ prefix, which belongs to the series numbering of the evaluation volumes, not to the sectors; where the two disagree the codes printed inside the document have been followed.

Finally, unlike Gatineau, Outremont has no cited residential district. Protection runs through the PIIA and its list of témoins architecturaux significatifs, through the individual classement of the Théâtre Outremont, and through the site patrimonial du Mont-Royal, declared in 2005, which overlaps the borough's southern flank below avenue Maplewood.
