Residential Architectural Styles of Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie (borough of Montréal)

Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie is the borough the Canadian Pacific built, and the only place on this site with a house type its own occupants put up with their own hands. When the Angus Shops opened in 1904 — 3,000 men at start-up, 12,000 during each of the world wars, 31 principal buildings and 35 annexes, a fire station, a hospital, a sawmill and a bank of its own, une ville dans la ville — the promoters U.-H. Dandurand and Herbert Holt formed the Rosemont Land Improvement Company and cut the Crawford farm north of the shops into long blocks with lanes and lots all of one size. The 1907 Pinsoneault atlas, which the cahier reproduces, shows a single model repeated across the whole subdivision. What put a labourer on one of those lots was not a mortgage but a promesse de vente needing a few dollars down, and what he built on it was a one-storey flat-roofed box, small, cheap, made of whatever came to hand, with the roof left flat so that a second storey could go on top when he could afford it. Five hundred and sixty-one of those maisons shoebox are still standing, and since 2019 every one of them is named at its own address in an annexe to the zoning by-law. Around them the borough is the ordinary Montréal fabric — contiguous brick duplex and triplex with exterior stairs, denser and more ornate along rue Saint-Denis — with three later inflections: a co-operative garden suburb of detached houses inaugurated in 1942, two ensembles of semi-detached Art déco duplex with their garages at the back of the lot, and, at the eastern edge, 1950s bungalows that the cahier calls une ambiance de banlieue en ville.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

How buildings are graded

Not a borough-wide grading. Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie characterised every one of the 561 shoebox houses on its territory, one by one, and gave each a « valeur architecturale » of 1, 2 or 3, judged on the interest of the architectural components of the façade and on the context of the building's siting. THE SCALE RUNS THE OPPOSITE WAY TO EVERY OTHER GRADING ON THIS SITE: a house of value 1 shows the fewest characteristics of interest and a house of value 3 the most. The scale is legally operative rather than advisory. Article 5 of by-law 01-279 defines a « maison shoebox d'intérêt » as a house on the Annexe F list « ayant une valeur architecturale de 2 ou 3 », and that defined term is what triggers Chapitre VIII and PIIA fascicule 10. Article 135.1 then ties redevelopment rights to the same number: only usage categories H.1, H.2 and H.3 — one, two or three dwellings — are permitted on a shoebox lot, and where the house is value 3, only H.1. So the most interesting shoeboxes are worth the least to a redeveloper, which is the point.

Before 1904 · Côte de la Visitation: farms and quarries

1700 – 1904

The boulevard Rosemont follows the old chemin de la côte de la Visitation, opened to settlement by the Sulpicians early in the eighteenth century and farmed by the settlers' descendants into the twentieth. Limestone was known on the côte from 1780 and worked for building stone — Montréal's grey stone — and later for the crushed rock that surfaced the roads until the middle of the twentieth century. The winding rue des Carrières, which led to the quarries, is the only surviving witness and the borough's one tracé fondateur d'intérêt patrimonial; its line still breaks the orthogonal grid. Nothing residential survives from the period.

1885–1930 · La Petite-Patrie and the tramway

1885 – 1930

La Petite-Patrie, named for Claude Jasmin's autobiographical novel, took its urban structure between 1885 and 1930 with the tramway. Francophones and Italian Montrealers settled the southern part from 1895, the latter keeping kitchen gardens and poultry; Notre-Dame-de-la-Défense followed early in the new century and Casa Italia decades later. The fabric is contiguous brick plex of one to three storeys, mostly duplex and triplex, with flat roofs, exterior stairs, balconies, decorative couronnements and artificial-stone inserts, and serially produced ornament repeated down each row. Rue Saint-Denis got the expensive version — grey stone on the façade more often, more refined detail, stained glass — and the cahier rates that street a sector of exceptional value on the strength of its plex alone. In 1931 the marché du Nord, now Jean-Talon, was built on the old Shamrock lacrosse grounds as Depression relief work and became the busiest market in Montréal.

Three flats stacked behind one brick front, the upper two reached by a stair climbing the outside, balconies at each level, a parapet or a run of brickwork finishing the roofline. Where it comes from

La Petite-Patrie's fabric took shape between 1885 and 1930 with the arrival of the tramway; the Rosemont side followed the Angus Shops from 1904, on land the Rosemont Land Improvement Company had cut into regular blocks with lanes. During the 1920s Rosemont was among the most active Montréal quarters for house-building. The exterior stair itself is not explained in this cahier; the mechanism — the mandatory front setback pushing the stair to the upper dwellings outside the building — is set out in the Sud-Ouest synthèse and is treated once, elsewhere on this site, rather than repeated in every borough.

1904–1940 · The Angus Shops and the self-built suburb

1904 – 1940

The Angus period, and the borough's own type. The shops drew thousands of workers to cheap peripheral lots, and the tramway and the growing road network meant a man no longer had to live beside the factory that employed him. The 1920s made Rosemont one of the most active Montréal quarters for house-building. The shoebox belongs to the beginning of that span and kept being built, through several phases, into the 1960s. Maison shoebox Maison de type « shoebox »

Style: Shoebox / Montréal faubourg vernacular
· 561 recorded in Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie (borough of Montréal)
source ›

A one-storey brick box, its short end to the street, a door in the middle with a window on each side, a cornice or parapet across the top, and a flat roof that was flat on purpose — so that the owner could add a floor when he had the money. Five hundred and sixty-one survive in Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie, more than in any other borough, and since 2019 each of them is named at its own address in an annexe to the zoning by-law and carries an architectural value of 1, 2 or 3. It is the only house on this site that its occupants built themselves.

Where it comes from

The Canadian Pacific opened the Angus Shops in 1904, and the promoters U.-H. Dandurand and Herbert Holt formed the Rosemont Land Improvement Company and subdivided the Crawford farm to the north of them. The cahier records what the 1907 Pinsoneault atlas shows: long blocks with lanes and lots all of one size, a single model. What let labourers onto those lots was not a mortgage but the promettant-acquéreur — a promise of sale from a land company needing only a few dollars down, which is the mechanism Guy Gaudreau documents for the first Rosemont and Villeray shoeboxes. A journalier could take a lot on those terms and build a small flat-roofed house on it with his own hands, out of whatever materials were available, and add to it later. Everything characteristic of the type follows from that: the small footprint, the perpendicular plan that wastes no street frontage, the plain brick front, and above all the flat roof, which is not a style but an unbuilt second storey.

Two things happen at once. The plex after the car: two flats behind a brick front with a flat roof, but paired instead of contiguous, with a driveway between each pair running back to a garage at the end of the lot, and the front door under a wrought-iron marquise on chains. Built by single promoters in the 1940s and 1950s in two ensembles urbains that the cahier singles out for their uniformity.

Where it comes from

Rosemont kept growing after 1945 on the post-war demographic boom and the housing shortage, and the cahier says whole streets went up at once. These two sectors are that process caught in a form: too uniform to be the work of many builders, still built as stacked dwellings in brick with a flat roof, but now paired and served by a driveway, with the stairwell brought back inside and lit through glass block. The vocabulary is Art déco; the plan is a plex adjusting to the suburb.

After 1960 · Closure

1960 – 2019

Angus declines through the 1960s and 1970s and closes for good in 1992; the site is split between a residential and an industrial redevelopment, and three buildings survive. Nouveau Rosemont fills in with 1950s bungalows. Then the pressure the 2004 cahier had already named — la tendance est à la densification des quartiers, ce qui met en péril l'existence des petites maisons d'un seul étage qui témoignent d'un mode d'occupation du territoire en voie de disparition — arrives in the land market. In May 2018 the borough council votes a moratorium on new demolition applications for shoebox houses. In December 2018 it publishes Annexe F, an address-by-address characterisation of all 561. On 12 February 2019 it adopts by-law 01-279-58 in third and final reading, the first Montréal borough to protect the type. Owners in thirty-nine zones force signature registers on the contested provisions; the borough carries them zone by zone in thirty-nine separate by-laws, 01-279-58-01 to 01-279-58-39, and on 27 March 2019 the whole territory is covered.

Where it comes from

The eastern part of the borough — what the cahier calls Nouveau Rosemont — developed late, after the Second World War, when large lots were still available and the population of the east end was rising fast. This sector is the 1950s result, and the cahier reads it as an import: bungalows « implantés dans un milieu urbain comprenant en majorité des duplex ». It is the far end of the borough's sequence, and the exact opposite of the shoebox at the other end — the same one-storey house, but detached, on a lawn, with a hipped roof and no intention of ever growing upward.

Notes on the approach

The arrondissement code is 26, read from inside the cahier. The file is 21_evaluation_patrimoine_rose.pdf and every sector code inside it reads 26. Its PDF Title metadata still says "maq1_CŽte StLuc Hampstead", left over from the template. Two independent reasons, in one document, never to infer a code from a filename. The cahier also cites 14.AP.1, which confirms that Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension is code 14.

The E-sector list was flagged incomplete and was. The Part 10 memo carried three; the cahier carries eleven, 26.E.1 to 26.E.11, plus seven I sectors, four U ensembles urbains, one N ensemble industriel and three AP archaeological sectors — twenty-six coded records, all of them enumerated in sectors.yaml. The cahier's one tracé fondateur d'intérêt patrimonial, the rue des Carrières, is described in the phase text above but is deliberately not encoded as a sector: it is a route rather than a sector, it sits outside the E/I/N/U/AP grammar in the cahier's own structure, it carries no code, and none of the sector values this site defines describes it without saying something false about it.

The grading counts were corrected against the primary document. The research memo gave the three architectural-value classes as 69 / 258 / 235, which sums to 562. Parsing Annexe F itself, row by row, gives 561 rows and 170 / 233 / 158 — exactly the distribution the Ville de Montréal's own article page publishes as 30.3 %, 41.5 % and 28.2 %. The memo's other Annexe F figure is confirmed exactly: 56 houses, just under ten per cent, are built en fonds de lot. Two further Annexe F columns that no summary mentions are also counted here: 118 houses carry the mention couronnement de qualité, and 232 stand next to another shoebox.

The value scale runs the opposite way to every other grading on this site. Westmount's I* is its best and its III its plainest; Île d'Orléans runs A to E, best first; Lévis and Terrebonne likewise. Rosemont's runs 1 to 3 with 3 the best — une maison ayant une valeur de 1 présente le moins de caractéristiques architecturales d'intérêt. Anyone reading two of these pages side by side will invert the scale without noticing, so it is stated here and again in the grading block itself.

The scale is not advisory. Article 5 of by-law 01-279 defines a maison shoebox d'intérêt as a house on the Annexe F list with an architectural value of 2 or 3, and that defined term is what triggers Chapitre VIII and PIIA fascicule 10. Article 135.1 then attaches development rights to the same number: only usage categories H.1 to H.3 are permitted on a shoebox lot — one, two or three dwellings — and where the house is value 3, only H.1. The most interesting shoeboxes are therefore worth the least to a redeveloper, which is the mechanism, not a side effect.

Shoebox houses have no status under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel. None is classed, none is cited, none sits in a declared or classed heritage site. Everything protecting them is municipal and lives in a zoning by-law: a defined term in article 5, an address list in Annexe F, dimensional rules in articles 11.1, 26.1 and 52.1, a compulsory couronnement in article 100.1, a usage cap in article 135.1, a fascicule of PIIA criteria, and a demolition by-law. Compare Mercier–Hochelaga-Maisonneuve, where the ancienne Cité-de-Maisonneuve carries a provincial classement: same city, opposite instrument. It is also worth saying that the borough had to invent this. The 2004 cahier saw the threat and named it, but the Évaluation series had no mechanism for protecting a building type as such — only sectors and individually listed buildings — and the shoebox is neither concentrated enough to be a sector nor grand enough to be listed.

Why the regulation exists, in one transaction. In September 2019 La Presse reported a couple who had paid $500,000 for an uninhabited shoebox in Rosemont in order to demolish it and build a single-family house of more than $700,000 in its place; the project reached roughly $1.3 million, with the excavation alone rising from $15,000 to $45,000 once 800 holes had to be drilled to weaken the rock. Nothing on this site illustrates more plainly what type-level regulation is for: the house was worth nothing and the lot was worth half a million, and no grading of individual buildings by architectural merit would have caught it, because the building was never the point.

Two figures that are press, not municipal. The island-wide count — around a thousand shoebox houses in Montréal, of which about half are in Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie — comes from La Presse of 19 October 2018 and is reported here as press. The municipal record supports only the borough figure, 561, and that one is exact because it is a list of addresses. The $500,000 / $700,000 transaction above is likewise press. No commercial real-estate site has been used for any date or count on this page.

One type, or two? Rosemont treats the shoebox as a single type spanning the early twentieth century to the 1960s. The adjoining borough of Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension splits the same object into a shoebox du début du 20e siècle and a shoebox du milieu du 20e siècle, and proposes calling it a uniplex instead — a single-family member of the plex family. Two boroughs of one city, working from the same building stock in the same years, disagree about whether this is one type or two and about what to call it. The disagreement is kept visible rather than resolved.
