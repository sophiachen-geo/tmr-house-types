Residential Architectural Styles of Ville-Marie (Montréal)

Ville-Marie is where Montréal started and where the argument about what is worth keeping was had. The borough holds the declared site patrimonial de Montréal — the Vieux-Montréal, about 1 400 metres east to west and 800 north to south, roughly a square kilometre, with 557 édifices et vestiges built between the seventeenth century and the twentieth — and it holds, four hundred metres uphill, the Mille carré doré, the richest domestic architecture on the island of Montréal, which carries no ensemble protection of any kind. It also holds the borough's own answer to the ruelle, which is a carriage gate driven through the front of a workers' house; a warehouse district that was emptied and then recycled into flats; and a stretch of downtown built by knocking the previous one down.

There is no per-type document for this territory. The Ville's Évaluation du patrimoine urbain of 2005 grades territory, not buildings: 67 secteurs de valeur patrimoniale exceptionnelle, 19 secteurs de valeur patrimoniale intéressante, one ensemble urbain d'intérêt, two ensembles industriels d'intérêt and six secteurs d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel. The ministerial plan de conservation that would describe the declared site's buildings has never been produced. What is left is one unusually explicit paragraph in the provincial register, and it is worth quoting whole, because three of the six type records on this page are lifted from it: « Les immeubles les plus anciens - habitations et édifices conventuels - qui datent de l'époque de la Nouvelle-France présentent une maçonnerie en pierre et des toits à pignon. Les nombreux édifices d'inspiration classique du Régime britannique sont caractérisés, entre autres, par la pierre calcaire grise. L'architecture éclectique du XIXe siècle s'observe dans les édifices à bureaux, banques, sièges sociaux de compagnies d'assurances et palais de justice. » The RPCQ adds a fourth sentence about the twentieth century — « Le site compte enfin quelques bâtiments d'intérêt du XXe siècle, notamment des édifices art déco, rationalistes et fonctionnalistes » — and then, in its list of characteristic elements, the detail that turns each of those sentences into a building: the roof pitch and the firebreak walls, the cut stone and the orders, the mixed structure of timber, cast iron and stone.

The word Golden in Golden Square Mile has two competing origins in circulation and this site asserts neither. English Wikipedia says the addition was coined by the Montréal journalist Charlie Lazarus, and attaches no citation to the claim. Tourisme Montréal is reported as saying it was added in the 1950s by real-estate developers, and Héritage Montréal's own blog says journalists of the 1950s embellished the name. The sources agree on the decade and on nothing else. What is certain is that the name in use before that was simply the Square Mile, or, before the 1930s, New Town or Uptown.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

1642 – 1760

The walled town. Ville-Marie was founded in 1642 as a missionary settlement by Paul de Chomedey de Maisonneuve, on ground the RPCQ records as frequented by Indigenous peoples since prehistory and holding archaeological evidence of three thousand years of settlement. The Sulpicians became seigneurs of the island in 1663; their superior François Dollier de Casson traced the first streets in 1672 with the notary-surveyor Bénigne Basset Des Lauriers — three streets parallel to the river, seven perpendicular — and rue Notre-Dame, opened that year on the crest of the coteau, is still there. 1760–1840 · British regime — classical stone

1760 – 1840

The fortifications came down between 1801 and 1817 and three commissioners were appointed to plan the ground they freed: a terrace along the river, an enlarged Champ-de-Mars, wider streets, and the three arteries — rues de la Commune, Saint-Antoine and McGill — that still bound the old town on three sides. Montréal became the principal city of the two Canadas and, from 1844 to 1849, the capital of the united province; import-export merchants replaced the fur magnates, and, in the RPCQ's line, the wooden houses of the artisans gave way to neoclassical stone. Grey limestone predominated until 1850. The faubourgs spread outward through the gap the walls had left — Saint-Laurent to the north, Québec and Sainte-Marie to the east, Saint-Antoine and the Récollets to the west — and the streets that had been the town's exits became its main roads.

Where it comes from

From 1800 Montréal became the principal city of the two Canadas. The political upheavals of the 18th century, the Conquest and American independence among them, drove population growth and immigration, and dynamic import-export merchants replaced the magnates of the fur trade. Grey limestone stayed the predominant material until 1850. The two great fires of 9 and 10 July 1852, which destroyed some 1 200 dwellings and left about 10 000 people homeless, ended wooden construction in the city outright and made stone the rule rather than the preference.

Where it comes from

Montréal became the undisputed metropolis of Canada in the second half of the 19th century, at the centre of a road, maritime and transcontinental rail network, and the old town turned into a financial and administrative city — the shop window of Canadian capitalism, in the RPCQ's phrase. Where it comes from

1930–1975 · Decline, demolition and the towers

1930 – 1975

Decline, then demolition. After 1945 Montréal lost the metropolitan role to Toronto, the business district moved north-west, and the old town, in the cahier's phrase, was no more than a shadow of itself: retail practically eliminated, residents down to a few hundred. Rue Dorchester was widened in 1954–55 and the modern city was built along it — Hydro-Québec's head office, the Reine Elizabeth in 1957–58, Place Ville-Marie in 1959, the CIL and CIBC towers, then the métro in 1966. Serious proposals were tabled for an expressway through Vieux-Montréal. Uphill, the mansions came down one at a time: Hugh Allan in 1943, Ogilvie 1944, Workman 1952, R. G. Reid 1956, Fred Molson and Richard B. Angus 1957, Hector Mackenzie 1960, and, on 8 September 1973, Van Horne. In Milton Park a single developer had bought 96 % of the properties and demolished 255 of them.

1964–2026 · Repopulation — Les Cours Le Royer to the co-ops

1964 – 2026

Repopulation. In 1964 the Québec government recognised the historical and architectural importance of the Vieux-Montréal and decreed it an arrondissement historique, which — the cahier's own words — is how it became a distinct urban reality. Survival then meant finding uses for empty floors, and the cahier records the mechanism and its first instance: « On y a recyclé plusieurs de ces bâtiments en condos, dont le premier exemple est celui des Cours Le Royer, posant ainsi le premier jalon d'un repeuplement du Vieux-Montréal. » Several thousand people live there now. Five hundred metres from where the Van Horne mansion stood, and ten years after it fell, the largest co-operative housing project in North America was finished in Milton Park. The declared site's limits were enlarged in 1995 to take in the old fortifications; certain powers over it passed to the Ville de Montréal in 2018; and in 2017 six blocks of the Mille carré doré were classed as the site patrimonial du Complexe-de-La-Maison-Alcan, which remains the only ensemble protection that quarter has.

Notes on the approach

(a) One arrondissement, two numbers, and the second one belongs to somebody else. Part 10 established that the number in a Montréal sector code is not the number in the cahier's filename. Ville-Marie extends that finding from code ≠ filename to code ≠ code, and this page reports what was verified rather than what was expected. Inside the 2005 cahier, every sector code printed beside every sector name begins 24: 24.E.1 to 24.E.67, 24.I.1 to 24.I.19, 24.U.1, 24.N.1–2, 24.AP.1–6, extracted from the PDF's own text. The cahier's filename begins 25. And the Ville's Plan d'urbanisme heritage sheet of January 2016 heads its list « 25 Arrondissement de Ville-Marie » — so the Plan d'urbanisme numbers this territory 25, agreeing with the filename and not with the codes. Two further things were checked and neither is in the brief. First, the 2016 map prints no sector codes at all; it is a legend map, so it cannot be the origin of a 25.E.n code for this territory. Second, and this is the sharp part: 25 in the 2005 series is Le Plateau-Mont-Royal, whose own cahier — published as file 11 — prints 25.E.1 to 25.E.15 and 25.I.1 to 25.I.3. The number the Plan d'urbanisme gives Ville-Marie is, in the sector series, the borough next door's. This site renders 24 as the primary code, because it is the one printed beside the sector names, and stores both.

(b) The Mille carré doré has no ensemble protection, and Pointe-Claire's 2.7 hectares do. Sectors 24.E.29 and 24.E.30 are graded exceptionnelle in the Ville's own document and hold, by common consent, the finest domestic architecture on the island. Héritage Montréal states the position without hedging: the Square Mile heritage ensemble "does not have a designation or specific strategy to protect and promote its heritage over current urban development considerations, such as urban planning parameters or building standards", and adds that the interiors, often of exceptional value, the gardens and the ancillary buildings enjoy no recognition at all — including in most of the buildings that have been classed, cited or taken into the Mount Royal heritage site, which reaches only the portion north of avenue du Docteur-Penfield. What has already been lost is on the record: English Wikipedia, citing Rémillard and Merrett's Mansions of the Golden Square Mile (1987), states that "By 1983, only 30% of the mansions in the northern half of the Square Mile had survived demolition; and only 5% survived south of Sherbrooke Street." Héritage Montréal's own page carries a more cautious version — "Only about 30% of the bourgeois homes survived, north of Sherbrooke Street" — and gives neither the year nor the southern figure; the fuller sentence is Wikipedia's, not Héritage Montréal's, and this site attributes it accordingly. Now set that against Pointe-Claire, in Part 11's West Island group: an irregular polygon of about 2.7 hectares on a point in Lac Saint-Louis, holding a church, a presbytery, a sacristan's house, a school, a former convent and a windmill, cité a site patrimonial by the municipality on 2 April 2013 after an avis de motion of 3 December 2012. Six blocks of the Mille carré doré are classed, as the Complexe-de-La-Maison-Alcan, and they are the exception that proves the rule. Designation tracks legibility, jurisdiction and local politics. It does not track architectural quality.

(c) Van Horne, and the two steps that are usually collapsed into one. The common shorthand — that the demolition of the Van Horne mansion founded Héritage Montréal — is wrong, and the correct sequence is more interesting. On 7 September 1973 the Ville de Montréal issued a demolition permit for the former residence of the Canadian Pacific executive William Cornelius Van Horne, at the corner of rues Sherbrooke and Stanley. Dismantling of the interiors began the same day. On 8 September 1973, a Saturday, the house came down. One month later, twenty-three citizens' groups joined to found Sauvons Montréal, a federation for the preservation of the region's housing and community heritage. Héritage Montréal was incorporated on 15 October 1975, with Phyllis Lambert as its first president. Three separate events over two years, not one. The government that had refused to classify the house then amended article 426 of the Cities and Towns Act and article 392 of the Municipal Code so that a demolition permit could be delayed up to a year where classification had been requested, and it began protecting the neighbours: the Shaughnessy house in November 1973, the Louis-Joseph Forget, Lord Atholstan, James Reid Wilson and Ernest Cormier houses, the Grey Nuns chapel and the fort towers in 1974, the Mount Royal and Mount Stephen clubs in 1975, the Grey Nuns motherhouse in 1976. Two details are disputed between sources and are left disputed here. The house's date: Héritage Montréal and the Encyclopédie du patrimoine culturel de l'Amérique française both say 1870, enlarged 1890; English Wikipedia says completed 1869. Its architect: Wikipedia names John William Hopkins and Daniel Berkley Wily; the Encyclopédie says the architect is unknown and is only often supposed to have been John W. Hopkins. And the founding of Sauvons Montréal: Héritage Montréal says twenty-three groups a month after the demolition, the Encyclopédie says October 1973 with about a dozen groups growing to twenty and then past thirty, English Wikipedia gives 28 September 1973.

(d) Milton-Parc, the counter-model — five hundred metres away and ten years later. Between 1958 and 1968 Concordia Estates Ltd bought, through intermediaries, 96 % of the properties in Milton Park, for a residential megaproject. Residents formed the Milton Parc Citizens Committee in November 1968; a demonstration on 26 May 1972 ended in 56 arrests; 255 properties were demolished for La Cité, of which only the first phase was ever built. In 1978 the Committee asked Héritage Montréal for help buying the remaining blocks, by then held by a company called Paxmill, and Héritage Montréal went to the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation. On 16 May 1979 CMHC bought the Paxmill blocks for $5.5 million — 723 residential units and 14 stores — with the express object of helping the residents set up co-operatives. Phyllis Lambert met the CMHC directors to persuade them. Backed by Héritage Montréal and Lambert, the residents then built the largest co-operative housing project in North America: the CCA records that "the Milton Parc housing project (1979-1982) renovated over 135 historic buildings and 597 dwelling units within Milton Parc's 6-block radius." A private bill of the Québec National Assembly created the governance that holds it — the co-operatives and non-profits own the land, common areas are held by a syndicate, and the declaration of co-ownership carries restrictions on social responsibility and non-speculation. Demolition and rehabilitation, five hundred metres apart, ten years apart, in the same city and largely by the same organisation. Three details in circulation do not agree and are recorded rather than resolved. Héritage Montréal dates the National Assembly's passage of the private bill to 23 June 1987. The Communauté Milton Parc's own À propos page is reported as dating its creation to December 1987, following the adoption of private bill 224, published as chapter 144 of the annual statutes of 1987, and as counting 616 dwellings in 146 residential and two commercial buildings, more than 1 000 low- and moderate-income residents now against more than 1 500 at its creation, and 16 member housing co-operatives with 6 housing societies rather than the fifteen and six the brief for this part gives — but that page returned HTTP 500 on every attempt on 17 August 2026 and the Internet Archive could not be reached from here, so those figures are reported at second hand from a search index and are not verified first-hand on this site. The bill number, the December 1987 date and the counts should be checked against the statute before they are relied on. One thing is not a discrepancy but a correction: Milton Park is not in Ville-Marie. It lies north of rue Sherbrooke, in Le Plateau-Mont-Royal, where the 2005 cahier codes it 25.E.12 Mont Sainte-Famille. It appears in the sector table above as the one cross-boundary row, marked as such.

(e) Vieux-Montréal stays a sector, and that is the point. Part 9 set a rule for promoting a sector to a place of its own on this site: it is not enough for the territory to carry a status — a distinct per-type source has to exist for it, or the new page merely repeats the old one. Charlesbourg passed that test. Vieux-Montréal has the same shape — a provincially declared site inside a borough, with its own boundaries, its own name and 557 buildings — and it fails, because the source does not exist. The ministerial plan de conservation was not retrieved, and on the evidence gathered here it has not been published: the Conseil du patrimoine culturel's own page for the site carries no plan, no règlement and no directive, only a link to the register entry, and the ministry's former plans-de-conservation index now redirects to a generic landing page. No post-2021 règlement or directive replacing it was found either. Without a per-type document there is nothing for a Vieux-Montréal page to say that this page does not already say from the RPCQ fiche and the cahier. So it stays sector SITE-93528 in the table above, with the twelve cahier sectors it covers — 24.E.36 to 24.E.47 — listed beneath it. This is the promotion rule's first negative case, and a rule that promotes everything is not a rule.

What this page does not have. Six of the eighty-nine sector rows carry a full transcription and about twenty more carry a summary; the rest carry the name printed in the cahier and nothing else, and say so. The two ensembles industriels d'intérêt and the six archaeological-potential sectors are counted but not encoded, because this site's sector vocabulary has no value for an ensemble industriel and no AP fiche was transcribed here. The Ville-Marie règlement d'urbanisme 01-282 was not retrieved, so its article numbers are absent. The 2020 plan de conservation the brief attributes to the Complexe-de-La-Maison-Alcan could not be verified; the classement itself was, from the register. Every empty cell in the type tables below is a source's silence, not an omission — and in the case of the Mille carré doré, the emptiest table on this page belongs to the best architecture, which is the argument of the whole part in one row.
