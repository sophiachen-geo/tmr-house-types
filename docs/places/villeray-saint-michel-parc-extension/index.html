Residential Architectural Styles of Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension

Three settlements became one borough by annexation, and none of them began as a plan. Villeray was made by a tramway: the Montreal Park and Island Railway ran an electric line north through the fields in 1893 along what is now rue Lajeunesse, land promoters subdivided either side of it, and the people who moved in were, in the cahier's phrase, surtout composée d'ouvriers-locataires. The village was erected in 1896, had 800 inhabitants when Montréal annexed it in 1905, and then filled very fast — a housing boom between 1925 and 1931 covering everything west of rue Saint-Hubert. Parc-Extension was a speculation at the end of avenue du Parc, mounted by promoters competing with the men building the model city next door at Ville Mont-Royal; lots went on sale in 1912 and the buildings came decades later. Ville Saint-Michel was a quarry hamlet on the côte Saint-Michel with lime kilns Pehr Kalm noticed in 1749; it was given a garden-city street plan in 1912, on the model Ebenezer Howard had published in 1898, and did not build it out until after 1945.

What holds the borough together is not its history but its by-law. Règlement 01-283-124, Annexe F sets out thirteen residential types over 109 pages, in three families — seven single-family houses, three plex, three apartment buildings — and gives each of them the same eight-page treatment: a page of historical context, an elevation, a description under three headings, a page or two of lettered variants each with its own elevation, and three pages tabulating the original components and the replacement components a permit may allow. No other borough on this site regulates at that grain. Several of the variants name the zones they apply in, and one of them, variant B of the post-war house, sets a different list of permitted claddings for zone H02-096 than for zones H03-081, H03-085, H03-125 and H03-131. That is a typology annexe operating as a material schedule keyed to a map.

The 2005 Évaluation du patrimoine urbain cahier sits under all of it and does something different: it grades territory. It publishes one exceptional sector, five interesting ones, three ensembles urbains d'intérêt and two archaeological records, and it is candid about how modest the material is — Son bâti date principalement de la première moitié du XXe siècle. Les bâtiments et secteurs d'intérêt patrimonial sont donc plutôt récents et modestes. The single exceptional sector is a street, rue Saint-Denis, and it is exceptional for its plex. Fifteen years later the by-law answered it directly by naming a type variant after it: plex de la rue Saint-Denis, the only variant in the whole annexe named after a street.

What protects these buildings

The instruments that actually govern change here, in the order the place record lists them. A design-review programme (PIIA) judges appearance and integration; a classement, citation or declaration protects a named building or a defined territory under the Loi sur le patrimoine culturel; an inventory records without protecting. They are not interchangeable, and a place can have several or none.

14.E.1 Rue Saint-Denis exceptional The borough's only exceptional sector, and the only one anywhere in this record that is a street rather than an area. Rue Saint-Denis was built out through the 1920s as Villeray urbanised, and although it now carries shops and offices this stretch was never the commercial street — that was rue Saint-Hubert. What it was instead was the address: the cahier calls it a voie de prestige because its buildings are bigger than those on the streets around it, above all the large three-storey plex. They form near-continuous runs, matched in gabarit, in brick cladding, in the unbroken line of the ground floor above the pavement, in their saillies, and in the rhythm, proportion and crowning of their openings. The by-law answers this sector directly: variant A of fiche 2.1 is called « plex de la rue Saint-Denis », the only variant in the whole annexe named after a street.
« Dans Villeray, la rue Saint-Denis est en quelque sorte une voie de prestige car elle comporte un bâti plus imposant que les rues avoisinantes, avec surtout des grands « plex » de trois étages. Ces bâtiments composent des suites relativement continues avec leurs gabarits semblables, leurs revêtements de brique, le niveau continu de leur rez-de-chaussée par rapport au trottoir, leurs saillies, les rythmes et les proportions de leurs ouvertures, ainsi que leurs couronnements. »

14.I.1 Saint-Roch interesting Parc-Extension had no village core to grow from. What it had instead was a property development mounted by promoters competing with the ones building the cité-modèle next door at Ville Mont-Royal. The cahier is precise about the timing, and it matters: lots went on sale in 1912, but real urbanisation came very much later. The oldest houses stand around the église Saint-Roch of 1927, and the building of the écoles Saint-Roch and Barclay created the institutional nucleus.
« Il n'y a pas de véritable cœur villageois à l'origine du quartier de Parc Extension, mais plutôt un développement immobilier de promoteurs concurrents de ceux qui tentent de développer la cité-modèle (Ville Mont-Royal). Sur le territoire de Parc Extension, les lots sont à vendre dès 1912, mais il faudra attendre beaucoup plus tard pour voir une véritable urbanisation. »
The cahier's « dès 1912 » is the one firm municipal date for the sale of Parc-Extension lots. The Part 10 research memo dates the "Park Avenue Extension" lots to 1907 and attributes them to Park Realty; neither the year nor the company appears in this cahier, which names only the development project — « le projet de développement connu sous le nom de Park Avenue Extension ». Both claims are recorded on the place page; only 1912 is sourced here.

14.I.3 Villeray interesting The largest of the interesting sectors and the one that explains the borough's plex. Villeray was born of the electric tramway the Montreal Park and Island Railway ran through in 1893; the village was erected in 1896 and annexed to Montréal in 1905, and annexation set off the urbanisation of everything west of rue Saint-Hubert, with a housing construction boom between 1925 and 1931. The cahier calls it a model working-class quarter whose plex « illustraient le triomphe de la modernité », and says this sector now holds the borough's greatest concentration of turn-of-the-century buildings. Traces of the old village survive mainly on rue Saint-Gérard south of rue Villeray. The cahier's own illustration for the sector is avenue Henri-Julien, « bordée de "plex" à deux ou trois étages ».
« L'annexion du village à la ville de Montréal en 1905 donne le coup d'envoi de l'urbanisation du secteur situé à l'ouest de la rue Saint-Hubert, avec un boom de construction domiciliaire entre 1925 et 1931. C'était à l'époque un quartier ouvrier modèle. Les « plex » qu'on y construisait illustraient le triomphe de la modernité. Aujourd'hui, ce secteur regroupe la plus grande concentration de bâtiments du début du siècle dans l'arrondissement. »
 rue Saint-Gérard, rue Villeray, avenue Henri-Julien, rue Saint-Hubert
14.I.4 Saint-Barthélémy interesting A parish ensemble on rue Jean-Talon Est — the modern church of 1957, its attached presbytery, the earlier temporary brick church and the school. The parish was erected in 1928. The cahier's argument for it is social rather than architectural: a grouping of high-quality community buildings of this kind draws the population's attachment and makes the focal point of the quarter.

14.I.5 Rousselot – Tillemont interesting Built in 1951 on Wartime Housing Limited models. The cahier gives the mechanism plainly: the model was developed during the Second World War to answer the housing crisis of the day, and was then taken up by developers chasing buyers who wanted a detached house at an affordable price. Here the houses are one and a half storeys, faced in masonry, with a porch at the centre of the principal façade — the borough's clearest built statement of by-law fiche 1.5, maison d'après-guerre.
« Ces ensembles ont été construits en 1951 sur le modèle des maisons de la Wartime Housing Limited. […] Dans cet ensemble, les maisons d'un étage et demi sont recouvertes de maçonnerie et ont un porche au centre de la façade principale. »

14.U.1 Ensembles de type Wartime urban-ensemble The borough's largest urban ensemble and the one the Part 10 brief flags as its Wartime Housing Limited model sector — groups built through the 1950s by various promoters, sometimes under federal auspices, on Wartime Housing Limited models. What makes them an ensemble rather than a set of houses is stated as a rule: each group holds very strong characteristics in common as regards setbacks, massing, projections and even claddings. The cahier's illustration is 23e Avenue, in Saint-Michel. The Évaluation series created the ensembles urbains d'intérêt category expressly to catch this kind of fabric — post-war residential development, the heritage of the former suburbs, whose identification it calls exploratory.
« Il s'agit d'ensembles construits au cours des années 1950 par différents promoteurs, parfois sous l'égide du gouvernement fédéral, sur le modèle des maisons de la Wartime Housing Limited. […] Chacun de ces ensembles présente donc des caractéristiques communes très fortes à l'égard des reculs, de la volumétrie, des saillies et même des revêtements. »
The category's own definition, from the cahier's § 1.2.2: « Le Plan d'urbanisme reconnaît l'intérêt de certains développements résidentiels d'après-guerre, qui composent une part importante du patrimoine des anciennes banlieues, en créant une catégorie appelée Ensembles urbains d'intérêt. On y retrouve, entre autres, des ensembles homogènes de bungalows, des secteurs de maisons qui adoptent le modèle développé par la Wartime Housing Limited ou des exemples des premières coopératives d'habitation. » The three selection criteria are: a group representative of the new ways of living that appeared during the Second World War; a group representative of the post-war demographic boom and suburban development; and planned groups showing a certain homogeneity of architectural characteristics.
 23e Avenue

14.AP.1 Carrière de calcaire et fours à chaux archaeological-potential A secteur d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel. The Saint-Michel limestone quarries were worked from the 18th century for building stone and for the lime that made mortar; Pehr Kalm recorded two lime kilns along the côte Saint-Michel on his 1749 journey without giving their exact position. The kilns were built of fire-hardened stone, lined with granite, and could stand close to six metres. The Limoges quarry and kilns north-east of the Saint-Michel village worked from 1879, and the quality of Limoges lime was renowned among Montréal masons. Extraction continued to about 1930, by a technique of breaking the stone with a sledge and carting it out by horse-drawn tombereau; the kilns were moved about 1883 to near the corner of avenue Papineau and rue Jarry. The cahier records that no archaeological research has ever been carried out on the territory of this borough, which is why it holds no recorded archaeological site at all.
« Les carrières de calcaire de Saint-Michel sont exploitées dès le XVIIIe siècle; elles fournissent de la pierre de construction et la chaux pour la fabrication du mortier. »
Rendered as an archaeological overlay, not as a heritage sector. The cahier's own definition of the class: « Le secteur d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel est un territoire dont l'évaluation du contexte biophysique ainsi que des caractéristiques géomorphologiques, archéologiques et historiques démontre une forte possibilité qu'il recèle des vestiges archéologiques. […] Le secteur d'intérêt archéologique "à fort potentiel" est un territoire où des mesures de protection des sites éventuels sont nécessaires. »
 rue Jarry, avenue Papineau
14.A.1 Ancien village de Villeray archaeological-potential The site of the village of Villeray, which came into being through the electric tramway the Montreal Park and Island Railway laid in 1893 and was erected as a municipality in 1896. A few traces of the old village remain above ground, mainly on rue Saint-Gérard south of rue Villeray.
« Le village de Villeray est né grâce au tramway électrique implanté en 1893 par la Montreal Park and Island Railway. En 1896, Villeray devient officiellement municipalité. Quelques traces de l'ancien village sont toujours présentes, principalement sur la rue Saint-Gérard, au sud de la rue Villeray. »
The cahier prints this code with a bare A, not AP — it is a « secteur d'intérêt archéologique » under heading B, one rank below the « secteur d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel » of heading A. The distinction is substantive: for the plain-A class the cahier says further study is needed before the probability of finding remains can be estimated, whereas for the AP class it says protective measures are necessary. This site's schema carries a single archaeological value, so both are filed as archaeological-potential and the difference is recorded here. A schema that wants to preserve the distinction would need a second value; that is a change to shared canon and is not made from this place record.

What the borough looked like before the tramway: a compact wood-clad house on a big lot, set back from the road, one storey under a mansard with the bedrooms in the attic and a covered gallery running the whole width of the front. The by-law keeps it because a few survive in the oldest parts of Villeray and of the former Cité de Saint-Michel, not because it is common.

Where it comes from

This is the borough's link to the rural Québec forms catalogued elsewhere on this site — the small village house of Charlesbourg's Trait-Carré, of Île d'Orléans, of Lévis. Here it is a survivor inside a twentieth-century street grid rather than the parent of what came after: nothing in Villeray's plex or shoebox descends from it. The by-law's ordering makes the point by putting it first and then never referring back to it.

Shoebox of the early 20th century Shoebox du début du 20e siècle

Style: Shoebox / Vernaculaire industriel (Québec industrial vernacular)
· 424 recorded in Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension
14.I.114.I.3
Phase attribution: verified

A single-storey brick box on a lot deeper than it is wide, set end-on and close to the street with the yard behind, its door in the middle and one tall window either side, and a cornice or a pedimented parapet raising the front above the flat roof. Built cheaply from what was to hand between 1900 and 1940, mostly in Villeray and Parc-Extension, and built to be built over later.

Where it comes from

The shoebox is the one self-built type on this site, and this is one of three records for it — with Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie's single type and with fiche 1.3 next door. The scholarly account is Guy Gaudreau, "Les premières maisons shoebox montréalaises de Rosemont et de Villeray" (Revue d'histoire urbaine / Urban History Review, DOI 10.3138/uhr-2020-0004), which pairs the same two boroughs this site now pairs. What is distinctive here is that the by-law does not simply describe the type: it regulates it, giving it three lettered variants and a table of permitted original and replacement components running to three further pages.

Historical context (fiche)
  • L’appellation shoebox renvoie à la volumétrie rectangulaire de ces maisons qui évoque une boîte à chaussures. La première phase de développement de cette typologie s’étend des années 1900 à 1940. Ce sont des constructions simples, réalisées à faible coût à partir des matériaux disponibles à l’époque. Ces constructions s’insèrent dans la trame urbaine aux côtés de duplex et de triplex. On les trouve principalement dans Villeray et Parc-Extension.
Siting & landscape
  • Generally contiguous with its neighbours, but it may also be detached or semi-detached.

Where it comes from

Boomtown is the most widely distributed form on this site, and this record is one of its two Montréal borough entries in Part 10. What this borough adds to the file is the frame: the fiche gives the construction method in plain French — a slatted wood frame, easy to build, quick to raise — where the Charlesbourg plan de conservation names it « une charpente claire, aussi appelée "Balloon Frame" » and most other sources describe only the parapet. The two readings belong together: the parapet is what the fast frame made possible to dress up.

Where it comes from

This is the plex family recorded in the words of the borough that has most of it. Two things here are not in the other Montréal plex records on this site. The same flat-roofed one-storey box, built between 1940 and 1960, with the ornament stripped off, the crown flattened, the ground floor lifted over a real basement, and the party walls given up for a gap on each side and a deeper front yard to park a car in. Where it comes from

This is the record that makes the shoebox argument visible, because it has no counterpart in Rosemont's single-type treatment. It is also the borough's numerically dominant shoebox: 735 of the 1 159 houses the borough counts fall in this fiche's years, and 544 of those in the eleven years 1950-1960 alone. The type is where the shoebox meets the bungalow — which the same by-law files six fiches later, at 1.7 — and the meeting happens in the siting and the section rather than in the roof, which stays flat.

<strong>The second half of the borough's shoebox split, and the reason it exists.</strong> Read the fiche's own sentence as a list of four changes, all of them dated 1940–1960: the architectural treatment simplifies; the crown becomes sober; the ground floor rises to make a basement; and the contiguous siting is replaced by semi-detached or detached siting with a deeper setback — the last of these expressly « afin de dégager l'espace pour garer une voiture ». Those are bungalow properties arriving inside a flat-roofed rectangular envelope, which is precisely the case for calling it a second type rather than a late phase of the first. Variant E is the one that matters for the vocabulary: a shoebox with a second dwelling in the basement, reached independently through an areaway under the front perron — the case that makes the borough's proposed name « uniplex » legible. Variant D, with the garage under the raised ground floor and a sunken driveway, is the same move made for a car instead of a tenant. Note that both fiches keep a variant A « implantée en fond de lot », so back-of-lot siting is not itself a period marker. The 735 count is derived: it is the sum of the 1940-1949 (191) and 1950-1960 (544) rows of the borough's 2019 guide, matching this fiche's own range, and it is not a figure the borough prints.

The last fiche in the annexe, and the exact counterpart of fiche 2.3 one family up: same 1960s date, same pale white or yellow brick sometimes glazed, same coloured masonry patterning, same flat projecting eave in place of a parapet, same basement garage off a driveway across the front. The two fiches describe one building culture applied at two densities, which is a stronger statement about 1960s Montréal than either makes alone. Note also the entrance: a « large vestibule » with an opening « très large et haute, d'aspect commercial ». The by-law is describing, without saying so, the point at which the front door of a dwelling stops looking domestic — the end of the sequence that began at fiche 1.2 with a door in the middle of a one-storey brick front, flanked by two windows.

Notes on the approach

The shoebox, and a disagreement between two boroughs of the same city. Villeray–Saint-Michel–Parc-Extension splits the shoebox into two numbered types. Fiche 1.2 is the shoebox du début du 20e siècle: « La première phase de développement de cette typologie s'étend des années 1900 à 1940. Ce sont des constructions simples, réalisées à faible coût à partir des matériaux disponibles à l'époque. […] On les trouve principalement dans Villeray et Parc-Extension. » Fiche 1.3 is the shoebox du milieu du 20e siècle: « À partir de 1940 jusqu'en 1960, le traitement architectural des shoebox se simplifie peu à peu. Le couronnement est sobre et le rez-de-chaussée est graduellement surélevé pour permettre l'aménagement d'un sous-sol. L'implantation majoritairement contiguë sera peu à peu remplacée par une implantation jumelée ou isolée, présentant une marge de recul plus importante afin de dégager l'espace pour garer une voiture. » Rosemont–La Petite-Patrie, immediately to the south-east, treats the shoebox as one type. Both boroughs are in the same city, both are regulating the same object, and their documents are roughly contemporary. This is a genuine typological disagreement, not a difference of detail, and this site records it as one rather than resolving it. That is also the honest position: this record was written without reading Rosemont's, and it states only this borough's case.

The case is stronger than it first looks, because this borough counts. Its own 2019 guide inventories 1 159 shoebox houses and tabulates them by decade: 16 in 1900-1909, 144 in 1910-1919, 136 in 1920-1929, 128 in 1930-1939, 191 in 1940-1949, and 544 in the eleven years 1950-1960 — 46.9 % of the whole stock in the last decade of its life. Add the 1940s and 63.4 % of the borough's shoeboxes postdate the moment fiche 1.2 stops. A borough whose shoebox population is mostly post-war has a practical reason to describe the post-war shoebox separately, and a borough whose shoeboxes are mostly older does not. The same table also shows where they are: François-Perrault 660, Saint-Michel 256, Villeray 153, Parc-Extension 90 — so the fiche's own claim that the early type is found principalement dans Villeray et Parc-Extension is a statement about the early type, not about the borough, whose shoebox mass sits in the two northern districts.

What actually differs between the two fiches is worth stating precisely, because it is not ornament alone. Four things change together between 1940 and 1960: the crown goes from a cornice or a pedimented parapet to something sober; the ground floor rises to make a real basement out of what had been a crawl space; the party walls are given up for a gap on each side; and the front margin deepens, expressly to park a car. Those are bungalow properties — this same by-law files the bungalow six fiches later, at 1.7, with a deep setback, a raised floor over a basement garage, detached siting and horizontal windows — arriving inside a flat-roofed rectangular envelope that does not change at all. Two of fiche 1.3's five variants build on the raised floor: variant D puts a garage under it, reached by a sunken drive, and variant E puts a second dwelling under it, reached independently through an areaway beneath the front perron.

« Uniplex ». Variant E is where the borough's proposed alternative name becomes legible. The 2018 heritage study behind the borough's shoebox guidance proposes that these houses could as well be called uniplex, on the ground that they are generally single-family dwellings that belong to the plex family, some of them with a dwelling in the basement — which would make the shoebox the family's member of one. That claim is recorded here in English only, and deliberately. The study itself could not be retrieved in this pass: its address on the Ville's legacy portal now redirects to a dead page, and no mirror was found. No French quotation for « uniplex » is published anywhere in this record, because the sentence was never read in the original. The name is worth keeping in view all the same, because the by-law's own family logic supports it: family 2.0 defines the plex as « de deux à six logements » whose distinguishing feature is that « chaque logement présente habituellement une entrée distincte en façade », and a shoebox with a basement flat entered through its own areaway satisfies that description at n = 2.

What the Part 10 brief did not have. The brief flagged that the plex extraction might have been truncated after fiche 2.2, and asked for a check. It was truncated, and by more than one fiche. Fiche 2.3, plex de la seconde moitié du 20e siècle, exists — and the by-law has a third family after it, 3.0 Immeubles d'appartements, holding fiches 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3. Thirteen fiches, not nine. Fiche 2.3 is the most quotable of the three plex: from the 1960s, « aussi connue sous le nom "plex italien" en raison de l'origine de ses constructeurs », with pale brick, white or light yellow and sometimes glazed, laid at times in coloured patterns the by-law calls fantaisistes, and its most homogeneous groups in Saint-Michel. It is the only type on this site named for the community that built it, and the name is the municipality's own — the borough's public guidance uses it too.

Family 3.0 matters for a different reason: it is where this by-law draws the outer boundary of the plex, and it draws it in an unusual place. « Ils se distinguent des bâtiments de type plex par l'intégration, à l'intérieur du plan de l'édifice, d'un escalier permettant l'accès aux logements et qui rompt avec la tradition montréalaise des escaliers extérieurs. En raison de leur configuration, ces immeubles peuvent comporter significativement plus de logements que les plex. » The line is the stair, and the dwelling count follows from it — where the Sud-Ouest typo-morphological study draws the same line at a shared hall serving six to twelve dwellings. Both tests belong on the exterior-stair topic page, and this one is the sharper, because it says outright that putting the stair inside breaks with a Montréal tradition.

The code, and why the filename cannot be trusted. This borough is arrondissement 14. That was read from inside the cahier, where all 121 sector-code occurrences carry the prefix 14, in a file published as 26_evaluation_patrimoine_villeray.pdf. The Part 10 memo's expectation was correct and the filename is not evidence of anything. The cahier also uses a code letter this site's schema does not carry: alongside 14.AP.1, a secteur d'intérêt archéologique à fort potentiel, it prints 14.A.1 with a bare A, for the lower rank of secteur d'intérêt archéologique. The difference is substantive — for the AP class the cahier says protective measures are necessary, for the A class it says further study is needed first — and both are filed here under one value with the distinction recorded on each sector.

Parc-Extension, and what could not be confirmed. The cahier gives the quarter's name from « le projet de développement connu sous le nom de Park Avenue Extension », near Ville Mont-Royal and a Canadian Pacific line, and dates the sale of lots to 1912: « Sur le territoire de Parc Extension, les lots sont à vendre dès 1912, mais il faudra attendre beaucoup plus tard pour voir une véritable urbanisation. » The Ville's MEM encyclopedia dates the quarter's beginning to 1910 and gives its area as 1.6 km². Two further claims in circulation could not be sourced here and are flagged rather than asserted: that the lots were Park Realty's and were laid out in 1907 — a date the cahier's own « dès 1912 » does not support, and which no municipal document consulted in this pass carries — and that 69 % des immeubles du quartier sont des duplex et des multiplex (4 à 12 logements), which comes from a Ville de Montréal quarter portrait that returned 404 on every route tried. The same gap leaves the wartime-house quadrilatère bounded by Bloomfield, Jarry, Wiseman and de Liège unconfirmed; it is encoded as a sector under a deliberately non-conforming code, PE-WH-1, with the doubt written into the record, so it can be verified or deleted rather than quietly inherited.
